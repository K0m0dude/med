background: >+
  Thyroid nodules are very common — detectable by ultrasound in a large proportion of adults, most often as an **incidental finding** on imaging done for another reason, and far more often benign than malignant (roughly 90–95% are benign). The task is to find the small minority that need tissue sampling, without over-investigating every incidental nodule found. This card covers the **triage pathway**; see the Thyroid cancer card for management once malignancy is confirmed.

  - **Causes**: colloid/hyperplastic nodules, thyroid cysts, follicular adenomas, thyroiditis, and — the minority — thyroid cancer (see that card for subtypes).
  - **Multinodular goitre** carries a similar (low) per-nodule cancer risk to a solitary nodule — having multiple nodules does not itself make malignancy less likely, so the largest/most sonographically suspicious nodule is the one biopsied, not necessarily the largest one by size alone.
diagnosis: >+
  - Most nodules are **asymptomatic**, found incidentally on a scan or by the patient/clinician feeling a lump. Ask about and examine for features that raise suspicion of malignancy: rapid growth, hardness/fixation, a hoarse voice (recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement), dysphagia, cervical lymphadenopathy, and a family history of thyroid cancer or MEN2.
  - Check whether the nodule is **functioning**: symptoms of thyrotoxicosis alongside a nodule suggest a toxic adenoma (see the Hyperthyroidism card) — functioning ("hot") nodules are very rarely malignant.
investigations: >+
  - **TSH first.** If **suppressed**, the nodule may be autonomously functioning — a radioisotope scan showing a "hot" nodule effectively excludes malignancy and avoids an unnecessary biopsy; manage as hyperthyroidism. If TSH is normal/high, proceed to ultrasound.
  - **Ultrasound with the British Thyroid Association U classification (U1–U5)** stratifies risk: **U1** normal, **U2** benign (no FNA needed, in the absence of other concerning clinical features), **U3** indeterminate, **U4** suspicious, **U5** malignant features — **U3 and above need fine needle aspiration (FNA)**. Suspicious sonographic features include marked hypoechogenicity, irregular margins, microcalcifications, a taller-than-wide shape, and increased internal vascularity.
  - **FNA cytology (Thy classification, Thy1–Thy5; broadly mirrored by the Bethesda system elsewhere)**: **Thy1** non-diagnostic (repeat sampling), **Thy2** benign (routine follow-up), **Thy3a/3f** atypia/possible or probable follicular neoplasm (repeat FNA or diagnostic surgery, since follicular lesions cannot be reliably distinguished from follicular carcinoma on cytology alone — malignancy is defined by capsular/vascular invasion, only seen on the full excised specimen), **Thy4** suspicious of malignancy, **Thy5** malignant (proceed directly to definitive management).
  - **Calcitonin** is not routine for every nodule, but is checked if medullary thyroid cancer is specifically suspected (family history, MEN2, or a suspicious nodule with an atypical cytology pattern) — see the Thyroid cancer card.
treatment: >+
  - **U2/Thy2 (benign)**: reassurance; routine clinical ± ultrasound follow-up rather than repeat biopsy, unless the nodule grows or develops new suspicious features.
  - **Thy1 (non-diagnostic)**: repeat FNA, ideally ultrasound-guided, since a substantial proportion are adequate on a second attempt.
  - **Thy3a/3f (atypia/follicular neoplasm)**: diagnostic **hemithyroidectomy** is usually needed, since cytology cannot reliably separate a follicular adenoma from a follicular carcinoma — the diagnosis is made on the final histology, which then dictates whether completion thyroidectomy is needed.
  - **Thy4/Thy5 (suspicious/malignant)**: proceed to surgery as for confirmed thyroid cancer (see that card) — typically total thyroidectomy, with the extent of neck dissection guided by pre-operative imaging and intra-operative findings.
  - **Simple/benign cysts**: aspiration can relieve symptomatic large cysts, though recurrence is common; a persistently recurring or partly solid cystic nodule should still be biopsied as a solid nodule would be.
  - **Large goitre with compressive symptoms** (dysphagia, stridor, positional breathlessness, or retrosternal extension) is an indication for surgery regardless of cytology, for symptom relief.
protocols: >+
  - A rapidly enlarging, hard, fixed thyroid mass with stridor or significant dysphagia, especially in an older patient, raises concern for **anaplastic thyroid cancer** — this needs urgent same-pathway assessment (airway assessment first) rather than routine outpatient work-up; see the Thyroid cancer card.
  - New hoarseness with a thyroid nodule suggests recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement by malignancy and should accelerate the referral pathway rather than being attributed to a coincidental viral laryngitis.
