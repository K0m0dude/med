background: >+
  A visual field defect localises a lesion along the visual pathway with remarkable precision, because the pathway's anatomy is so consistent — this card is included here because **pituitary and suprasellar disease is one of the classic causes** (see the Pituitary disorders card), but the same localising logic applies whatever the underlying cause.

  - **Retina/optic nerve** (in front of the chiasm): a lesion here affects **one eye only**. Causes: optic neuritis, anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy, retinal vascular occlusion, glaucoma, optic nerve compression (including by a pituitary adenoma extending asymmetrically), or **giant cell arteritis** (ischaemic optic neuropathy — a vision-threatening emergency in an older patient with new visual loss and systemic features).
  - **Optic chiasm**: fibres from the **nasal** half of each retina (which see the **temporal** visual field) cross here; temporal retinal fibres (seeing the nasal field) do not cross. A lesion compressing the chiasm therefore characteristically affects the **outer (temporal) field of both eyes** — a **bitemporal hemianopia**. The classic cause is a **pituitary adenoma compressing from below** (affecting the lower chiasmal fibres first, so the **upper temporal quadrants** are lost first) or a **craniopharyngioma compressing from above** (affecting the upper fibres first, so the **lower temporal quadrants** go first).
  - **Optic tract** (just behind the chiasm): a lesion here affects the same (contralateral) side of the field in **both** eyes — a **homonymous hemianopia** — but because the two eyes' fibres haven't yet merged into an orderly retinotopic bundle, it tends to be **incongruous** (not quite identical in each eye).
  - **Optic radiation**: fibres fan out through the temporal and parietal lobes to the occipital cortex. **Temporal lobe (Meyer's loop)** damage causes a contralateral **superior** quadrantanopia; **parietal lobe** damage causes a contralateral **inferior** quadrantanopia (mnemonic: "**P**arietal = **I**nferior, **T**emporal = **S**uperior" — PITS).
  - **Occipital (primary visual) cortex**: a lesion here causes a **congruous** (near-identical in both eyes) homonymous hemianopia, often with **macular sparing** (central vision preserved) because the macula's cortical representation is large, at the occipital pole, and has a dual blood supply (middle and posterior cerebral arteries) — so a posterior cerebral artery infarct typically spares it.
diagnosis: >+
  - **Confrontation visual field testing** (comparing the patient's field to your own, one eye at a time) is a quick bedside screen; **formal (automated/Humphrey) perimetry** is needed to map a defect precisely and follow it over time, especially for suspected chiasmal compression.
  - Ask about the **tempo**: sudden onset suggests a vascular event (stroke, retinal artery/vein occlusion, pituitary apoplexy) or giant cell arteritis; gradual onset over weeks to months suggests a slowly growing mass (pituitary adenoma, meningioma, glioma).
  - **Associated features help localise further**: a headache with a bitemporal hemianopia and other pituitary hormone symptoms points to a sellar/suprasellar mass (see the Pituitary disorders card); a painful third nerve palsy alongside sudden visual loss and headache suggests pituitary apoplexy or, classically, a posterior communicating artery aneurysm; limb weakness or sensory loss alongside a homonymous hemianopia points to a larger vascular territory (e.g. MCA) rather than an isolated visual pathway lesion; jaw claudication, scalp tenderness and a raised ESR/CRP alongside sudden monocular visual loss in an older patient point to giant cell arteritis.
investigations: >+
  - **MRI brain with pituitary views** is the key investigation whenever a chiasmal or retrochiasmal lesion is suspected, since it images the whole pathway and surrounding structures (CT is used if MRI is contraindicated or unavailable acutely).
  - If pituitary/chiasmal compression is confirmed: a full **anterior pituitary hormone panel** (see the Pituitary disorders card), since mass effect and hormonal dysfunction commonly coexist.
  - Sudden monocular visual loss in an older patient: urgent **ESR/CRP** and temporal artery assessment (biopsy where indicated) if giant cell arteritis is suspected — this cannot wait for routine ophthalmology review.
  - Homonymous field loss of sudden onset: treat and investigate as an acute stroke (see the Stroke and TIA factfile, Acute medicine) — urgent CT head, with the same time-critical pathway as any other stroke presentation.
treatment: >+
  - Management is entirely that of the **underlying cause** — there is no treatment directed at the visual field defect itself. A chiasmal lesion from a pituitary adenoma is managed per the Pituitary disorders card (dopamine agonist for a prolactinoma, surgery for other mass-effect-causing adenomas); a homonymous hemianopia from stroke is managed per the Stroke and TIA factfile; giant cell arteritis needs urgent high-dose corticosteroids to protect the contralateral eye, started on clinical suspicion without waiting for biopsy confirmation.
  - **Visual field recovery after relieving chiasmal compression** (e.g. pituitary surgery) can be substantial if the compression was relatively recent, but is often incomplete if the defect has been present for a long time before treatment — this is part of the rationale for not delaying surgery once significant visual loss is confirmed.
  - Patients with a confirmed visual field defect need safety-netting around **driving** — a bitemporal or homonymous hemianopia can mean a patient no longer meets the visual field standard for driving, and this should be discussed and, where relevant, reported (DVLA guidance) rather than left for the patient to realise themselves.
protocols: >+
  > Red flag: sudden visual field loss — whether monocular or homonymous — should be treated as an emergency until proven otherwise. The differential (stroke, giant cell arteritis, retinal artery/vein occlusion, pituitary apoplexy) includes several time-critical diagnoses, so same-day assessment is needed rather than a routine outpatient referral.

  - A new visual field defect with a severe headache and any feature of hypopituitarism (collapse, hypotension) is **pituitary apoplexy** until proven otherwise — see the emergency protocol in the Pituitary disorders card.
  - A new visual field defect with limb weakness, speech disturbance, or facial droop is an acute stroke presentation — follow the local hyperacute stroke pathway (Stroke and TIA factfile, Acute medicine), not a routine ophthalmology referral.
  - Sudden painless monocular visual loss in anyone over 50, especially with scalp tenderness, jaw claudication or malaise, needs same-day assessment for giant cell arteritis and urgent high-dose steroids if suspected — delay risks irreversible bilateral blindness.
