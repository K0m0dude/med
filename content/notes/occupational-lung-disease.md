background: |+
  Lung disease caused by workplace exposure to dusts, fibres, fumes or allergens — an **occupational history (every job held, not just the current one, since latency can be decades) is essential** for every patient with unexplained respiratory disease, and is easy to omit from a routine history.

  Asbestos-related disease

  - A spectrum driven by inhaled asbestos fibres, with **very long latency (often 20–50 years)** between exposure and disease: **pleural plaques** (the commonest finding, usually asymptomatic, not itself premalignant but a marker of past exposure), **benign asbestos pleural effusion**, **asbestosis** (progressive pulmonary fibrosis, dose-related), **mesothelioma** (pleural, essentially always asbestos-related, not clearly dose-related — even limited exposure carries risk), and an **increased risk of bronchial lung cancer** (further markedly increased by concurrent smoking, which multiplies rather than simply adds to the risk).

  Pneumoconioses (mineral dust diseases)

  - **Silicosis**: inhaled crystalline silica (mining, quarrying, stonemasonry, sandblasting) — causes nodular upper-zone fibrosis; also **increases TB risk substantially**, and is linked to an increased risk of lung cancer and autoimmune disease.
  - **Coal worker's pneumoconiosis**: inhaled coal dust — ranges from simple (often asymptomatic, nodular) to **progressive massive fibrosis** (larger conglomerate masses, more symptomatic, can progress even after exposure stops).

  Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (occupational triggers)

  - An immune reaction to an inhaled organic antigen at work — **farmer's lung** (mouldy hay), **bird fancier's/pigeon fancier's lung**, and reactions to contaminated metalworking fluids or mouldy grain — covered in full in the Interstitial lung disease card; included here as it is fundamentally an occupational exposure disease.

  Occupational asthma and byssinosis

  - **Occupational asthma**: new-onset asthma, or worsening of pre-existing asthma, caused or triggered by a workplace sensitiser — isocyanates (spray painting), flour/grain dust (baking), wood dust, latex, and laboratory animal allergens are classic examples.
  - **Byssinosis**: a reaction to cotton, flax or hemp dust, causing chest tightness classically worse on returning to work after a break ("Monday morning fever/tightness") — now rare in the UK with modern dust control.
diagnosis: |+
  - **Asbestosis**: progressive breathlessness and a dry cough over years, with **bibasal fine crackles** and finger clubbing — clinically similar to other fibrotic ILD, so the occupational history is what points to the diagnosis.
  - **Pleural plaques**: usually asymptomatic, found incidentally on imaging — do not themselves cause breathlessness or restrictive physiology to a significant degree, so don't automatically attribute symptoms to plaques alone.
  - **Mesothelioma**: persistent chest pain (often dull, poorly localised), breathlessness (from a pleural effusion), weight loss — presents similarly to other pleural malignancy; see the Lung cancer card for full detail.
  - **Silicosis/coal worker's pneumoconiosis**: breathlessness and cough developing over years of exposure, sometimes with a long delay after exposure has ended, especially in progressive massive fibrosis.
  - **Occupational asthma**: the key clue is a **symptom pattern that relates to work** — worse on workdays, better on days off/holidays, with a delay in recognising the link sometimes of years. Ask directly and specifically about this pattern, since patients rarely volunteer it unprompted.
investigations: |+
  - **HRCT chest**: asbestosis shows a **UIP-like pattern with a basal/subpleural predominance** (similar to IPF — see the ILD card), often with associated pleural plaques (calcified pleural plaques are highly specific for asbestos exposure, supporting the diagnosis context even if not itself causing disease). Silicosis/coal worker's pneumoconiosis show upper-zone nodularity, with large conglomerate masses in progressive massive fibrosis.
  - **Pulmonary function tests**: a restrictive pattern with reduced gas transfer, as in other fibrotic ILD (asbestosis); an obstructive pattern in occupational asthma.
  - **Occupational asthma**: **serial peak flow monitoring at and away from work** (e.g. 4 times a day over several weeks, including days off) is the key objective test — a clear fall in PEF and increase in variability on workdays, improving away from work, supports the diagnosis; specific inhalation challenge testing is sometimes used in specialist centres.
  - **Mesothelioma**: pleural fluid cytology is often negative/inconclusive; **pleural biopsy (image-guided or thoracoscopic)** is usually needed for a confident histological diagnosis — see the Lung cancer card.
  - A detailed, dated **occupational history** (employer, job role, substances, duration, protective equipment used) is itself a key part of the "investigation," both for diagnosis and for any later compensation claim.
treatment: |+
  - **Remove the patient from further exposure** wherever the disease is still occupational-asthma-type/reversible — this is the single most effective step, and the earlier it happens after symptoms start, the better the chance of avoiding permanent impairment. Standard asthma treatment (ICS/formoterol-based, as per the Asthma factfile) is used alongside avoidance.
  - **Asbestosis, silicosis, coal worker's pneumoconiosis**: management is largely supportive, as for other fibrotic lung disease — pulmonary rehabilitation, oxygen assessment, vaccination, smoking cessation (critical, given the multiplicative interaction with asbestos for lung cancer risk), and symptom management; there is no specific antifibrotic proven for these occupational fibroses in the way pirfenidone/nintedanib are used in IPF, though this is an evolving area.
  - **Silicosis**: enhanced TB surveillance given the substantially increased reactivation risk (see the Tuberculosis card).
  - **Mesothelioma and asbestos-related lung cancer**: managed as described in the Lung cancer card.
  - **Compensation and support**: patients (or their families, after death) with an asbestos- or other occupationally-caused lung disease should be made aware of the **Industrial Injuries Disablement Benefit** and, for mesothelioma and some other asbestos diseases, additional compensation schemes — a GP/respiratory letter documenting the occupational history and diagnosis is often needed to support a claim, so this history should be clearly recorded even when it doesn't change immediate clinical management.
protocols: |+
  - Any new diagnosis of an occupational lung disease should prompt consideration of whether it is **reportable** under RIDDOR (Reporting of Injuries, Diseases and Dangerous Occurrences Regulations) by the employer, and whether other workers at the same site may be similarly exposed — occupational health/public health involvement is appropriate for a new cluster or a clear ongoing workplace hazard.
  - **Mesothelioma or a new asbestos-related cancer diagnosis**: early referral for legal/compensation advice is appropriate given statutory time limits on some claims — this is a practical point worth raising proactively, as patients and families are often unaware time limits apply.
  - See the Lung cancer card for the emergency complications of mesothelioma and lung cancer, and the Interstitial lung disease card for acute exacerbation of fibrotic lung disease.
