background: |+
  Respiratory failure means the lungs can no longer maintain adequate gas exchange, defined by arterial blood gas values on room air (or on a known, recorded inspired oxygen if already receiving supplemental oxygen).

  - **Type 1 (hypoxaemic)**: **PaO₂ <8 kPa**, with a **normal or low PaCO₂**. The underlying problem is **ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) mismatch** or a shunt — some alveoli are perfused but poorly ventilated (fluid, collapse, consolidation) or blood bypasses ventilated alveoli altogether. CO₂ stays normal or low because CO₂ diffuses much more readily than O₂ across the alveolar membrane, and the patient typically hyperventilates in response to hypoxaemia, which washes out extra CO₂.
  - **Type 2 (hypercapnic)**: **PaO₂ <8 kPa with PaCO₂ >6 kPa**. The underlying problem is **inadequate alveolar ventilation** overall (not just mismatch) — not enough fresh gas is reaching the alveoli to clear CO₂, whatever the cause (reduced respiratory drive, respiratory muscle weakness/fatigue, increased airway resistance, or restricted chest wall/lung mechanics).
  - **Causes of type 1**: pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, PE, ARDS, pneumothorax, severe asthma (early/compensated stage), and interstitial lung disease.
  - **Causes of type 2**: COPD (the commonest cause encountered day to day), severe/exhausting asthma (a late, ominous sign — see the Asthma factfile), opioid or sedative overdose, neuromuscular disease (myasthenia gravis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, motor neurone disease), chest wall deformity (severe kyphoscoliosis), obesity hypoventilation syndrome, and exhaustion from any cause of type 1 failure that has progressed (the patient can no longer sustain the compensatory hyperventilation).
  - **The two are not mutually exclusive** — a patient can start in type 1 failure (e.g. a severe pneumonia) and progress into type 2 as they tire; and a patient with chronic type 2 failure (COPD) can develop worsening type 1-pattern hypoxaemia on top during an acute infective exacerbation.
diagnosis: |+
  - **Type 1**: breathlessness, tachypnoea, cyanosis, and signs of the underlying cause (focal chest signs in pneumonia, wheeze in asthma, pleuritic pain in PE) — the patient is often working hard to breathe but can still "blow off" CO₂ adequately, at least initially.
  - **Type 2**: breathlessness (though sometimes surprisingly little, especially in chronic compensated disease), with signs of CO₂ retention — bounding pulse, warm peripheries, flapping tremor (asterixis), headache, drowsiness progressing to confusion and reduced consciousness (CO₂ narcosis) as it worsens.
  - **Acute vs chronic matters as much as the gas values themselves**: a chronic (e.g. COPD) patient can tolerate a PaCO₂ persistently in the high single figures with a near-normal pH (renal compensation has raised bicarbonate to match), while the same PaCO₂ developing acutely, with a low pH, represents true decompensated failure — always interpret the **pH and bicarbonate alongside the PaO₂/PaCO₂**, not the gases in isolation.
investigations: |+
  - **Arterial blood gas (ABG)** is the key test — defines the type, shows the pH/bicarbonate (acute vs chronic, degree of compensation), and the base excess/lactate (added physiological stress). A venous gas with SpO₂ can screen or trend but an arterial sample is needed to confirm and fully characterise significant respiratory failure.
  - Investigate the underlying cause in parallel: CXR, FBC/CRP, ECG, and condition-specific tests as guided by the clinical picture (D-dimer/CTPA for suspected PE, BNP/echo for suspected cardiac cause, sputum/cultures for infection) — treating the cause is as important as treating the gas exchange failure itself.
  - In suspected **neuromuscular causes of type 2 failure**, measure **vital capacity** serially (rather than relying on blood gases alone, which can remain deceptively normal until very late, since these patients can maintain normal gas exchange right up until sudden, severe decompensation) — see the Seizures/neurology content for Guillain-Barré monitoring principles, and involve critical care early if vital capacity is falling.
treatment: |+
  - **Type 1**: give **supplemental oxygen** to correct hypoxaemia (target SpO₂ 94–98% in someone not at risk of hypercapnia) while treating the underlying cause — oxygen alone does not fix the V/Q mismatch/shunt, so the cause must be addressed (antibiotics for pneumonia, diuretics for pulmonary oedema, anticoagulation for PE, and so on — see the relevant condition-specific factfile).
  - **Type 2**: the key decision is **how much oxygen to give and when to add ventilatory support**, since over-oxygenating someone whose ventilatory drive partly depends on hypoxaemia (or whose V/Q relationships are disturbed by removing hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction) can worsen CO₂ retention. Use **controlled oxygen** (typically titrated to SpO₂ 88–92% in anyone at risk of hypercapnic failure, e.g. known or suspected COPD) rather than withholding oxygen altogether — correcting dangerous hypoxaemia always takes priority over a theoretical risk of worsening hypercapnia.
  - **Non-invasive ventilation (NIV, typically BiPAP)** is the key treatment for persisting type 2 failure with a respiratory acidosis (**pH <7.35 with PaCO₂ >6 kPa**) despite initial medical therapy and controlled oxygen — most evidence and routine practice centres on COPD exacerbations, but the same physiological principle (supporting inadequate alveolar ventilation) extends to other causes of type 2 failure where appropriate. Reassess the ABG at 1 hour and again at 4 hours to confirm it is working.
  - **CPAP** (continuous positive airway pressure, as opposed to NIV's two pressure levels) is used mainly for **type 1** failure from cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, recruiting collapsed alveoli and improving oxygenation, rather than for ventilatory support in type 2 failure.
  - **Invasive mechanical ventilation** is needed when NIV fails, is contraindicated (reduced consciousness/inability to protect the airway, facial trauma, vomiting, inability to tolerate the mask, or copious secretions), or the patient is too unwell for a trial of NIV in the first place — decided with critical care, and ideally in the context of a clear, previously discussed ceiling of care.
protocols: |+
  > Red flag: a drowsy, confused patient with known or suspected type 2 respiratory failure needs an urgent ABG and senior/critical care review — this can represent CO₂ narcosis, and further sedation (including for agitation caused by hypoxia/hypercapnia itself) risks precipitating respiratory arrest.

  - **NIV failing** (persisting or worsening acidosis at 1–4 hours, falling consciousness, patient intolerance) needs an urgent decision about escalation to invasive ventilation versus a ceiling of care — made early, with the patient (where possible) and family involved, rather than defaulted into at the point of crisis.
  - Whatever the cause, **always treat the underlying driver of respiratory failure in parallel with supporting gas exchange** — oxygen, NIV or ventilation buy time, but do not themselves treat pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, PE, or a myasthenic crisis.
  - See the COPD, Asthma, and Pleural disease factfiles (Acute medicine) and the PE/VTE notes for condition-specific emergency protocols that commonly present as respiratory failure.
