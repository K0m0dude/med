background: >+
  Hypoparathyroidism is **PTH deficiency** (low or absent hormone), causing hypocalcaemia with hyperphosphataemia (the opposite biochemical pattern to primary hyperparathyroidism). **Pseudohypoparathyroidism** is a distinct entity with the same biochemistry of hypocalcaemia and hyperphosphataemia but caused by **target-organ resistance to PTH** — PTH itself is normal or high.

  - **Post-surgical (the commonest cause)**: accidental damage to, devascularisation of, or removal of the parathyroid glands during thyroid or parathyroid surgery. Can be transient (most cases, recovering over days to weeks) or permanent.
  - **Autoimmune**: isolated, or as part of **autoimmune polyglandular syndrome type 1** (hypoparathyroidism + Addison's disease + chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis — onset usually in childhood).
  - **Genetic/congenital**: **DiGeorge syndrome** (22q11 deletion — congenital absence of the parathyroid glands, alongside cardiac defects and immunodeficiency), activating calcium-sensing receptor mutations, familial isolated hypoparathyroidism.
  - **Functional**: severe **hypomagnesaemia** impairs both PTH release and peripheral PTH action, producing a reversible hypoparathyroid-like state that will not respond to calcium/vitamin D alone.
  - **Pseudohypoparathyroidism type 1A (Albright hereditary osteodystrophy)**: a *GNAS* gene defect causing PTH resistance, with a characteristic phenotype — short stature, short 4th/5th metacarpals, rounded face, and (often) learning difficulties/obesity — alongside resistance to other G-protein-coupled hormones (can cause hypothyroidism and hypogonadism too).
diagnosis: >+
  - Neuromuscular irritability from hypocalcaemia: perioral and peripheral paraesthesiae, muscle cramps, and **tetany**. **Chvostek's sign** and **Trousseau's sign** (see the Calcium metabolism card) reveal latent tetany. Severe cases: seizures, laryngospasm, and a prolonged QT with arrhythmia risk.
  - **Always check calcium after total thyroidectomy** (and in anyone with new unexplained neuromuscular symptoms post-neck surgery) — symptoms typically appear within the first 24–48 hours if the parathyroids have been compromised.
  - Pseudohypoparathyroidism: the physical phenotype above, often recognised before (or instead of) overt hypocalcaemia is noted.
investigations: >+
  - **Calcium, phosphate, PTH and magnesium together.** **Low calcium + high phosphate + low/undetectable PTH** = hypoparathyroidism. **Low calcium + high phosphate + normal/high PTH** = pseudohypoparathyroidism (once vitamin D deficiency and CKD — which also raise PTH appropriately — have been excluded).
  - Post-thyroidectomy: a **PTH checked around 12–24 hours after surgery** helps predict whether hypoparathyroidism will be transient or permanent — a PTH that has recovered into the normal range makes permanent hypoparathyroidism unlikely, while a persistently very low/undetectable PTH makes it more likely.
  - If autoimmune or genetic hypoparathyroidism is suspected without a surgical history: 22q11 deletion testing (DiGeorge) if other features fit, adrenal and other autoimmune screens if APS1 is suspected, and *GNAS* testing for suspected pseudohypoparathyroidism.
treatment: >+
  - **Acute symptomatic hypocalcaemia**: IV calcium gluconate (e.g. 10 mL of 10% over 10 minutes with cardiac monitoring, repeated or as an infusion depending on severity and response) — the same acute approach as any other cause of symptomatic hypocalcaemia. **Check and correct magnesium** at the same time, since calcium replacement will not work if magnesium remains low.
  - **Chronic maintenance**: oral calcium supplements plus **calcitriol or alfacalcidol** (active vitamin D) rather than standard (inactive) vitamin D — in true hypoparathyroidism, the renal activation step normally driven by PTH is itself impaired, so plain vitamin D is insufficient. Aim for a calcium at or just below the lower end of the normal range, not fully normal — over-correction increases urinary calcium excretion and the long-term risk of nephrocalcinosis/renal stones and renal impairment.
  - **Recombinant human PTH** (e.g. PTH 1–84, given subcutaneously) is an option for patients who remain poorly controlled or need high doses of calcium/calcitriol despite standard treatment, under specialist endocrine supervision.
  - Pseudohypoparathyroidism is managed the same way biochemically (calcium and active vitamin D), since the end problem is the same hypocalcaemia — PTH replacement itself is not useful, as resistance is at the receptor/signalling level, not a hormone deficiency.
protocols: >+
  > Red flag: new neuromuscular symptoms, perioral tingling or carpopedal spasm in the first 1–2 days after thyroid or parathyroid surgery should prompt an urgent calcium check — don't wait for a scheduled blood round.

  - Severe acute hypocalcaemia (laryngospasm, seizures, significant arrhythmia/prolonged QT) needs the same emergency IV calcium approach as any cause of hypocalcaemic crisis — see the Calcium metabolism card.
  - Routine post-thyroidectomy practice at many centres includes prophylactic/early calcium (and sometimes calcitriol) supplementation, especially after total thyroidectomy or when multiple parathyroids were at risk — follow local protocols.
