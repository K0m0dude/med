background: >+
  Thyrotoxicosis is the clinical state of excess circulating thyroid hormone; hyperthyroidism specifically means the thyroid itself is over-producing it (as opposed to, say, thyroiditis releasing stored hormone, or exogenous over-replacement).

  - **Graves' disease (~75% of cases)**: autoimmune — **TSH-receptor stimulating antibodies (TRAb)** directly stimulate the thyroid. Associated with other autoimmune disease, and with **Graves' orbitopathy** (thyroid eye disease) and, rarely, pretibial myxoedema and thyroid acropachy.
  - **Toxic multinodular goitre** and **toxic adenoma**: one or more autonomously functioning nodules, independent of TSH or antibody drive — commoner in older patients and in iodine-deficient areas.
  - **Thyroiditis** (subacute/de Quervain's, post-partum, silent): transient release of pre-formed hormone from an inflamed gland, causing a self-limiting thyrotoxic phase (often followed by a hypothyroid phase, then recovery) — **not** true hyperthyroidism, and radioiodine uptake is low, unlike Graves' or a toxic nodule.
  - **Secondary (central) hyperthyroidism**: rare — a **TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma**, driving the thyroid from above (high/inappropriately normal TSH *with* a high T4, unlike every other cause where TSH is suppressed).
  - **Amiodarone** can cause thyrotoxicosis by two different mechanisms (iodine-induced excess hormone synthesis, or a destructive thyroiditis), which matters because they're treated differently (see Treatment).
diagnosis: >+
  - Weight loss despite a normal/increased appetite, heat intolerance and sweating, tremor, palpitations/tachycardia (or new **atrial fibrillation**, especially in older patients), anxiety and irritability, diarrhoea, oligo/amenorrhoea, and proximal muscle weakness.
  - Signs: fine tremor, warm moist skin, lid lag and lid retraction (sympathetic overactivity — occurs in any cause of thyrotoxicosis), tachycardia, a goitre (diffuse in Graves', nodular in toxic MNG, tender in subacute thyroiditis).
  - **Graves'-specific eye disease** (not seen in other causes): proptosis/exophthalmos, periorbital oedema, conjunctival injection, and in severe cases, diplopia or reduced visual acuity from optic nerve compression (an ophthalmology emergency) — this is immune-mediated orbital inflammation, distinct from the lid lag/retraction common to all thyrotoxicosis.
investigations: >+
  - **TSH suppressed, free T4/T3 raised** in primary hyperthyroidism of any cause; **TSH not suppressed** (normal or high) with a high T4 points to secondary (TSH-secreting adenoma) disease — image the pituitary.
  - **TRAb (TSH-receptor antibody)**: positive in **~90%** of Graves' disease — the single most useful test to confirm Graves' without needing a radioiodine uptake scan in most cases. TPO antibodies are less specific (also positive in Hashimoto's).
  - **Radioiodine uptake scan** differentiates causes when the picture is unclear: diffusely **increased** uptake (Graves'), focally **increased** uptake (toxic adenoma/MNG), or **decreased/absent** uptake (thyroiditis, or exogenous thyroid hormone) — useful because thyroiditis needs no antithyroid drug and resolves on its own.
  - ECG for AF or other arrhythmia; FBC before starting an antithyroid drug (baseline, given the agranulocytosis risk described below).
treatment: >+
  - **Symptom control**: a **β-blocker** (propranolol) for tremor, palpitations and anxiety while definitive treatment takes effect.
  - **Antithyroid drugs**: **carbimazole** first-line (converted to methimazole), either as **dose titration** (start around 15–40 mg/day, then reduce to a maintenance dose of roughly 5–15 mg/day as thyroid function normalises) or **block-and-replace** (higher-dose carbimazole fully blocking the thyroid, with levothyroxine added back in) — both achieve similar control; titration has a lower side-effect burden, block-and-replace gives more stable levels. A typical course runs **12–18 months**, then drugs are stopped to see if remission has occurred (more likely in Graves' than in nodular disease, which usually needs definitive treatment).
  - **Propylthiouracil** is used instead of carbimazole specifically in the **first trimester of pregnancy** (carbimazole carries a small risk of aplasia cutis and other embryopathy) and in thyroid storm (see below) — but carries its own, rarer, risk of severe hepatotoxicity, so is not preferred long-term.
  - > Red flag: warn every patient starting carbimazole or propylthiouracil about **agranulocytosis** — sore throat, mouth ulcers or fever should prompt an urgent FBC and stopping the drug immediately until results are back, since this can progress to life-threatening neutropenic sepsis.
  - **Radioiodine (I-131)**: definitive treatment for Graves', toxic multinodular goitre and toxic adenoma. Avoided in pregnancy/breastfeeding, and used cautiously (or avoided) in active moderate-to-severe Graves' eye disease, which it can worsen — steroid cover may be given alongside it in at-risk patients. Most patients become hypothyroid afterwards and need lifelong levothyroxine.
  - **Surgery (total or subtotal thyroidectomy)**: for large goitres with compressive symptoms, suspicion of malignancy, patient preference, or when drugs/radioiodine have failed or are contraindicated (including in pregnancy, where it can be done in the second trimester if needed). The patient should be rendered **euthyroid before surgery** wherever possible to avoid precipitating a thyroid storm.
  - **Amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis**: type 1 (iodine-induced excess synthesis, usually on a background of underlying nodular disease) is treated with antithyroid drugs; type 2 (destructive thyroiditis) is treated with steroids — distinguishing the two (colour-flow Doppler ultrasound, inflammatory markers) matters because the treatments differ, and specialist endocrine input is usually needed.
protocols: >+
  Thyroid storm (thyrotoxic crisis)

  > A life-threatening hypermetabolic state, usually precipitated by infection, surgery, trauma, iodine load (including contrast or radioiodine), or abruptly stopping antithyroid drugs, on a background of poorly controlled thyrotoxicosis. Mortality remains significant even with treatment.

  - Suspected clinically — fever, marked tachycardia or arrhythmia (including new AF), agitation progressing to delirium/psychosis/coma, GI symptoms (vomiting, diarrhoea, jaundice) and cardiovascular compromise. The **Burch–Wartofsky score** supports the diagnosis but treatment should not be delayed for a formal score if the clinical picture fits.
  - **Treatment (give in this order, propylthiouracil before iodine)**: supportive care and cooling for fever (avoid aspirin — it displaces thyroid hormone from binding proteins, worsening free hormone levels); **propylthiouracil** (preferred acutely — blocks new hormone synthesis **and** peripheral T4→T3 conversion) given at least **1 hour before** iodine (Lugol's solution/potassium iodide), since giving iodine first can be used by the still-active gland as substrate for more hormone synthesis; a **β-blocker** (propranolol, which also reduces T4→T3 conversion) for rate control; **IV hydrocortisone** (reduces T4→T3 conversion and covers possible relative adrenal insufficiency from the hypermetabolic state); and aggressive treatment of the precipitant (e.g. antibiotics for infection).
  - **Critical care/HDU level care**, with endocrinology involved early. Plasmapheresis is a rescue option for severe, refractory cases.

  Graves' eye disease with visual loss

  - Sudden deterioration in visual acuity, colour vision or a new relative afferent pupillary defect in a patient with Graves' orbitopathy suggests **optic nerve compression** — an emergency needing same-day ophthalmology assessment, high-dose steroids, and consideration of urgent orbital decompression.
