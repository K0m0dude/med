background: |+
  Accumulation of fluid (or blood, pus, gas) in the pericardial space under enough pressure to compress the heart, impairing diastolic filling and reducing cardiac output — a clinical and physiological diagnosis, not simply "a large effusion" (see the Pericardial effusion card for that distinction).

  - **What matters is the rate of accumulation, not just the volume**: the pericardium can stretch to accommodate a large *slowly* accumulating effusion (sometimes over a litre) with little haemodynamic effect, whereas a *rapid* accumulation of even 150–200 mL (e.g. from trauma or free wall rupture) can cause tamponade, since the pericardium has not had time to stretch.
  - **Causes**: any cause of pericardial effusion (see that card) can tamponade if it accumulates fast enough or becomes large enough — malignancy, pericarditis, uraemia, and post-cardiac surgery are common medical causes; **trauma, aortic dissection (Type A, into the pericardium), post-MI free wall rupture, and iatrogenic injury (after PCI, pacemaker insertion, or cardiac biopsy)** are important causes of rapid, haemorrhagic tamponade.
diagnosis: |+
  - **Beck's triad**: **hypotension, raised JVP, and muffled/quiet heart sounds** — classic, but often incomplete in practice (not all three present), so its absence should not be used to exclude tamponade.
  - **Pulsus paradoxus**: an exaggerated fall in systolic BP (**>10 mmHg**) during inspiration — reflects exaggerated ventricular interdependence when the pericardium is non-compliant. Also seen (less specifically) in severe asthma/COPD and massive PE.
  - Other signs: tachycardia (compensatory), tachypnoea, cool peripheries, and **Kussmaul's sign** (JVP rising, rather than falling, on inspiration — reflects impaired right heart filling, also seen in constrictive pericarditis).
  - Think of it in anyone with a known pericardial effusion, recent cardiac procedure, chest trauma, aortic dissection, or malignancy who develops unexplained hypotension or shock — tamponade can present simply as **obstructive shock** without every classical sign being obvious.
investigations: |+
  - **Echocardiography is the key investigation** — looks for **diastolic collapse of the right atrium and right ventricle** (the right heart is lower pressure and collapses first), a **"swinging heart"** within a large effusion, a **dilated, poorly-collapsing inferior vena cava**, and exaggerated respiratory variation in mitral/tricuspid inflow velocities (echocardiographic correlate of pulsus paradoxus).
  - **ECG**: **low voltage QRS complexes** and, with a very large swinging effusion, **electrical alternans** (beat-to-beat alternation in QRS amplitude/axis, from the heart physically swinging within the fluid) — neither is sensitive, but both are suggestive when present.
  - **CXR**: an enlarged, globular ("water bottle") cardiac silhouette with a rapidly accumulating effusion, though this takes a significant volume to develop and a normal CXR does not exclude tamponade, especially if acute.
  - Bedside/point-of-care ultrasound is increasingly used for rapid diagnosis in an unstable or trauma patient, where there may not be time for formal echocardiography.
treatment: |+
  - **Pericardiocentesis (needle aspiration of the pericardial fluid, usually echo-guided)** is the definitive emergency treatment — relieves the pressure on the heart immediately and is often both diagnostic (fluid analysis) and therapeutic.
  - **IV fluids** can temporarily support filling pressures and buy time while definitive drainage is arranged, but **do not treat the underlying problem** and should not substantially delay pericardiocentesis in a deteriorating patient.
  - **Traumatic haemopericardium, or tamponade from aortic dissection/free wall rupture**, is better managed with **urgent surgery (thoracotomy/sternotomy)** rather than needle pericardiocentesis alone where possible — the underlying bleeding source needs direct surgical control, and needle drainage alone risks re-accumulation or incomplete control of ongoing haemorrhage.
  - Treat the underlying cause once the emergency is addressed — e.g. malignancy (may need a pericardial window/drain for recurrent effusions), uraemia (dialysis), infection, or pericarditis (see that card).
protocols: |+
  > Red flag: tamponade is a form of **obstructive shock** and one of the reversible causes in the ALS cardiac arrest algorithm ("tamponade" among the 4 Ts) — consider and treat it specifically during any arrest where it is plausible (recent cardiac procedure, trauma, known effusion, dissection), since standard resuscitation drugs will not fix the underlying mechanical problem.
  - Caution: in **tamponade from aortic dissection**, pericardiocentesis should be used sparingly/as a bridge only (small-volume aspiration to support the patient en route to theatre) rather than complete drainage — fully relieving the tamponade can remove the back-pressure that was limiting ongoing haemorrhage from the dissection, worsening bleeding. This decision is made with cardiothoracic surgery, not in isolation.
  - Sudden deterioration (hypotension, rising JVP) in a patient known to have a pericardial effusion, or in the days after cardiac surgery/PCI/pacemaker insertion, should prompt urgent echocardiography for tamponade rather than being attributed to another cause by default.
