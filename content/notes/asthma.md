background: >+
  - Chronic inflammatory airway disease with variable, reversible airflow
  obstruction and bronchial hyper-responsiveness. Type 2 (allergic) inflammation
  with eosinophils, IgE and mast cells is typical; airway smooth muscle
  constriction, mucus plugging and oedema narrow the lumen.

  - **Triggers:** viral respiratory infection, allergens, exercise, cold air,
  smoke, pollution, NSAIDs and aspirin, β-blockers, emotional stress. Poor
  adherence to inhaled corticosteroid is a common cause of admission.

  - **Risk factors for fatal asthma:** previous ICU admission or ventilation,
  repeated admissions, heavy reliance on SABA, poor adherence, psychosocial
  problems.

diagnosis: >+
  > **BTS/SIGN severity:**

  >

  > **Moderate:** increasing symptoms, PEF 50–75% of best or predicted, no
  severe features.

  >

  > **Acute severe (any one):** PEF 33–50%, RR ≥25, HR ≥110, unable to complete
  sentences in one breath.

  >

  > **Life-threatening (any one):** PEF <33%, SpO₂ <92%, PaO₂ <8 kPa, "normal"
  PaCO₂ (4.6–6.0 kPa), silent chest, cyanosis, poor respiratory effort,
  arrhythmia, exhaustion, altered consciousness, hypotension.

  >

  > **Near-fatal:** raised PaCO₂ and/or need for mechanical ventilation.


  - Signs: wheeze, tachypnoea, tachycardia, accessory muscle use,
  hyperinflation, pulsus paradoxus. **A silent chest is ominous, not
  reassuring.** A rising or normal CO₂ in an acute severe attack means the
  patient is tiring.

  - Look for the trigger (infection, ICS non-adherence) and for complications
  (pneumothorax, pneumonia, lobar collapse).

  - Differentials: COPD exacerbation, pulmonary oedema, PE, pneumothorax,
  anaphylaxis, upper airway obstruction, vocal cord dysfunction.


  - **Chronic “uncontrolled” asthma is defined as**: any exacerbation needing
  oral corticosteroids, or needing reliever treatment **≥3 days a week**, or
  waking at night with symptoms.

investigations: >+
  **Acute presentation:**


  - **Peak expiratory flow** (compare with personal best or predicted), SpO₂,
  RR, HR, ability to speak.

  - **ABG** if SpO₂ <92% or any life-threatening feature. Pay attention to PaCO₂
  and pH.

  - **CXR** if life-threatening features, suspected pneumothorax or
  consolidation, or failure to respond.

  - U&E (K⁺ can fall with high-dose salbutamol), FBC, CRP, and theophylline
  level if on it.


  **Chronic presentation:**


  - Eosinophil count (≥ 300 cells/µL for high risk/ 150-300 for intermediate )or
  FeNO (≥ 50 ppb/25-49 for intermediate) first.

  - If neither confirms the Dx, bronchodilator reversibility on spirometry
  (**>12% and >200 mL rise** is positive).

  - if unavailable, 2 weeks of twice-daily PEF (**≥20% variability** is
  positive).

  - If none of these confirm it but suspicion remains, refer for bronchial
  challenge testing.

treatment: >+
  - **Oxygen** to keep SpO₂ 94–98%.

  - **Nebulised salbutamol 5 mg** (oxygen-driven), repeated every 15–30 min or
  continuously in life-threatening asthma. Add **ipratropium 500 mcg** nebulised
  for severe or life-threatening attacks.

  - **Steroids early:** prednisolone 40–50 mg orally (or hydrocortisone 100 mg
  IV if unable to swallow), continue for 5 days.

  - **Magnesium sulfate 1.2–2 g IV over 20 min** (single dose) for acute severe
  asthma not responding to initial bronchodilators, and for life-threatening
  asthma.

  - Life-threatening or not responding: **senior and ICU review early**. IV
  aminophylline or IV salbutamol only on senior advice. Intubation is high risk
  and done by an expert. NIV is not routine.

  - Repeat PEF 15–30 min after starting treatment. Monitor K⁺, ABG and lactate
  (salbutamol can cause type B lactic acidosis).

  - **Discharge criteria:** PEF >75% of best or predicted, stable on discharge
  medication for 12–24 h. Check inhaler technique, give a written **personal
  asthma action plan**, a course of oral steroids, and follow up with GP within
  2 working days and respiratory clinic within 4 weeks. Assess for psychosocial
  and adherence problems.


  **Chronic management follows the 2024 joint BTS/NICE/SIGN guideline:**


  - **AIR (anti-inflammatory reliever) therapy**: a low-dose ICS/formoterol
  inhaler taken **as needed** for symptom relief, with no separate daily
  maintenance inhaler. This is the **first-line starting point for newly
  diagnosed, mild asthma**. Only specific licensed products
  (budesonide/formoterol, in practice) can be used this way — check the product
  is licensed for AIR use before prescribing it this way.

  - **MART (maintenance and reliever therapy)**: the **same single
  ICS/formoterol inhaler**, used as a **regular twice-daily maintenance dose**,
  with additional doses taken as needed for symptoms on top. Patients on MART
  don’t need a separate reliever.

  - **Starting point depends on symptom burden**: as-needed AIR for most new
  diagnoses; start at **low-dose MART instead** for a patient who is highly
  symptomatic at presentation.

  - **Stepping up**: if uncontrolled on as-needed AIR, step up to low-dose MART.
  If uncontrolled on low-dose MART, step up to **moderate-dose MART**. Beyond
  that, add-on therapy (LTRA and/or LAMA) is considered, then ICS/LABA/LAMA
  triple therapy, with specialist referral if still uncontrolled — **no
  high-dose ICS is used in this pathway**, unlike the old ladder.

  - **Stepping down**: if controlled on ICS alone or low-dose MART, step down to
  low-dose ICS/formoterol PRN (AIR).

  - **Dry powder inhalers are preferred first-line** (lower carbon footprint
  than pressurised metered-dose inhalers) where the patient can use one
  effectively.

  - Every patient needs a **personalised written action plan** matched to
  whichever pathway they’re on (an AIR plan or a MART plan — not
  interchangeable), and FeNO can be used at review, and before/after any
  treatment change, to help judge control objectively.

protocols: >+
  - **BTS/SIGN acute asthma algorithm** and the asthma attack care bundle.
  **ABCDE**, NEWS2.

  - **ICU referral** for a life-threatening attack, deteriorating PEF, rising
  PaCO₂, exhaustion, or altered consciousness. Never sedate.

  - Anaphylaxis can present as asthma. Consider it if there is urticaria,
  angioedema or hypotension.
