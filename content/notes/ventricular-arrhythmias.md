background: |+
  Arrhythmias arising from the ventricular myocardium or conduction system below the AV node — the most immediately dangerous arrhythmias, since they can cause haemodynamic collapse and sudden cardiac death within minutes.

  - **Ventricular ectopics (premature ventricular contractions)**: extra beats arising from an irritable ventricular focus. Very common, usually benign in a structurally normal heart, but **frequent ectopics, or ectopics in the context of structural heart disease, ischaemia or a cardiomyopathy, carry more significance** and may indicate a substrate for more serious arrhythmia.
  - **Ventricular tachycardia (VT)**: a run of **≥3 consecutive ventricular ectopic beats at a rate >100/min**. **Monomorphic VT** (every beat looks the same) is usually associated with a fixed anatomical substrate — most often **scar from a previous MI**, but also cardiomyopathy or, rarely, a structurally normal heart (idiopathic VT). **Polymorphic VT** (beat-to-beat variation in QRS morphology) suggests an unstable, dynamic electrical substrate — **torsades de pointes** is the classic example, occurring on a background of a **long QT interval**.
  - **Long QT syndrome**: congenital (inherited ion channel mutations) or, much more commonly, **acquired** — drugs (antiarrhythmics, some antipsychotics, antiemetics like ondansetron, macrolide/quinolone antibiotics, methadone, and many others), electrolyte disturbance (**hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypocalcaemia**), bradycardia, and structural heart disease — all predispose to torsades.
  - **Ventricular fibrillation (VF)**: chaotic, disorganised ventricular electrical activity with no coordinated contraction and no cardiac output — a **cardiac arrest rhythm**, almost always caused by acute ischaemia, a prior infarct scar, or a severe cardiomyopathy/channelopathy.
diagnosis: |+
  - **Ventricular ectopics**: usually asymptomatic or felt as an occasional "skipped beat" or a thump; a compensatory pause often follows.
  - **VT**: palpitations, dizziness, syncope, chest pain, or breathlessness — **but VT can also be surprisingly well-tolerated for a period, especially in a younger patient with good LV function**, so a patient being "stable" does not exclude VT or make it safe to ignore. **Any regular broad-complex tachycardia should be treated as VT until proven otherwise**, particularly in a patient with known ischaemic heart disease — assuming it is "just an SVT with aberrant conduction" is a classically dangerous error.
  - **Torsades de pointes**: episodic, often self-terminating runs causing recurrent syncope, but can degenerate into VF and sudden death — look specifically for a **prolonged QTc** on the resting/preceding ECG and a relevant drug or electrolyte trigger.
  - **VF**: the patient is in **cardiac arrest** — unresponsive, not breathing normally, no cardiac output.
investigations: |+
  - **12-lead ECG** (or rhythm strip in an emergency) is the key investigation. **VT**: a **broad-complex (QRS ≥120 ms), regular tachycardia**; features favouring VT over SVT with aberrancy include **AV dissociation** (independent P waves marching through, unrelated to the QRS), **capture or fusion beats**, a very broad QRS (**>160 ms**), and **concordance** (all chest leads uniformly positive or negative) — various formal algorithms (e.g. Brugada criteria) exist, but the practical rule is: **if there is any doubt, treat it as VT**.
  - **12-lead ECG between episodes**: measure the **QTc** (looking for long QT syndrome, congenital or a persisting acquired cause) and look for a prior MI (Q waves), LV hypertrophy, or a Brugada pattern (a specific cause of VF/sudden death, with a characteristic coved ST elevation in V1–V3).
  - **U&E, magnesium and calcium** (correctable triggers, especially for torsades), **troponin** (ischaemic trigger), and a **medication review** for any QT-prolonging or pro-arrhythmic drug.
  - **Echocardiography** to assess LV function and look for structural heart disease/cardiomyopathy — the single most useful predictor of future risk and the main factor in deciding on an ICD (implantable cardioverter-defibrillator).
  - **Ambulatory monitoring** for suspected paroxysmal ventricular ectopics/non-sustained VT not captured on a resting ECG; **genetic testing** is considered for suspected inherited arrhythmia syndromes (long QT, Brugada) alongside family screening.
treatment: |+
  - **Ventricular ectopics**: reassurance if infrequent and the heart is structurally normal; reduce triggers (caffeine, alcohol, stress); a beta-blocker for troublesome symptomatic ectopics. Frequent ectopics with structural heart disease warrant cardiology assessment rather than reassurance alone.
  - **Sustained VT, haemodynamically stable**: **IV amiodarone** (loading dose followed by infusion) is the standard first-line antiarrhythmic. If drug treatment fails, or the patient deteriorates at any point, proceed to synchronised DC cardioversion.
  - **Sustained VT, haemodynamically unstable** (or any VT with adverse features — shock, syncope, myocardial ischaemia, heart failure): **immediate synchronised DC cardioversion**, per the Resuscitation Council UK tachycardia algorithm.
  - **Torsades de pointes**: **IV magnesium sulfate** (even if the serum magnesium is normal), correct potassium to the high-normal range, stop any QT-prolonging drug, and consider temporary pacing (overdrive pacing suppresses the arrhythmia by shortening the QT interval via a faster heart rate) for recurrent episodes; **if it degenerates into VF/pulseless arrest, defibrillate** as for any shockable arrest rhythm.
  - **Long-term prevention**: treat/avoid the underlying cause (revascularise ischaemia, optimise heart failure therapy, stop the causative drug); an **ICD** is offered for selected patients at high risk of sudden cardiac death — notably survivors of a cardiac arrest due to VT/VF without a reversible cause, sustained VT with structural heart disease, or significantly impaired LV function post-MI meeting specific criteria — decided with specialist input. **Catheter ablation** is an option for recurrent monomorphic VT, particularly scar-related VT.
protocols: |+
  Ventricular fibrillation / pulseless VT (ALS, shockable rhythm)

  > VF and pulseless VT are managed identically in cardiac arrest — both are "shockable" rhythms in the Resuscitation Council UK ALS algorithm.

  - **Immediate defibrillation** (single shock), then resume **CPR for 2 minutes** before rhythm/pulse check and further shocks as needed. **Adrenaline 1 mg IV** and **amiodarone 300 mg IV** are given after the **third shock** if the rhythm remains shockable, with adrenaline repeated every 3–5 minutes thereafter and a further dose of amiodarone (150 mg) after the fifth shock if still in a shockable rhythm.
  - Identify and treat **reversible causes (4 Hs and 4 Ts)** throughout — hypoxia, hypovolaemia, hypo-/hyperkalaemia and other metabolic disturbance, hypothermia; thrombosis (coronary or pulmonary), tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins.
  - **Post-resuscitation care** after return of spontaneous circulation includes urgent coronary angiography if an ischaemic cause is likely, targeted temperature management, and ICU-level supportive care — see the Deteriorating patient factfile (Acute medicine) for the full ALS algorithm.

  - Any patient recovering from VT/VF arrest without an immediately identified and fully reversible cause should be assessed for an ICD before discharge, and not simply discharged once the acute rhythm has settled.
