background: |+
  A descriptive term, not a single disease, for patients who have **persistent airflow obstruction with features of both asthma and COPD** — most often an older patient with a significant smoking history (fitting COPD) who also has a strong atopic/reversible component (fitting asthma), or a long-standing asthmatic whose airway obstruction has become partly fixed over years.
  - Neither the BTS/SIGN/NICE asthma guideline nor the standard COPD guideline specifically defines or names "ACOS" as a formal diagnosis — in practice, these patients are managed pragmatically by treating whichever features (reversibility, eosinophilic inflammation, exacerbation pattern) are dominant, rather than waiting for a specific label.
  - Why it matters practically: patients with an overlap picture tend to have **more frequent exacerbations, faster lung function decline, and worse quality of life** than either "pure" asthma or "pure" COPD, and they need **inhaled corticosteroid-containing treatment** (unlike COPD alone managed by airflow limitation severity without an asthmatic/eosinophilic component — see the COPD factfile).
diagnosis: |+
  - Features pointing towards an overlap picture rather than "pure" COPD: **significant bronchodilator reversibility**, a history of **childhood or earlier-life asthma or atopy** (eczema, hay fever, allergic rhinitis), marked **diurnal or day-to-day symptom variability**, and **peripheral blood eosinophilia**.
  - Features pointing towards "pure" COPD: onset after age 40, substantial smoking history, slowly progressive breathlessness without much variability, and minimal reversibility.
  - Many patients don't fall cleanly into either category — the practical question to ask is **"does this patient have evidence of a reversible/eosinophilic component that would benefit from ICS, on top of their fixed airflow obstruction?"** rather than trying to force a binary diagnosis.
investigations: |+
  - **Spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility testing**: a post-bronchodilator **FEV1/FVC <0.7** confirms fixed airflow obstruction (as in COPD); a large reversibility response (an increase in FEV1 of **≥12% and ≥200 mL**) suggests a significant asthmatic/reversible component, even though some reversibility can also occur in COPD alone.
  - **Blood eosinophil count** — a raised eosinophil count (commonly using thresholds around **≥300 cells/microlitre**, with ≥150 regarded as some evidence of a steroid-responsive phenotype) supports an eosinophilic/asthma-like component likely to respond to inhaled corticosteroids.
  - **FeNO** (fractional exhaled nitric oxide), where available, can support an eosinophilic/asthmatic component in the same way as in asthma diagnosis.
  - A history of **atopy** (skin prick testing or specific IgE if relevant) and a clear account of symptom pattern/variability complete the picture — there is no single confirmatory test, and the diagnosis is built from the whole picture rather than one result.
treatment: |+
  - **Do not withhold inhaled corticosteroid-containing treatment** in a patient with COPD-pattern airflow obstruction who also has features above suggesting an asthmatic/eosinophilic component — this is the single most important practical point, since under-treating the asthmatic component risks more frequent exacerbations.
  - Typical approach: **ICS/LABA** (or triple therapy, ICS/LABA/LAMA, if exacerbations continue) rather than a LAMA/LABA combination alone (the usual default in COPD without an asthmatic component) — see the COPD factfile for the non-overlap COPD ladder for comparison.
  - **Smoking cessation**, pulmonary rehabilitation, vaccination (influenza, pneumococcal), and inhaler technique optimisation apply exactly as in COPD and asthma individually.
  - For patients with a strong, clearly reversible/eosinophilic component, treatment can otherwise follow the **asthma AIR/MART approach** (see the Asthma factfile and the chronic asthma management notes) rather than the COPD ladder, at clinical discretion — the guiding principle is to treat the dominant, treatable trait rather than apply one algorithm rigidly.
  - Refer to a respiratory specialist for diagnostic uncertainty, frequent exacerbations despite optimised inhaled therapy, or consideration of biologic therapy (as used in severe eosinophilic asthma) in appropriately selected patients.
protocols: |+
  - Acute exacerbations are managed the same way regardless of the "label" — treat as an acute asthma attack if there is a strong reversible/wheezy component with significant variability, or as an infective COPD exacerbation if that picture dominates (see the Asthma and COPD factfiles, Acute medicine) — most patients in practice need elements of both approaches (bronchodilators, steroids, and antibiotics only if there are signs of bacterial infection).
  - As with COPD, use **controlled oxygen** (target SpO₂ 88–92%) in anyone at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure, which is just as relevant in overlap patients as in pure COPD.
