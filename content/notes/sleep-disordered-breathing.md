background: |+
  A spectrum of conditions causing abnormal breathing during sleep, with **obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA)** by far the commonest.

  - **Obstructive sleep apnoea**: repeated partial (hypopnoea) or complete (apnoea) collapse of the pharyngeal airway during sleep, causing intermittent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation (frequent brief arousals as the airway reopens, usually not remembered by the patient). Risk factors: obesity (the strongest), male sex, neck circumference, retrognathia/craniofacial abnormalities, enlarged tonsils, alcohol and sedative use, and smoking.
  - **Central sleep apnoea**: breathing effort itself stops transiently (no airway obstruction) because the brainstem's respiratory drive is impaired — associated with heart failure (classically **Cheyne-Stokes breathing**, a crescendo-decrescendo pattern), opioid use, and some neurological conditions. Much less common than OSA.
  - **Obesity hypoventilation syndrome (Pickwickian syndrome)**: a distinct entity — chronic daytime hypercapnia (awake PaCO₂ >6 kPa) in someone with obesity, not fully explained by another lung or neuromuscular cause, usually with coexisting OSA. Reflects a combination of the mechanical load of obesity on breathing and blunted central respiratory drive.
  - **Consequences**: excessive daytime sleepiness (with real road traffic and occupational accident risk), hypertension, an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, arrhythmia (including atrial fibrillation), and (in more severe/longstanding OSA or obesity hypoventilation syndrome) pulmonary hypertension and type 2 respiratory failure.
diagnosis: |+
  - **OSA**: loud snoring, witnessed apnoeas (often reported by a partner, not the patient), choking/gasping during sleep, unrefreshing sleep, morning headache, and **excessive daytime sleepiness** — the cardinal daytime symptom, which is what drives most of the real-world harm (accidents, impaired function) and the DVLA implications.
  - Quantify sleepiness with the **Epworth Sleepiness Scale** (0–24; **≥11 is considered abnormal**) — useful for screening, monitoring treatment response, and informing (though not solely determining) DVLA decisions.
  - **Central sleep apnoea**: similar daytime symptoms, but ask specifically about heart failure symptoms and opioid use, since these are the key associated/causative conditions to identify and address.
  - **Obesity hypoventilation syndrome**: more marked daytime sleepiness and breathlessness than OSA alone, with signs of chronic hypercapnia (morning headache, confusion) and, in more advanced disease, signs of cor pulmonale (peripheral oedema, raised JVP).
investigations: |+
  - **Sleep study** is the key diagnostic test — ranging from **home overnight pulse oximetry/limited-channel studies** (screening, widely used first-line given accessibility) to full **polysomnography** (the reference standard, measuring airflow, effort, oxygenation and sleep staging) for more complex or uncertain cases.
  - Severity is graded by the **apnoea-hypopnoea index (AHI)**, events per hour of sleep: **mild 5–14, moderate 15–29, severe ≥30**.
  - **Obesity hypoventilation syndrome**: confirmed by an awake **ABG showing hypercapnia** (PaCO₂ >6 kPa) in someone with obesity and a sleep study showing sleep-disordered breathing, once other causes of hypercapnia have been considered.
  - Baseline assessment should also include BMI, neck circumference, blood pressure, and screening for associated cardiovascular risk and (in more severe/longstanding disease) an ECG/echo if pulmonary hypertension or cor pulmonale is suspected.
treatment: |+
  - **Lifestyle measures apply to everyone with OSA**: weight loss (can be curative in some cases), reducing alcohol and sedative use (especially near bedtime), smoking cessation, and positional therapy for those whose apnoeas are mainly supine.
  - **CPAP (continuous positive airway pressure)** is first-line for **moderate-to-severe symptomatic OSA** (NICE TA139) — a continuous positive pressure splints the airway open; effective, but adherence is the main practical challenge (mask fit, claustrophobia, nasal symptoms) and needs proactive support rather than a one-off fitting.
  - **Mandibular advancement devices** (worn at night, moving the jaw/tongue forward) are an alternative for mild-moderate OSA or where CPAP isn't tolerated.
  - **Surgery** (e.g. tonsillectomy where tonsillar hypertrophy is the clear cause, particularly relevant in children) is used in selected cases rather than routinely in typical adult OSA.
  - **Central sleep apnoea**: optimise treatment of the underlying cause (heart failure management, reviewing/reducing opioid dose where possible) — CPAP or adaptive servo-ventilation is used in some cases under specialist guidance, though adaptive servo-ventilation is specifically avoided in significant heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, where trial evidence showed harm.
  - **Obesity hypoventilation syndrome**: **NIV (usually overnight)** is typically needed in addition to the measures above, given the combination of mechanical load and blunted respiratory drive — weight loss remains important long-term but NIV addresses the hypoventilation in the meantime.
protocols: |+
  Driving (DVLA)

  > These rules matter practically — patients often aren't aware of them until asked directly, and a missed conversation here has real safety and legal consequences for the patient.

  - **OSA without excessive sleepiness**: no need to notify the DVLA, and driving can continue.
  - **OSA(S) with excessive sleepiness likely to impair safe driving (or any sleepiness from any cause with this effect)**: the patient **must stop driving immediately** and should be advised to do so as soon as this is suspected clinically — **the DVLA itself only needs to be notified once a formal diagnosis of OSA is confirmed**, but the patient should stop driving from the point of clinical suspicion if sleepiness is affecting safety.
  - Once treatment (usually CPAP) has controlled the sleepiness to the satisfaction of the treating clinician/sleep service, driving can resume — Group 2 (HGV/bus) licence holders face stricter monitoring requirements for ongoing compliance.
  - This responsibility rests with the patient in the first instance (advise them clearly and document that advice was given), not solely with the clinician notifying the DVLA directly.

  - Severe, untreated OSA/obesity hypoventilation syndrome presenting acutely with marked hypercapnic respiratory failure is managed as for any type 2 respiratory failure (see that card) — NIV, with investigation and treatment of any precipitant (commonly a chest infection on top of the underlying condition).
