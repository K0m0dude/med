background: |+
  Excess fluid within the pericardial space (normally containing only a small amount of lubricating fluid, 15–50 mL). An effusion is an anatomical/imaging finding; whether it causes **tamponade** depends on its size, the rate of accumulation, and the pericardium's compliance — see the dedicated Cardiac tamponade card for that physiological complication.

  - **Causes**: **idiopathic/viral** (often alongside pericarditis — see that card), **malignancy** (lung and breast cancer, lymphoma — often large and recurrent), **uraemia** (chronic kidney disease, especially on dialysis), **post-cardiac injury** (post-MI, including Dressler's syndrome — an autoimmune pericarditis/effusion weeks after MI or cardiac surgery), **hypothyroidism** (classically a large, slow-accumulating, well-tolerated effusion), **autoimmune/connective tissue disease** (SLE, rheumatoid arthritis), **bacterial/TB pericarditis**, trauma, and iatrogenic (post-procedure).
  - **Composition**: transudates (heart failure, hypothyroidism, nephrotic syndrome) versus exudates (infection, malignancy, autoimmune disease) — mirrors the same transudate/exudate logic as pleural effusions (see the Pleural disease factfile, Acute medicine).
diagnosis: |+
  - Often **asymptomatic**, found incidentally on imaging done for another reason — particularly when small or slow-growing. Symptomatic effusions cause breathlessness, chest discomfort/fullness, or cough, sometimes worse lying flat.
  - **Examination**: may be entirely normal with a small effusion. A large effusion can cause quiet/muffled heart sounds and, rarely, **Ewart's sign** (dullness and bronchial breathing below the angle of the left scapula, from lung compression by the effusion).
  - **The key clinical task is recognising when an effusion has progressed to tamponade** — new hypotension, raised JVP, tachycardia, or pulsus paradoxus in a patient with a known effusion should prompt immediate reassessment for tamponade (see that card) rather than being assumed to be "just the effusion."
investigations: |+
  - **Echocardiography** is the key test — confirms the effusion, estimates its size (small/moderate/large) and circumferential distribution (free-flowing versus loculated), and assesses for any physiological sign of tamponade (chamber collapse, IVC plethora) at the same time.
  - **ECG**: may show low-voltage QRS and, if a large effusion is present, electrical alternans (see the Cardiac tamponade card).
  - Chase the cause: U&E (uraemia), TFTs, inflammatory markers, autoimmune screen (ANA, rheumatoid factor) where suggested by the clinical picture, and malignancy work-up if no other cause is apparent, especially with a large or recurrent effusion.
  - **Pericardiocentesis for diagnostic fluid analysis** (cell count, cytology, culture, biochemistry — mirroring pleural fluid analysis) is used where the cause is unclear, particularly if malignancy or infection is suspected, alongside therapeutic drainage if the effusion is large or causing symptoms/tamponade.
treatment: |+
  - **Small, asymptomatic effusions with no tamponade physiology**: treat the underlying cause (if identified) and monitor with serial echocardiography — drainage is not needed just because an effusion is present.
  - **Large or symptomatic effusions**: **pericardiocentesis** (needle drainage, usually echo-guided) relieves symptoms and provides fluid for diagnosis.
  - **Recurrent effusions** (commonly malignant): a **pericardial window** (surgical or percutaneous, creating a permanent drainage route into the pleural or peritoneal space) or an indwelling pericardial catheter reduces the need for repeated aspiration.
  - **Uraemic effusion**: intensifying dialysis often resolves it; **hypothyroid effusion**: resolves with thyroid hormone replacement (see the Hypothyroidism card, Endocrinology) — both are reasons not to rush to drainage if the patient is otherwise stable, since treating the cause may be all that's needed.
  - **Dressler's syndrome**: NSAIDs (as for pericarditis — see that card); drainage reserved for a large or tamponading effusion.
protocols: |+
  - **Tamponade physiology developing in a patient with a known effusion** is a medical emergency — see the Cardiac tamponade card for diagnosis and management; do not wait for a scheduled follow-up echo if the clinical picture changes acutely.
  - A **new or rapidly enlarging effusion with no clear cause**, especially in an older patient or one with a history of malignancy, should prompt urgent investigation for an underlying cancer rather than being labelled idiopathic by default.
