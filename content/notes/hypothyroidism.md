background: >+
  - **Primary hypothyroidism (the vast majority of cases)**: the thyroid itself fails. **Hashimoto's (autoimmune) thyroiditis** is the commonest cause in iodine-sufficient countries, often with positive **anti-TPO antibodies**; other causes include iatrogenic (post-thyroidectomy, post-radioiodine, external neck radiotherapy), drug-induced (amiodarone, lithium, some immunotherapies), and iodine deficiency (globally the commonest cause overall).
  - **Secondary (central) hypothyroidism**: pituitary or hypothalamic disease reduces TSH (or TRH) drive — rarer, and the TSH is **not** raised despite a low T4 (a key difference from primary disease, where TSH rises appropriately). Causes overlap with the Pituitary disorders card (non-functioning adenoma, apoplexy, surgery, radiotherapy, Sheehan's syndrome).
  - **Subclinical hypothyroidism**: raised TSH with a normal free T4, often picked up incidentally — common, and the decision to treat depends on the degree of TSH elevation and the clinical picture (see Treatment).
diagnosis: >+
  - Fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, constipation, dry skin and hair, hair thinning, hoarse voice, menorrhagia, low mood, and reduced cognitive processing. Signs: bradycardia, delayed relaxation of tendon reflexes, goitre (in Hashimoto's — can also, less commonly, cause gland atrophy with no goitre), and in severe/longstanding disease, a puffy "myxoedematous" facial appearance and macroglossia.
  - **Secondary hypothyroidism** often presents alongside other pituitary hormone deficits rather than in isolation — look for associated features of hypogonadism, cortisol deficiency, or a visual field defect if a pituitary mass is the underlying cause.
investigations: >+
  - **TSH raised, free T4 low** = primary hypothyroidism. **TSH low/normal (inappropriately not raised) with a low free T4** = secondary/central hypothyroidism — this pattern should prompt assessment of the rest of the anterior pituitary axis and pituitary imaging, not just a thyroxine dose increase.
  - **Anti-TPO antibodies** support a Hashimoto's diagnosis (positive in ~95%) but are not required to start treatment if the biochemistry and clinical picture already fit.
  - **Subclinical hypothyroidism** thresholds commonly used: **TSH ≥10 mU/L** (on two occasions, a few weeks apart) — treat; **TSH 5.5–10 mU/L** — treat if symptomatic, antibody-positive, pregnant or planning pregnancy, or under 65 with a reasonable symptom burden (a trial of levothyroxine, reviewed after a few months); otherwise, recheck in 6 months.
treatment: >+
  - **Levothyroxine (T4)** replacement, titrated against **TSH** (aim for the normal range — in secondary hypothyroidism, use free T4 instead, since TSH cannot be used as the target). Typical adult starting/maintenance dose is often quoted as around **1.6 micrograms/kg/day**, but the *starting* dose and pace of titration depend heavily on age and cardiac risk (see below). Recheck TSH roughly **6–8 weeks** after any dose change, since it takes this long to equilibrate.
  - > Red flag: **do not start a full replacement dose in older patients or those with ischaemic heart disease** — start low (e.g. 25–50 micrograms/day) and increase gradually. A sudden jump in metabolic rate can precipitate angina or myocardial infarction in a heart that has adapted to a hypothyroid state.
  - **Practical absorption points** that matter clinically: take on an empty stomach, ideally **30–60 minutes before food**; separate from calcium, iron supplements and proton pump inhibitors (all reduce absorption); and review the dose if a patient switches brands, as bioavailability can differ slightly between formulations.
  - **Pregnancy**: hypothyroidism needs tighter control — increase the levothyroxine dose (classically by around 25–50%, often approximated as an extra 2 doses per week) as soon as pregnancy is confirmed, and check TSH each trimester, since maternal hypothyroidism affects fetal neurodevelopment, especially early on.
  - Secondary hypothyroidism: **always check for co-existing cortisol deficiency and treat it first** — see the next card section and the Pituitary disorders card; starting thyroxine alone can increase the metabolic clearance of cortisol and precipitate an adrenal crisis in someone with unrecognised ACTH deficiency.
protocols: >+
  Myxoedema coma

  > A rare but life-threatening decompensation of severe, usually longstanding, untreated hypothyroidism — often precipitated by infection, cold exposure, sedating drugs, or stopping thyroxine, typically in an older patient.

  - Features: **hypothermia**, reduced consciousness progressing to coma, bradycardia and hypotension, hyponatraemia, hypoglycaemia, and hypoventilation with CO₂ retention. Mortality is high even with treatment.
  - **Management**: critical care-level supportive care (passive rewarming, ventilatory support if needed, careful fluid and glucose management); **IV levothyroxine** (with or without added liothyronine (T3) for a faster effect, per specialist/ICU protocol); and **IV hydrocortisone** given *before or with* the thyroxine, since unmasked coexisting adrenal insufficiency (or a reduced cortisol reserve relative to the sudden rise in metabolic demand) can otherwise precipitate an adrenal crisis. Treat the precipitant (e.g. sepsis) at the same time.
  - Have a low threshold to involve endocrinology and critical care early — this diagnosis is easy to miss (an unconscious, hypothermic, bradycardic patient has a broad differential) but treatment cannot safely wait for a formal specialist review once suspected.
