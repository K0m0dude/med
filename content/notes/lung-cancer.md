background: |+
  The commonest cause of cancer death in the UK. **Smoking is the dominant risk factor** (including passive exposure); other risks include asbestos exposure (also causes mesothelioma — see the Occupational lung disease card), radon, and a family/genetic predisposition. Broadly split by histology, which drives both behaviour and treatment.

  - **Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC, ~80–85%)**: adenocarcinoma (commonest overall, and the commonest subtype in never-smokers), squamous cell carcinoma (strongly smoking-linked, tends to cavitate, more often central), and large cell carcinoma.
  - **Small cell lung cancer (SCLC, ~15%)**: arises from neuroendocrine cells, almost always in smokers, centrally located, characterised by **rapid growth, early metastasis, and a high initial response to chemotherapy** — but usually not curable, since it is nearly always disseminated (at least microscopically) by diagnosis. Strongly associated with paraneoplastic syndromes.
  - **Mesothelioma**: a distinct pleural (rarely peritoneal) malignancy, essentially always linked to **asbestos exposure**, often with a very long latency (20–50 years) between exposure and diagnosis — see the Occupational lung disease card for exposure and compensation detail.
  - **Metastatic spread**: lymphatic (hilar/mediastinal nodes), direct local invasion (chest wall, brachial plexus — a **Pancoast tumour**, at the lung apex, can cause Horner's syndrome and brachial plexus signs), and haematogenous (bone, liver, brain, adrenal glands — adrenal metastases are common and often asymptomatic).
diagnosis: |+
  - Persistent cough, haemoptysis, breathlessness, chest pain, unexplained weight loss and anorexia, recurrent or slowly-resolving chest infections. Often insidious, and frequently detected only once locally advanced or metastatic, since early disease is often asymptomatic.
  - Examination: clubbing, supraclavicular or persistent cervical lymphadenopathy, signs of a pleural effusion, and focal chest signs from a mass or post-obstructive collapse/consolidation.
  - **Paraneoplastic syndromes** (especially with SCLC): **SIADH** (ectopic ADH — see the AVP/SIADH card, Endocrinology), **ectopic ACTH** causing Cushing's syndrome (see the Adrenal disorders card), **Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome** (proximal weakness that *improves* with sustained contraction, unlike myasthenia gravis, from antibodies against presynaptic calcium channels), and hypercalcaemia (more typical of squamous NSCLC, via PTHrP, rather than true bone metastasis in many cases).
  - **Pancoast tumour**: Horner's syndrome (miosis, ptosis, anhidrosis — from sympathetic chain involvement) and brachial plexus signs (shoulder/arm pain, weakness, wasting of the small muscles of the hand) from apical tumour invasion.
  - A new, unprovoked **VTE** (DVT or PE) can be the first presentation of an underlying malignancy, including lung cancer — see the VTE card (Acute medicine).
investigations: |+
  - **NICE NG12 referral criteria**: refer urgently (suspected cancer pathway) for CXR findings suggestive of lung cancer, or urgent CXR (within 2 weeks) in anyone **≥40 years** with **two or more unexplained symptoms** (or one or more if they have ever smoked) from: cough, fatigue, breathlessness, chest pain, weight loss, appetite loss. **Consider** an urgent CXR for persistent/recurrent chest infection, clubbing, supraclavicular/persistent cervical lymphadenopathy, suspicious chest signs, or thrombocytosis. Unexplained weight loss, appetite loss, or an unprovoked DVT/PE should also prompt consideration of an urgent cancer pathway referral.
  - **CT chest (contrast-enhanced), extending to include the liver and adrenals**, is the key staging investigation once a CXR is suspicious or a strong clinical suspicion persists despite a normal CXR.
  - **PET-CT** for staging in anyone who could potentially have treatment with curative intent, to detect occult nodal or distant disease that CT alone may miss.
  - **Tissue diagnosis** — bronchoscopy with biopsy/washings (central lesions), CT-guided percutaneous biopsy (peripheral lesions), or EBUS (endobronchial ultrasound)-guided sampling of mediastinal nodes for both diagnosis and nodal staging. **Molecular/biomarker testing** (EGFR, ALK, ROS1, PD-L1 and others) on NSCLC tissue is now routine, since it directly determines whether targeted therapy or immunotherapy is suitable.
  - **TNM staging** underpins treatment planning in NSCLC. SCLC is more often staged pragmatically as **limited stage** (confined to one hemithorax/radiotherapy field) versus **extensive stage** (beyond this), since it is disseminated in the majority at diagnosis.
  - Every patient with suspected or confirmed lung cancer should be discussed at a **lung cancer MDT**.
treatment: |+
  - **NSCLC, early stage**: surgical resection (lobectomy, or a smaller resection/stereotactic radiotherapy if surgery isn't suitable due to comorbidity) is the mainstay for operable, localised disease, with adjuvant chemotherapy for higher-risk resected disease. **Radical (curative-intent) radiotherapy** (often with concurrent chemotherapy) is used for locally advanced disease not suitable for surgery.
  - **NSCLC, advanced/metastatic**: treatment is guided by molecular profile — **targeted therapy** (e.g. an EGFR or ALK inhibitor) for tumours with the relevant driver mutation, **immunotherapy** (checkpoint inhibitors, e.g. pembrolizumab) particularly where PD-L1 expression is high, and platinum-based chemotherapy where neither applies or as a further line — increasingly used in combination. Treatment is palliative in intent at this stage, aiming to prolong survival and control symptoms rather than cure.
  - **SCLC**: chemotherapy (platinum plus etoposide, or similar regimens) is the backbone for both limited and extensive stage, since surgery has almost no role given near-universal micrometastatic spread at diagnosis; radiotherapy is added for limited-stage disease. **Treatment should start without undue delay** given how rapidly SCLC can progress. **Prophylactic cranial irradiation** is considered in selected patients who respond well, to reduce the high rate of brain metastasis, though its use is individualised.
  - **Mesothelioma**: chemotherapy (± immunotherapy) is the mainstay for most patients, since the disease is rarely curable by surgery alone; management is largely palliative, with specific attention to pleural effusion control and symptom management. Patients (or their families) should be made aware of the **Industrial Injuries Disablement Benefit** and related compensation schemes given the occupational origin.
  - **Palliative care involvement early** is appropriate for most lung cancer patients given the overall stage distribution at diagnosis — symptom control (breathlessness, pain, cough), psychological support, and advance care planning run alongside oncological treatment rather than only after it is exhausted.
protocols: |+
  - **Superior vena cava obstruction, metastatic spinal cord compression, hypercalcaemia of malignancy, and neutropenic sepsis during chemotherapy** are all managed as described in the Cancer and oncology emergencies factfile (Acute medicine) — lung cancer (especially SCLC) is one of the commonest underlying causes of each of these.
  - **New brain metastases** (headache, seizure, focal neurological deficit, personality change) need urgent imaging and dexamethasone — see the Visual field defects and Pituitary disorders cards (Endocrinology) and the Cancer factfile (Acute medicine) for the general approach.
  - A **massive haemoptysis** from a tumour eroding into a vessel is managed as for any major haemoptysis (see the Bronchiectasis card for the general approach — positioning, resuscitation, bronchial artery embolisation) with urgent oncology/respiratory and interventional radiology input.
