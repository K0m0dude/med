background: |+
  Infection of bone and bone marrow. The route of spread, the host and the presence of prosthetic material determine the organism, the presentation and how hard it is to cure.

  - **Haematogenous**: the commonest route in children (metaphysis of long bones) and, in adults, the **vertebral bodies** (IV drug use, bacteraemia, endocarditis, urinary or line sources).
  - **Contiguous spread**: from an adjacent focus — **diabetic foot ulcers (the commonest adult scenario)**, pressure sores, open fractures, orthopaedic surgery and implants.
  - **Acute vs chronic**: acute osteomyelitis develops over days to weeks, with viable bone. **Chronic** osteomyelitis (often defined as persisting beyond ~6 weeks) is characterised by **devitalised bone (sequestrum)**, surrounding new bone (involucrum), sclerosis and often a draining **sinus tract**. It is as much a surgical and histological diagnosis as a time-based one.
  - **Risk factors**: diabetes and peripheral arterial disease, IV drug use, sickle cell disease, immunosuppression, orthopaedic hardware, open fractures, pressure ulcers, haemodialysis.
  - **Organisms**: *Staphylococcus aureus* (commonest, including MRSA); streptococci; **Salmonella** (sickle cell disease); **Pseudomonas** (IV drug use, puncture wounds through the shoe, diabetic foot); coagulase-negative staphylococci (implants); polymicrobial with anaerobes and Gram-negatives (diabetic foot, pressure sores); *Kingella kingae* (children under 4); *Brucella* (vertebral disease, unpasteurised dairy).

  Tuberculous osteomyelitis

  - Skeletal TB accounts for about 10% of extrapulmonary TB. The **spine is the commonest site (about half)**, then hip and knee. **Pott's disease** = spinal TB, usually thoracolumbar, arising from haematogenous seeding (often a reactivated focus).
  - Infection starts in the vertebral body next to a disc or under the anterior longitudinal ligament, tracks beneath the ligaments to involve **three or more levels**, and eventually collapses vertebrae (**gibbus** kyphosis). A **paravertebral or psoas "cold" abscess** (no heat, redness or pain) is typical.
  - More likely with HIV, other immunosuppression, and origin from or travel to high-incidence countries. The course is **indolent**: months of back pain, night sweats and weight loss, with neurological deficit (Pott's paraplegia) as the feared complication.

  > **Microbiology and pathology**
  >
  > - Long-bone metaphyses have slow-flowing, looped sinusoidal capillaries with relatively few phagocytes, so blood-borne bacteria settle here. *S. aureus* adheres via surface adhesins (MSCRAMMs, including fibronectin- and collagen-binding proteins).
  > - Inflammation within rigid bone raises intramedullary pressure and compromises the blood supply, causing **ischaemic bone necrosis (sequestrum)**. The periosteum is lifted and lays down reactive bone (involucrum); pus may drain through a cloaca and sinus tract.
  > - **Chronic disease persists because of biofilm** (glycocalyx-encased, slow-growing bacteria on dead bone and metal), small-colony variants that survive inside osteoblasts, and avascular bone that antibiotics and immune cells cannot reach. This is why debridement is central.
  > - Bone loss is host-mediated: IL-1, TNF-α and RANKL drive osteoclast activation, and neutrophil proteases add to tissue damage.
  > - TB is different: a Th1 response forms **caseating granulomas** in bone; there is little pyogenic response, hence the cold abscess and slow tempo.
diagnosis: |+
  - **Acute**: localised bone pain and tenderness, warmth, swelling, fever and malaise. Children may limp or refuse to bear weight. **Fever is often absent in adults** and in vertebral disease.
  - **Vertebral osteomyelitis/discitis**: insidious, unremitting back pain (including at night), often with raised inflammatory markers; **neurological signs signal epidural extension** (see Protocols).
  - **Chronic**: persistent or relapsing pain, a draining sinus, or a non-healing ulcer with exposed bone.
  - **Diabetic foot**: an ulcer that is deep or large, a **positive probe-to-bone test**, a "sausage toe", or visible bone should be treated as osteomyelitis until proven otherwise (see the diabetes complications notes).
  - **TB**: back pain with constitutional symptoms, a gibbus, cold abscess, or neurological deficit, in someone with relevant exposure or immunosuppression.
investigations: |+
  - **CRP and ESR** (ESR is especially useful in vertebral disease and for monitoring response); WCC is often normal in chronic disease. **Blood cultures before antibiotics** (positive in roughly half of haematogenous cases).
  - **Bone biopsy with culture and histology is the gold standard** for microbiological diagnosis. Take it **before antibiotics if the patient is stable**; send for TB culture and NAAT if suspected.
  - **Plain X-ray**: normal for the first 10–14 days, then periosteal reaction, lysis, sclerosis, sequestrum; disc-space narrowing and endplate erosion in vertebral disease.
  - **MRI** is the most sensitive early test (marrow oedema, abscess, epidural spread) and is the investigation of choice for suspected vertebral infection. CT shows sequestra and guides biopsy. Labelled white cell scan or FDG-PET where MRI is not possible (for example metalwork).
  - **TB**: MRI of the **whole spine** (skip lesions), CXR and sputum, IGRA (supportive but cannot distinguish latent from active disease), HIV test, and image-guided biopsy (caseating granulomas, acid-fast bacilli, culture, NAAT).
treatment: |+
  - **Acute (UK local guidelines are consistent; always check yours)**: start after cultures if the patient is stable, but don't delay if septic. Empirical **IV flucloxacillin 2 g QDS**; **clindamycin** if penicillin-allergic; **vancomycin or teicoplanin** if MRSA risk. Tailor to culture. After a period of IV treatment, **switch to a well-absorbed oral agent** (per microbiology). **Total course around 6 weeks** is typical for acute disease. Consider OPAT.
  - **Surgery** to drain abscesses, **debride necrotic bone and sequestra**, and remove or revise infected hardware. Antibiotics alone rarely cure chronic disease.
  - **Chronic**: **surgical debridement is the mainstay**, with culture-directed antibiotics for weeks to months, individualised with infectious diseases/microbiology and orthopaedics. Rifampicin is used as an adjunct against biofilm in implant-related infection (never as monotherapy).
  - **Diabetic foot osteomyelitis**: MDT foot team, offloading, revascularisation if ischaemic, bone resection or a prolonged antibiotic course (NICE NG19 and local policy).
  - **Tuberculous osteomyelitis (NICE NG33)**: the **standard 6-month regimen** (2 months RIPE then 4 months rifampicin and isoniazid, with pyridoxine) applies to bone and joint TB, **including spinal TB without CNS involvement**. Longer treatment (up to 12 months) is for **CNS TB** or spinal TB with CNS involvement. **Do not extend beyond 6 months for residual deformity.** Surgery is **not** routine; consider it only for **spinal instability or cord compression**. See the Tuberculosis card (Respiratory) for drugs and monitoring.
protocols: |+
  > Red flag: back pain with fever (or a known source of bacteraemia) plus **any new neurological sign, urinary retention or saddle numbness** is spinal epidural abscess until proven otherwise — urgent whole-spine MRI, blood cultures, and same-day neurosurgical/spinal opinion. Delay turns a treatable infection into permanent paralysis.

  - **Sepsis** from osteomyelitis: follow the Sepsis Six pathway (see the Sepsis and infection factfile, Acute medicine).
  - **Failure to improve** on appropriate antibiotics: reconsider an undrained collection, retained sequestrum or infected metalwork, resistant or unusual organisms (including TB), or a mimic (Charcot arthropathy, malignancy such as Ewing sarcoma, gout).
  - **Sickle cell disease**: bone pain is usually infarction, but osteomyelitis (classically *Salmonella* or *S. aureus*) mimics it — fever, persistent focal pain or failure to settle needs cultures and imaging (see the Sickle cell factfile).
