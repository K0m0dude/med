background: |+
  Three imported infections you will meet in UK practice after travel. **Malaria** is the one that kills quickly and must always be excluded first. See also the Fever in the returning traveller card.

  Malaria

  - ***Plasmodium falciparum*** (most severe; sub-Saharan Africa), ***P. vivax*** and ***P. ovale*** (relapsing), ***P. malariae*** (quartan, may persist for decades), ***P. knowlesi*** (zoonotic, South-East Asia, fast replication). Spread by female *Anopheles* mosquitoes.
  - **Most falciparum malaria presents within 3 months of return (and most within a month)**, but vivax/ovale can relapse months to years later.
  - Fever is **tertian** (every 48 h; *falciparum*, *vivax*, *ovale*), **quartan** (72 h; *malariae*) or daily (*knowlesi*), but a classic periodicity is often absent early on — don't wait for it.

  Typhoid and paratyphoid (enteric fever)

  - ***Salmonella* Typhi** and ***S.* Paratyphi A/B/C**, spread faecal–orally through contaminated food and water. Most UK cases are from **South Asia**, with a growing problem of **fluoroquinolone resistance** and, from parts of Pakistan, **extensively drug-resistant (XDR) strains** (resistant to ceftriaxone).
  - Humans are the only reservoir. **Chronic carriage** (gallbladder) can persist for years.

  Dengue

  - **Flavivirus (DENV-1 to 4)** transmitted by *Aedes aegypti* and *albopictus*; the commonest arboviral infection of travellers, especially from Asia, the Caribbean and Latin America. Incubation 4–10 days.
  - **Second infection with a different serotype carries the greatest risk of severe disease.**

  > **Microbiology and pathology**
  >
  > - **Malaria life cycle**: sporozoites → **hepatocyte (liver stage; asymptomatic)** → merozoites → **red cell invasion and asexual replication (erythrocytic schizogony; symptoms occur when infected red cells rupture)** → some differentiate into gametocytes for transmission to the mosquito. ***P. vivax* and *P. ovale* form dormant hypnozoites in the liver** (causing relapse; need primaquine or tafenoquine after G6PD testing).
  > - **Falciparum severity**: infected red cells express **PfEMP1** on surface knobs, binding endothelial receptors (ICAM-1, EPCR, CD36) so that they **sequester in the microvasculature** (brain, placenta via VAR2CSA, gut, kidney) and form rosettes with uninfected cells. This causes microvascular obstruction, tissue hypoxia, cytokine-driven endothelial activation (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6) and **cerebral malaria**, as well as haemolysis, anaemia, lactic acidosis, hypoglycaemia and AKI.
  > - **Host genetics**: sickle trait, thalassaemia and G6PD deficiency protect against severe falciparum malaria; **Duffy-negative** red cells are resistant to *P. vivax*, which needs the Duffy antigen to invade.
  > - **Typhoid**: invades the terminal ileum via M cells and **Peyer's patches** (SPI-1 type III secretion), survives and replicates **inside macrophages** (SPI-2) and disseminates via the mesenteric nodes to the liver, spleen, bone marrow and gallbladder. The **Vi capsular polysaccharide** limits complement deposition and phagocytosis (and is the vaccine target). Hyperplasia then necrosis of Peyer's patches in the third week causes **haemorrhage and perforation**.
  > - **Dengue**: severe disease is largely **vascular leak** — NS1 antigen and a cytokine surge damage the endothelial glycocalyx; **antibody-dependent enhancement** (non-neutralising antibodies from a prior serotype help the virus enter Fc-receptor-bearing monocytes) increases viral load in secondary infection.
diagnosis: |+
  Malaria

  - Fever with headache, rigors, myalgia, vomiting, diarrhoea and malaise, sometimes with jaundice. **Any fever after travel to a malaria-endemic area is malaria until proven otherwise**, whatever the prophylaxis taken.
  - **Severe falciparum malaria**: impaired consciousness or seizures, prostration, respiratory distress/pulmonary oedema, shock, acidosis, hypoglycaemia, AKI, jaundice, severe anaemia (Hb <80 g/L), spontaneous bleeding/DIC, **parasitaemia >10%** (higher-risk even above 2% in non-immune travellers).

  Typhoid

  - **Stepwise rising fever** over days, headache, malaise, abdominal pain, **constipation more than diarrhoea early on**, **relative bradycardia** (Faget sign), hepatosplenomegaly, and sometimes **rose spots** (pale blanching macules on the trunk). Leucopenia is common.
  - **Third-week complications**: intestinal haemorrhage and perforation (surgical emergency), encephalopathy.

  Dengue (WHO classification)

  - **Febrile phase (days 1–3/4)**: abrupt fever, severe headache, **retro-orbital pain**, myalgia/arthralgia ("break-bone"), rash.
  - **Critical phase (around defervescence, days 3–7)**: **plasma leakage** (pleural effusion, ascites, rising haematocrit), thrombocytopenia, bleeding and shock. **Warning signs**: abdominal pain/tenderness, persistent vomiting, clinical fluid accumulation, mucosal bleeding, lethargy/restlessness, liver enlargement >2 cm, rising haematocrit with a rapidly falling platelet count.
  - **Recovery phase**: reabsorption of leaked fluid, with a risk of **fluid overload**, and a late itchy rash.
investigations: |+
  - **Malaria**: **rapid diagnostic test and thick and thin blood films, urgently, same day.** If negative and suspicion persists, **repeat films at 12–24 hour intervals (three in total)**. Report species and **parasite count (%)**. Also FBC (thrombocytopenia, anaemia), U&E, LFTs, glucose, lactate, clotting, blood cultures, and G6PD status (before primaquine/tafenoquine).
  - **Typhoid**: **blood cultures** (positive in a majority; take before antibiotics), with stool and urine culture later; bone marrow culture is more sensitive when antibiotics have been given. FBC (leucopenia), LFTs (mild transaminitis). **Request sensitivities** — resistance is common.
  - **Dengue**: **NS1 antigen and/or PCR in the first ~5–7 days**, then **IgM/IgG serology**. FBC with **serial haematocrit and platelets**, LFTs (raised AST/ALT), U&E.
  - All three: a travel history timeline, plus exclusion of other causes (see the Fever in the returning traveller card).
treatment: |+
  Malaria (UK Malaria Treatment Guidelines; always involve infectious diseases/tropical medicine)

  - **Severe falciparum**: **IV artesunate 2.4 mg/kg at 0, 12 and 24 hours, then daily**, in a high-dependency/ICU setting, with glucose and lactate monitoring. Once the patient can take oral medication (and parasitaemia has fallen), **complete with a full course of an oral artemisinin combination therapy (ACT)**.
  - **Uncomplicated falciparum**: an **ACT** (artemether–lumefantrine given at 0, 8, 24, 36, 48 and 60 hours with fatty food, or dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine); atovaquone–proguanil is an alternative.
  - **Non-falciparum**: **ACT or chloroquine** for the blood stage, then **primaquine (14 days) or tafenoquine (single dose) after confirming G6PD status** to clear hypnozoites in *P. vivax* and *P. ovale*. *P. knowlesi* is treated as potentially severe.
  - **Prevention** (**ABCD**): Awareness of risk, Bite prevention, Chemoprophylaxis (choice by area: atovaquone–proguanil, doxycycline or mefloquine), prompt Diagnosis of any fever after travel. Malaria is notifiable.

  Typhoid

  - **Azithromycin** for uncomplicated disease (**fluoroquinolones** are unreliable from South Asia); **IV ceftriaxone 2 g daily** for severe or complicated disease, typically 7–14 days. **XDR strains** (especially Pakistan) need azithromycin and/or a carbapenem on specialist advice. Treat complications (perforation: surgery).
  - **Public health**: notifiable; exclude food handlers/healthcare/childcare workers until microbiological clearance; vaccines (Vi polysaccharide, oral Ty21a) protect incompletely against Typhi and **not at all against Paratyphi**.

  Dengue

  - **Supportive**: oral fluids; **paracetamol** for fever; **avoid NSAIDs and aspirin** (bleeding risk). **Titrated isotonic IV crystalloid** for warning signs or shock, guided by haematocrit and clinical response — avoid over-resuscitation (the leakage reverses in the recovery phase). Platelets are transfused only for significant bleeding, not for a number. There is no specific antiviral.
  - Admit patients with **warning signs**, comorbidity, pregnancy, extremes of age, or who cannot maintain oral intake.
protocols: |+
  > Red flag: a febrile traveller with a positive malaria film **and any severe feature** needs IV artesunate and critical care input the same day. A **negative first film does not exclude malaria**. Never withhold treatment for a delayed senior review.

  - **Cerebral malaria and hypoglycaemia**: check capillary glucose (quinine- and disease-related hypoglycaemia is common) and treat seizures; avoid steroids and fluid overload (pulmonary oedema).
  - **Typhoid perforation** (sudden severe abdominal pain, peritonism, usually in week 3): urgent surgical review; resuscitate and continue antibiotics.
  - **Severe dengue** (shock, severe bleeding, organ failure): critical care; careful fluid titration and monitoring of haematocrit.
  - Rapidly deteriorating returning traveller with haemorrhagic features: also run the **viral haemorrhagic fever risk assessment** (see the Fever in the returning traveller card).
