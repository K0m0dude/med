background: |+
  Blood in the pleural space. The great majority are **traumatic** (blunt or penetrating chest injury, often alongside rib fractures — see that card), from laceration of the lung parenchyma, an intercostal vessel, an internal mammary vessel, or, in major injury, a great vessel. Also seen **iatrogenically** (after central line insertion, thoracic surgery, or pleural procedures) and rarely **spontaneously** (anticoagulation, a bleeding diathesis, a ruptured pleural adhesion/vascular malformation, or bleeding from a tumour).
  - Blood in the pleural space is also irritant and can itself trigger an inflammatory/fibrotic reaction if not adequately drained, which is part of the rationale for early, complete drainage rather than a "wait and see" approach in anything beyond a trivial collection.
diagnosis: |+
  - Dyspnoea, tachypnoea, pleuritic chest pain, and — in significant bleeding — signs of haemorrhagic shock (tachycardia, hypotension, cool peripheries). Reduced chest expansion, **dullness to percussion**, and reduced breath sounds on the affected side (can mimic a simple pleural effusion clinically).
  - Always consider it alongside associated chest trauma — rib fractures, pneumothorax (a combined **haemopneumothorax** is common), pulmonary contusion — and examine for these together rather than in isolation.
investigations: |+
  - **CXR** is the initial screening test but is **unreliable for estimating volume** — a large haemothorax can look deceptively modest on a supine film, since blood layers out posteriorly rather than forming the obvious meniscus seen on an erect film.
  - **CT chest with contrast** is more sensitive, better estimates volume, and identifies a bleeding source or associated injury — used whenever trauma imaging is otherwise indicated.
  - **Bedside ultrasound (eFAST)** is useful in the acute trauma setting for rapid detection, particularly where CT is not immediately available.
  - **Pleural fluid analysis** (if diagnostic/therapeutic aspiration is performed in a less clear-cut, non-trauma context): a **pleural fluid haematocrit ≥50% of the peripheral blood haematocrit** defines a haemothorax (as opposed to a blood-stained effusion from another cause).
treatment: |+
  - **Chest drain (tube thoracostomy)** is the mainstay of initial management for all but the smallest, truly incidental collections — this both drains the blood (preventing fibrothorax/empyema) and allows ongoing blood loss to be measured, which directly informs the decision about surgery. A reasonably large-bore drain is generally preferred, since blood (and clot) drains less reliably through a narrow tube than simple pleural fluid does.
  - **Autotransfusion** of drained blood is used in some major trauma settings where the system/expertise is available.
  - Manage haemorrhagic shock in parallel as needed — IV access, crystalloid/blood products, and activation of the major haemorrhage protocol for significant ongoing blood loss (see the GI bleeding and Fluid therapy cards, Acute medicine, for the general major haemorrhage approach).
  - **Retained haemothorax** (blood/clot persisting despite a chest drain, from loculation) — seen on follow-up imaging — may need **image-guided drain repositioning, intrapleural fibrinolytics, or video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS)** to evacuate the clot and reduce the risk of later empyema or fibrothorax (lung trapped by organised pleural scarring, causing a restrictive defect).
protocols: |+
  Massive haemothorax — indications for urgent thoracotomy

  > Thoracotomy is the definitive treatment for ongoing major intrathoracic bleeding that a chest drain alone will not control, and the decision is based on both the chest drain output and the patient's haemodynamic status, not drain output in isolation.

  - **Initial chest drain output >1,500 mL** on insertion, **or**
  - **Ongoing output of around 200 mL/hour for 2–4 consecutive hours**, **or**
  - Persistent haemodynamic instability despite resuscitation, attributable to intrathoracic bleeding.
  - This needs immediate senior trauma/cardiothoracic surgical involvement — don't wait for the full time window to elapse if the patient is unstable; the thresholds above guide the decision rather than replace clinical judgement moment to moment.
  - As with any chest trauma, **suspected tension pneumothorax takes priority** and is treated immediately on clinical grounds (needle decompression) — see the Pleural disease factfile (Acute medicine) — before haemothorax-specific management proceeds.
