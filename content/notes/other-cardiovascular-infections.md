background: |+
  Infective endocarditis has its own card (Acute medicine). This card covers the other infections of the heart and vessels, including those that **complicate prosthetic material**, and the **immune-mediated** cardiac disease that follows group A streptococcal infection.

  Rheumatic fever

  - A **delayed, immune-mediated** complication of **group A streptococcal (GAS) pharyngitis**, 2–4 weeks later, in children and young people (peak 5–15 years). Now rare in the UK but common in low-resource settings. Can progress to **chronic rheumatic heart disease** (mitral stenosis most characteristically, years later).

  Cardiac device and prosthetic infections

  - **Cardiac implantable electronic device (CIED) infection**: pocket infection or device-related endocarditis (coagulase-negative staphylococci and *S. aureus*), usually within months of implantation or a generator change.
  - **Prosthetic valve endocarditis**: **early (<1 year)** — mainly healthcare-associated *S. aureus* and coagulase-negative staphylococci; **late** — resembles native valve endocarditis.

  Vascular infections

  - **Infected (mycotic) aneurysm**: infection of the arterial wall, either seeding of an atherosclerotic aneurysm or septic embolism from endocarditis (including intracranial aneurysms). Organisms: *S. aureus*, *Salmonella*, streptococci. **Vascular graft infection**: early (<4 months; *S. aureus*, Gram-negatives) or late (low-virulence organisms such as coagulase-negative staphylococci, biofilm).
  - **Infective pericarditis and myocarditis**: purulent pericarditis (*S. aureus*, pneumococcus), **TB pericarditis**, viral myocarditis (coxsackie B), **Lyme carditis** (*Borrelia*), **Chagas disease** (*Trypanosoma cruzi*), and diphtheria toxin myocarditis. See the Pericarditis and myocarditis card (Cardiology).

  > **Microbiology and pathology**
  >
  > - **Group A streptococcus (*S. pyogenes*)**: Gram-positive cocci in chains, β-haemolytic, Lancefield group A. The **M protein** is the key virulence and immune-targeting antigen. Rheumatic fever arises by **molecular mimicry**: antibodies and T cells against M protein and the group A carbohydrate cross-react with **cardiac myosin, laminin and valvular endothelium** (and basal ganglia in Sydenham's chorea), causing a type II hypersensitivity/T-cell-mediated injury, not direct infection.
  > - **Histology**: **Aschoff bodies** (granulomas with fibrinoid necrosis, multinucleate giant cells and **Anitschkow cells** — macrophages with "caterpillar" chromatin) in all three layers (pancarditis). Healing causes valve leaflet fibrosis, commissural fusion and calcification.
  > - **Biofilm on prosthetic material**: coagulase-negative staphylococci (*S. epidermidis*) form biofilm using the **ica operon** polysaccharide intercellular adhesin; these are tolerant to antibiotics and immune cells, which is why **device or graft removal** is usually needed.
  > - **Mycotic aneurysm** is a misnomer (the term does not mean fungal): bacteria in the vessel wall cause inflammation and weakening, so aneurysms can enlarge or rupture rapidly.
diagnosis: |+
  Rheumatic fever (**revised Jones criteria**: evidence of preceding GAS infection **plus 2 major, or 1 major and 2 minor** criteria)

  - **Major**: **carditis** (including subclinical, seen on echocardiography), **migratory polyarthritis**, **Sydenham's chorea**, **erythema marginatum**, **subcutaneous nodules**. Polyarthralgia (and monoarthritis) count in high-risk populations.
  - **Minor**: fever, raised ESR/CRP, prolonged PR interval, arthralgia (lower thresholds in high-risk populations).
  - **Evidence of preceding GAS**: raised or rising **ASO or anti-DNase B titres**, positive throat culture or rapid antigen test, or recent scarlet fever.

  Device, graft and vascular infections

  - **CIED**: fever, local pocket erythema, swelling, pain, erosion or pus; or bacteraemia with no other source in a patient with a device.
  - **Vascular graft infection**: fever, groin/graft-site pain or sinus, pus, bacteraemia; **aorto-enteric fistula** may present as a "herald" GI bleed.
  - **Mycotic aneurysm**: fever, a tender pulsatile mass, or unexplained bacteraemia; **intracranial** mycotic aneurysm presents with headache, focal deficit or subarachnoid haemorrhage in a patient with endocarditis.
  - **Lyme carditis**: **AV block** (often fluctuating high-grade block) in a young person with tick exposure, ± erythema migrans.
investigations: |+
  - **Rheumatic fever**: **throat swab/rapid antigen test**, **ASO and anti-DNase B**, ESR/CRP, **ECG** (prolonged PR), **echocardiography** (valvulitis, regurgitation, effusion), blood cultures to exclude infective endocarditis.
  - **CIED infection**: **at least two sets of blood cultures** (before antibiotics), **transoesophageal echocardiography** (lead vegetations), pocket swab/fluid culture at extraction, and CT/PET-CT if the focus is unclear.
  - **Vascular graft or mycotic aneurysm**: **blood cultures**, **CT angiography** (peri-graft fluid/gas, aneurysm morphology), and **FDG-PET/CT** for equivocal graft infection. Look for an endocarditis source with echocardiography.
  - **Lyme carditis**: Lyme serology (two-tier), ECG monitoring.
treatment: |+
  - **Rheumatic fever**: **penicillin (phenoxymethylpenicillin or a single IM dose of benzathine benzylpenicillin)** to eradicate GAS; **aspirin or NSAIDs** for arthritis; **corticosteroids** for severe carditis; heart failure management; and treatment for chorea in severe cases. **Secondary prophylaxis** with **benzathine benzylpenicillin every 3–4 weeks (or oral penicillin V twice daily)** for many years — at least 10 years or until age 21 (whichever is longer), and longer if there is carditis or residual valve disease.
  - **CIED infection**: **complete device extraction** (generator and all leads) **plus prolonged IV antibiotics**; antibiotics alone usually fail. Re-implant only after infection has been cleared (blood cultures negative), at a new site.
  - **Prosthetic valve endocarditis**: prolonged IV antibiotics (often with rifampicin and gentamicin for staphylococci) and **early surgical review** (see the Infective endocarditis factfile, Acute medicine).
  - **Mycotic aneurysm/graft infection**: **prolonged IV antibiotics plus surgical debridement and vascular reconstruction** (excision with in-situ or extra-anatomic repair); endovascular repair may be used as a bridge in unstable patients.
  - **Purulent pericarditis**: **urgent drainage** (pericardiocentesis/window) plus IV antibiotics. **TB pericarditis**: standard antituberculous therapy with prednisolone (NICE NG33) and drainage if tamponade. **Lyme carditis**: IV ceftriaxone, with temporary pacing for high-grade block (it usually resolves).
protocols: |+
  > Red flag: **fever with a prosthetic valve, device or graft** is endocarditis or device/graft infection until proven otherwise — two or three sets of blood cultures before antibiotics, echocardiography, and early input from cardiology/infectious diseases.

  - **Aortic mycotic aneurysm rupture** or **aorto-enteric fistula** (GI bleed in a patient with an aortic graft) is a surgical emergency — resuscitation, CT angiography and urgent vascular surgery.
  - **Acute rheumatic fever with severe carditis** and heart failure needs inpatient care and cardiology input.
  - See the Pericardial effusion and Cardiac tamponade cards (Cardiology) for the emergency approach to purulent pericarditis causing tamponade.
