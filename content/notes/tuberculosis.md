background: |+
  Infection with *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, spread by airborne droplets from someone with active pulmonary/laryngeal disease. Most initial infections are contained by the immune system (granuloma formation) without ever causing illness.

  - **Latent TB infection (LTBI)**: the organism is present but contained — **asymptomatic and not infectious**. Around a quarter to a third of the world's population is estimated to have latent infection; only a minority will ever progress to active disease, but the risk is lifelong and rises sharply with any cause of immunosuppression.
  - **Active (primary or reactivation) disease**: the immune system fails to contain the infection, either at the time of initial exposure (primary TB — more common in young children and the immunosuppressed) or years to decades later as latent infection reactivates (more common in adults, often with a triggering factor).
  - **Risk factors for reactivation/progression**: HIV (by far the most significant — TB and HIV markedly increase each other's severity and risk), other immunosuppression (biologics, chemotherapy, transplant), diabetes, malnutrition, chronic kidney disease, silicosis (see the Occupational lung disease card), homelessness, overcrowding, and close contact with an infectious case.
  - **Extrapulmonary TB** (more likely with HIV co-infection): lymph nodes (commonest extrapulmonary site), pleura, bone/spine (Pott's disease), CNS (TB meningitis), genitourinary, and miliary (disseminated, via haematogenous spread — more severe, can affect almost any organ).
  - **Drug-resistant TB**: **MDR-TB** = resistant to at least isoniazid and rifampicin; **XDR-TB** = MDR-TB with additional resistance to key second-line drugs — both need specialist management and markedly longer, more complex treatment.
diagnosis: |+
  - **Pulmonary TB**: a chronic (typically >3 weeks), often productive cough, haemoptysis, fever, night sweats, weight loss and malaise — a classic "constitutional" symptom pattern alongside the respiratory ones.
  - **Extrapulmonary disease** presents according to the site involved: painless, often matted cervical lymphadenopathy; back pain and/or a spinal deformity (Pott's disease); subacute meningitis with cranial nerve palsies (TB meningitis); sterile pyuria and haematuria (genitourinary TB).
  - Ask specifically about **country of birth/travel, known TB contacts, HIV status, and immunosuppression** — these materially change the pre-test probability and shape the work-up.
investigations: |+
  - **CXR**: classically upper lobe cavitation, consolidation, or fibrotic scarring in reactivation disease; a **Ghon focus** (primary granuloma) ± hilar lymphadenopathy (the combination is a **Ghon complex**) in primary infection; **miliary TB** shows widespread small ("millet seed") nodules.
  - **Sputum samples (at least three) for microscopy (Ziehl-Neelsen stain for acid-fast bacilli), culture, and nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT)** — culture remains the reference standard (also allows drug susceptibility testing) but can take **weeks**, so NAAT is used for a faster result and to guide early isolation/treatment decisions. If someone can't produce sputum spontaneously, induced sputum or bronchoscopy with lavage is used.
  - **Latent TB testing**: **Mantoux (tuberculin skin test)** or an **interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA)**, used for contact tracing and new-entrant/immunosuppression screening — **neither test distinguishes latent infection from active disease**, so a positive result in someone with compatible symptoms still needs full investigation for active TB (sputum/imaging), not just latent TB treatment.
  - **HIV test** should be offered to everyone newly diagnosed with TB, given how strongly the two interact.
  - **Extrapulmonary disease**: lymph node biopsy/aspirate, CSF analysis (for suspected TB meningitis — typically high protein, low glucose, lymphocytic pleocytosis), or imaging/biopsy of the relevant site, with the same microbiological tests applied to that sample.
treatment: |+
  - **Active respiratory (and most non-respiratory) TB — standard regimen**: **rifampicin and isoniazid for a full 6 months**, with **pyrazinamide and ethambutol added for the first 2 months** only ("RIPE"). **Pyridoxine (vitamin B6)** is co-prescribed with isoniazid to reduce the risk of peripheral neuropathy.
  - **TB meningitis and other CNS/bone disease** is treated with a **longer course** (typically around **12 months**), reflecting poorer drug penetration and higher stakes of treatment failure; corticosteroids are added for TB meningitis to reduce inflammatory complications.
  - **Latent TB treatment** (once active disease has been excluded): **3 months of rifampicin and isoniazid (with pyridoxine)**, or **6 months of isoniazid alone (with pyridoxine)** — the choice depends on interactions, hepatotoxicity risk and adherence considerations.
  - **Key drug side effects to know**: **rifampicin** — hepatotoxicity, a potent enzyme inducer (reduces efficacy of the combined oral contraceptive pill and many other drugs), and harmless orange discolouration of body fluids (tears, urine — warn the patient, including about soft contact lens staining); **isoniazid** — hepatotoxicity and peripheral neuropathy (hence routine pyridoxine); **pyrazinamide** — hepatotoxicity and hyperuricaemia (can precipitate gout); **ethambutol** — **optic neuritis/colour vision disturbance** (check visual acuity and colour vision before starting and if any visual symptoms develop, and warn the patient to report changes promptly).
  - **Directly observed therapy (DOT)** is used for patients at higher risk of non-adherence (given the serious consequences of incomplete treatment — relapse and the development of drug resistance).
  - **MDR/XDR-TB**: managed by specialist TB services with individualised, prolonged (often many months to around 2 years historically, though newer shorter regimens are increasingly used) multi-drug regimens guided by susceptibility testing.
protocols: |+
  - TB is a **notifiable disease** — notify the local health protection team promptly on clinical suspicion, without waiting for microbiological confirmation, since **contact tracing** needs to start as early as possible.
  - **Infectiousness and isolation**: pulmonary/laryngeal TB is generally considered non-infectious after **about 2 weeks of appropriate treatment** (or sooner if smear-negative) — isolate (ideally with negative-pressure facilities for MDR-TB or high-risk cases) until this point, particularly around immunocompromised patients and young children.
  - **Contact tracing**: close contacts are offered testing (Mantoux/IGRA ± CXR) to identify latent or active infection; **BCG vaccination** may be offered to unvaccinated, TB-negative contacts in some circumstances, particularly in children, per national guidance.
  - **Any patient with TB and a significant new visual symptom on ethambutol** should have the drug stopped immediately and an urgent ophthalmology/specialist review — don't wait for a scheduled check, since early recognition is what prevents permanent visual loss.
  - **Deteriorating conscious level, new focal neurology, or a severe headache in someone with known or suspected TB** should raise concern for TB meningitis, a medical emergency needing urgent imaging, LP (where safe) and treatment — delay materially worsens outcome.
