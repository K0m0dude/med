background: >+
  - **Type 1 (T1DM):** autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β-cells (anti-GAD,
  IA-2, ZnT8 antibodies) leading to absolute insulin deficiency. Usually
  younger, lean, rapid onset. Needs insulin for life. Without insulin,
  unrestrained lipolysis leads to ketogenesis.

  - **Type 2 (T2DM):** insulin resistance plus progressive relative β-cell
  failure. Risk factors: obesity, age, family history, South Asian, Black
  African-Caribbean and Middle Eastern ethnicity, gestational diabetes, PCOS.

  - **Pancreatic (type 3c) diabetes:** loss of islet cells after chronic
  pancreatitis, pancreatic cancer or pancreatectomy, cystic fibrosis,
  haemochromatosis. Both **insulin and glucagon** are lost, so it is brittle
  with a high risk of hypoglycaemia. Exocrine failure is common (steatorrhea,
  weight loss, needs pancreatic enzyme replacement such as Creon).

  - **Gestational diabetes:** Gestational diabetes is diabetes that can develop
  during pregnancy. It affects women who haven't been affected by diabetes
  before and usually goes away again after giving birth. It is diagnosed 24 to
  28 weeks into pregnancy.

  - **Type 5 diabetes:** Formerly known as malnutrition-related diabetes
  mellitus, or MRDM. It stems from severe chronic childhood or foetal
  undernutrition that stunts proper pancreatic development and insulin capacity.

  - Other causes: steroids, Cushing's, acromegaly, thiazides, antipsychotics,
  checkpoint inhibitors (autoimmune diabetes and DKA), MODY, neonatal diabetes.

  - **DKA:** insulin deficiency with counter-regulatory hormone excess leads to
  hyperglycaemia, ketone production, osmotic diuresis, dehydration and metabolic
  acidosis. Total body K⁺ is depleted even if the measured K⁺ is normal or high.

  - **HHS:** severe hyperglycaemia and dehydration with enough insulin to
  suppress ketosis. Older T2DM patients, developing over days, high mortality.


  ### Complications


  - **Mechanism**: diabetic foot disease results from three things acting
  together — **peripheral neuropathy** (sensory loss → unnoticed injury; motor →
  small muscle wasting and deformity; autonomic → dry, cracked skin and abnormal
  blood flow), **peripheral arterial disease** (impaired healing, risk of
  critical limb ischaemia), and impaired infection control from hyperglycaemia
  itself.


  - **Screening (NICE NG19)**: annual foot assessment for everyone with diabetes
  — 10 g monofilament and vibration sense (neuropathy), foot pulses (vascular),
  and skin/deformity/footwear check — stratified into **low / moderate / high
  risk**, or **“active”** if an ulcer or infection is already present, which
  sets the review frequency and whether a foot protection service is involved.


  - **Diabetic foot ulcer**: refer to the **multidisciplinary foot care
  service** for any new ulceration, spreading infection, or suspected Charcot
  foot. Management centres on **debridement and offloading** (pressure removal
  is at least as important as the dressing used) plus treating infection (oral
  antibiotics for mild infection; IV antibiotics and urgent surgical/vascular
  assessment for limb-threatening infection) and assessing for revascularisation
  if ischaemia is contributing.
    > Red flag: a **hot, swollen, often painless foot** in someone with diabetic neuropathy is **Charcot neuroarthropathy** until proven otherwise — easily mistaken for cellulitis or gout. Plain X-ray can be normal early (MRI is more sensitive). Treatment is **immediate non-weight-bearing and offloading** (total contact cast), **not antibiotics** unless genuinely infected — treating it as cellulitis without offloading allows progressive joint destruction and deformity. NICE NG19 specifies referral to the multidisciplinary foot service **within 1 working day**, with triage within a further working day.

    **Other complications**:
    - **Retinopathy**: annual digital photographic screening from diagnosis (T2DM) — background → pre-proliferative → proliferative, plus maculopathy; pan-retinal photocoagulation for proliferative disease, anti-VEGF injections for diabetic macular oedema.
    - **Nephropathy**: annual urine ACR and eGFR; ACEi/ARB if ACR is raised (even if normotensive), with an SGLT2 inhibitor added for renal protection — see the Chronic kidney disease factfile (Acute medicine).
    - **Painful peripheral neuropathy** (NICE NG173): amitriptyline, duloxetine, gabapentin or pregabalin — try one, switch rather than combine if ineffective; tramadol as rescue only.
    - **Autonomic neuropathy**: gastroparesis (erratic glucose control, early satiety — prokinetics), postural hypotension, erectile dysfunction, gustatory sweating.
    - **Macrovascular disease** (IHD, stroke, PAD)

diagnosis: >+
  > **Diagnostic criteria:** symptoms plus random glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L, or
  fasting ≥7.0, or HbA1c ≥48 mmol/mol (6.5%). If asymptomatic, confirm with a
  second test. HbA1c is unreliable with anaemia, haemoglobinopathy, recent
  transfusion, pancreatic disease and rapid-onset T1DM.


  - Classic symptoms: polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, fatigue, blurred
  vision, recurrent infections (thrush, skin).

  - **DKA:** vomiting, abdominal pain (can mimic an acute abdomen), Kussmaul
  breathing (deep, rapid), acetone breath, dehydration (dry mucosa, tachycardia,
  hypotension), drowsiness. Precipitants: infection, missed insulin, new
  diagnosis, MI, pancreatitis, steroids, **SGLT2 inhibitors (euglycaemic DKA,
  glucose may be under 14)**.

  - **DKA diagnosis (JBDS):** all three of glucose >11 mmol/L or known diabetes,
  ketones ≥3.0 mmol/L (or urine ketones 2+), and venous pH <7.3 or bicarbonate
  <15 mmol/L.

  - **Severe DKA** (needs senior or critical care input): ketones >6, pH <7.0,
  bicarbonate <5, K⁺ <3.5, GCS <12, SBP <90, HR >100 or <60, anion gap >16.

  - **HHS:** glucose ≥30 mmol/L, osmolality ≥320 mosm/kg, hypovolaemia, ketones
  <3, pH >7.3 and bicarbonate >15.

  - **Hypoglycaemia:** glucose <4 mmol/L. Sweating, tremor, palpitations,
  hunger, confusion, seizure, coma. Ask about sulfonylureas and insulin, missed
  meals, alcohol, renal failure.

investigations: >+
  - Capillary glucose **and** capillary ketones (beta-hydroxybutyrate).

  - **Venous blood gas:** pH, bicarbonate, K⁺, lactate. Calculate the anion gap:
  (Na⁺ + K⁺) − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻).

  - U&E (AKI is common), plasma osmolality, FBC, CRP, blood and urine cultures,
  urinalysis, CXR.

  - ECG (hypo- or hyperkalaemia, MI as the trigger), troponin, lipase or amylase
  if abdominal pain, pregnancy test.

  - HbA1c, lipids, urine ACR, foot check, retinal screening status once well.

  - New T1DM: islet autoantibodies and C-peptide if the type is unclear.
  Pancreatic diabetes: CT or MRI pancreas, faecal elastase.

treatment: >+
  ### DKA (JBDS 2021 principles, follow the local integrated care pathway)


  - **Fluids:** 0.9% NaCl 1 L over 1 h (500 mL bolus first if SBP <90, and think
  sepsis or another cause). Then 1 L over 2 h twice, 1 L over 4 h twice, 1 L
  over 6 h. Add K⁺ per the potassium rule below and adjust for heart failure,
  the elderly and the young.

  - **Fixed-rate IV insulin infusion (FRIII) 0.1 units/kg/h**, started at the
  same time as fluids. Continue the patient's long-acting (basal) insulin
  alongside it.

  - **Potassium:** K⁺ >5.5: none. 3.5–5.5: 40 mmol/L in each bag. <3.5: senior
  review and extra K⁺ (insulin will drop it further).

  - Add **10% glucose at 125 mL/h** when glucose <14 mmol/L and continue
  insulin.

  - Monitor hourly capillary glucose and ketones, VBG at 60 min, 2 h, then
  2-hourly. **Targets:** ketones fall ≥0.5 mmol/L/h, bicarbonate rise ≥3
  mmol/L/h, glucose fall ≥3 mmol/L/h. If not met, check the insulin pump,
  cannula and fluids, and consider raising the insulin rate.

  - **Resolved when** ketones <0.6 and pH >7.3. Then switch to subcutaneous
  insulin when eating and drinking (stop the infusion 30–60 min after the first
  SC dose).

  - Bicarbonate is not routinely given. Give LMWH prophylaxis. Treat the
  trigger. Diabetes specialist team review before discharge. Stop SGLT2
  inhibitors.

  - **Complications:** hypokalaemia, hypoglycaemia, cerebral oedema (mainly
  children and young people), ARDS, VTE, fluid overload.


  ### HHS


  - Slow and careful. IV 0.9% NaCl to restore volume, aiming for a glucose fall
  of ≤5 mmol/L/h and Na⁺ fall not too rapid (cerebral oedema and osmotic
  demyelination risk).

  - Start low-dose FRIII (0.05 units/kg/h) only if glucose is not falling with
  fluids, or if ketonaemia is present.

  - **LMWH for everyone** (high thrombosis risk). Daily foot check. Treat
  precipitants (infection, MI, stroke).


  ### Hypoglycaemia


  - Conscious and able to swallow: 15–20 g fast-acting carbohydrate (150–200 mL
  fruit juice, glucose tablets), recheck in 10–15 min and repeat, then a
  long-acting carbohydrate snack.

  - Unconscious or unable to swallow: **IV 10% glucose 150–200 mL over 15 min**
  (or 20% glucose 75–100 mL), or **IM glucagon 1 mg** if no IV access. Glucagon
  works poorly in alcohol excess, liver disease and starvation, and after
  sulfonylureas.

  - Sulfonylurea hypoglycaemia can recur for days. Observe and consider
  octreotide with specialist advice.


  ### Chronic and inpatient management


  - **T1DM:** basal-bolus insulin. **T2DM:** metformin first line, then add
  SGLT2 inhibitor (especially with CVD, HF or CKD), DPP-4 inhibitor, GLP-1
  agonist, sulfonylurea or pioglitazone. Cardiorenal-protective agents are
  prioritised.

  - **Never omit basal insulin in T1DM inpatients.** If nil by mouth or unwell,
  use a variable-rate IV insulin infusion (VRIII) with glucose and K⁺.

  - Hold metformin in AKI, sepsis and after IV contrast (lactic acidosis risk).
  Sick-day rules: stop SGLT2i, metformin, ACEi/ARB and diuretics when acutely
  unwell and dehydrated.

  - **Pancreatic diabetes:** low-dose insulin with caution, pancreatic enzyme
  replacement, and avoid alcohol.


  > ## Insulin

  >

  > **Insulin types and regimens**

  >

  > - **Rapid-acting analogues** (insulin aspart, lispro, glulisine) — onset ~15
  min, given with meals (“bolus”); **long-acting analogues** (glargine, detemir,
  degludec) — flat profile, once or twice daily (“basal”); **intermediate-acting
  (isophane/NPH)** — older, peaked profile, still used once/twice daily,
  especially in T2DM; **biphasic/mixed** (e.g. biphasic aspart) — fixed
  rapid:intermediate ratio, twice daily, simpler but less flexible.

  > - **Basal-bolus (multiple daily injection, MDI)** is **NICE’s regimen of
  choice for all adults with T1DM**: one or two daily basal injections plus a
  rapid-acting bolus with each meal, adjusted meal-by-meal.

  > - **Continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII/pump)**: rapid-acting
  insulin only, continuous basal rate plus patient-triggered boluses. **Hybrid
  closed-loop (“artificial pancreas”) systems** — a pump linked to a continuous
  glucose monitor via an algorithm that auto-adjusts basal delivery — are now
  recommended by NICE (TA943) as an option for adults with T1DM, with a 5-year
  national rollout; many services currently prioritise access by HbA1c ≥58
  mmol/mol or disabling hypoglycaemia despite optimised MDI/CSII + CGM, while
  availability broadens.

  > - **T2DM starting insulin**: usually NPH (isophane) added to existing oral
  agents is the traditional first step, though a long-acting analogue is often
  preferred if hypoglycaemia risk is a concern or someone else administers the
  injections; basal-bolus is reserved for inadequate control on basal insulin
  alone.

  >

  > **Dosing and titration**

  >

  > - Typical **total daily dose**: ~0.2–0.4 units/kg/day at diagnosis of T1DM
  (often lower during the “honeymoon” period), rising to **0.5–1.0
  units/kg/day** in established disease. Requirements **>1 unit/kg/day** suggest
  insulin resistance (obesity, infection, steroids, puberty) rather than a
  dosing error.

  > - A common starting split in basal-bolus regimens is roughly **50% basal /
  50% bolus** (bolus divided across meals), then individualised.

  > - Titrate by **10–20% increments**, no more than every 2–3 days, based on
  home glucose patterns: a high **fasting/pre-meal** reading reflects the
  **basal** dose; a high **post-prandial/pre-next-meal** reading reflects the
  preceding **bolus** dose.

  > - Structured education (**DAFNE** for T1DM) teaches carbohydrate-counting
  and an individualised **insulin:carbohydrate ratio** plus a **correction
  factor** (how much 1 unit lowers glucose), allowing flexible rather than fixed
  bolus dosing — NICE recommends offering this to everyone with T1DM.

  >

  > **How “adequate” control is judged clinically**

  >

  > - **HbA1c target**: ≤48 mmol/mol (6.5%) for T1DM where achievable without
  problematic hypoglycaemia; individualised and often less tight for T2DM or
  frailty.

  > - **Capillary/CGM glucose targets** (T1DM): fasting/pre-meal **4–7 mmol/L**,
  post-meal **5–9 mmol/L**.

  > - **Continuous/flash glucose monitoring** is now standard of care for T1DM
  and adds **“time in range”** (3.9–10 mmol/L, target >70%) as a metric that
  HbA1c alone can miss — particularly useful for spotting a hypoglycaemia
  pattern hidden behind an apparently good average.

  >

  > **Safety**

  >

  > - Insulin is a high-risk medication: always write “**units**” in full (never
  “U” — “10U” has been misread as “100”), and never omit an inpatient’s basal
  insulin even when nil by mouth — switch to a **variable-rate IV insulin
  infusion** instead.

  > - **Sick-day rules**: never stop insulin when unwell — requirements often
  *increase*; check ketones if glucose is persistently high.

protocols: >+
  - **JBDS DKA guideline** (adults) and the local DKA integrated care pathway.
  **JBDS HHS** and **hypoglycaemia** guidelines. **VRIII** pathway for surgical
  and NBM patients.

  - ABCDE assessment, NEWS2 and Sepsis Six if infection is the trigger.

  - Escalate: severe DKA, GCS <12, K⁺ outside 3.5–5.5 on presentation,
  pregnancy, HHS, or failure to respond (refer to critical care or the diabetes
  team).

  - Newly diagnosed T1DM or any DKA needs a diabetes specialist team referral
  before discharge.

  - DVLA: hypoglycaemia awareness and insulin treatment have driving
  implications.
