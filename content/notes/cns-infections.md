background: |+
  Infection of the meninges (meningitis), brain parenchyma (encephalitis, abscess) or the spaces around them (subdural empyema, spinal epidural abscess). Bacterial meningitis and HSV encephalitis are the **can't-miss, treat-before-confirmed** diagnoses. The sepsis factfile (Acute medicine) covers the first emergency hour; this card goes deeper.

  Meningitis

  - **Bacterial**: *Neisseria meningitidis* (young adults, teenagers; meningococcal septicaemia), *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (commonest overall, highest mortality and sequelae), *Haemophilus influenzae* (rare post-Hib vaccine), ***Listeria monocytogenes*** (older age, pregnancy, immunosuppression, alcohol dependence), and in neonates GBS and *E. coli*.
  - **Viral** (commonest, usually benign): enteroviruses, HSV-2 (including recurrent "Mollaret's"), VZV, mumps.
  - **TB meningitis**: subacute, basal meningitis with cranial nerve palsies and hydrocephalus. **Cryptococcal meningitis**: HIV with low CD4 count, subacute, raised ICP.

  Encephalitis and abscess

  - **Encephalitis**: inflammation of the brain itself. **HSV-1 is the commonest sporadic cause**; also VZV, enteroviruses, arboviruses; **autoimmune encephalitis** (for example anti-NMDA receptor, anti-LGI1) is the important non-infectious mimic.
  - **Brain abscess**: contiguous spread (ENT, dental, mastoid), haematogenous spread (endocarditis, right-to-left cardiac shunts, lung abscess), or after neurosurgery/trauma. Organisms: **streptococci (especially *S. anginosus* group)**, anaerobes, *S. aureus* (post-surgical/trauma), Enterobacterales. In HIV: toxoplasma, TB, *Nocardia*.

  > **Microbiology and pathology**
  >
  > - ***N. meningitidis***: Gram-negative diplococcus; polysaccharide capsule (serogroups B, C, W, Y relevant in the UK); colonises the nasopharynx via type IV pili. Its **lipo-oligosaccharide (endotoxin)** activates TLR4, producing the cytokine storm of meningococcal septicaemia (purpura, DIC, shock). **Terminal complement deficiency (C5–C9), asplenia and properdin deficiency** predispose to recurrent invasive Neisseria disease (the membrane attack complex is the key defence against it).
  > - **Pneumococcus**: encapsulated Gram-positive diplococcus; the capsule resists phagocytosis, and pneumolysin damages host cells. It enters the CSF across the choroid plexus or the blood–brain barrier.
  > - **Why the CSF is vulnerable**: it contains little complement and few immunoglobulins, so opsonisation is poor and bacteria multiply almost unchecked. Bacterial cell-wall fragments (TLR2) and endotoxin then trigger IL-1β and TNF-α release from microglia and astrocytes → neutrophil influx, **increased blood–brain barrier permeability (vasogenic oedema)**, impaired CSF resorption, vasculitis and thrombosis (infarcts). Rapid bacterial lysis by antibiotics transiently increases this inflammation, which is the rationale for **dexamethasone** (best evidence in pneumococcal disease; reduces hearing loss and mortality).
  > - **HSV encephalitis**: HSV-1 reactivates from the trigeminal ganglion and reaches the **temporal and orbitofrontal lobes**; pathology is haemorrhagic, necrotising, with intranuclear inclusions.
diagnosis: |+
  - **Meningitis**: fever, headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, vomiting, altered mental state. **The classic triad is present in a minority**, so don't wait for it. **Non-blanching rash** suggests meningococcal disease (check with the glass test, but its absence doesn't exclude it). Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs are insensitive.
  - **Septicaemia features** (limb pain, cold peripheries, mottled skin, abnormal capillary refill, shock) can precede meningism.
  - **Encephalitis**: fever with **confusion, behavioural/personality change, seizures, focal deficit or reduced consciousness** — look at the brain, not the meninges. Temporal lobe involvement may cause aphasia, memory loss or odd olfactory/gustatory phenomena.
  - **Brain abscess**: headache (commonest), focal deficit, seizures, raised ICP; **fever is often absent**, so a "tumour-like" presentation is common.
  - **TB/cryptococcal**: days to weeks of headache, vomiting, confusion, cranial nerve palsies (especially VI), and visual loss from raised ICP.
investigations: |+
  - **Blood cultures** (before antibiotics where it doesn't delay them), **blood PCR for *N. meningitidis* and *S. pneumoniae***, FBC, CRP, U&E, glucose, lactate, clotting, and a throat swab for meningococcal culture/PCR.
  - **Lumbar puncture** (if no contraindication) for opening pressure, cell count, protein, paired glucose, Gram stain, culture and PCR. **Don't delay antibiotics for LP or imaging.** CT before LP is **not routine**: reserve it for **focal neurology, new seizures, abnormal pupils, markedly reduced or falling GCS**, or other signs of raised ICP (see NICE NG240 for the full list; the thresholds were tightened compared with older guidance). Also avoid LP with shock, extensive purpura, coagulopathy, or local infection at the site.
  - **Encephalitis**: CSF HSV/VZV/enterovirus PCR (can be negative in the first 72 hours — repeat if suspicion persists), **MRI** (temporal lobe T2/FLAIR change), **EEG** (periodic lateralised discharges), and autoimmune encephalitis antibodies if PCR-negative.
  - **Abscess**: contrast CT/MRI (ring-enhancing lesion with restricted diffusion on DWI). **Don't perform LP** (herniation risk). Look for the source (echocardiogram, dental/ENT assessment, chest imaging).
  - **TB/cryptococcal meningitis**: Ziehl–Neelsen and TB culture/NAAT; **cryptococcal antigen (CSF and serum)** and India ink, with measurement of opening pressure.

  | CSF | Bacterial | Viral | TB | Cryptococcal |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Appearance | Turbid | Clear | Clear (fibrin web) | Clear |
  | Cells | Neutrophils (hundreds–thousands/µL) | Lymphocytes (tens–hundreds) | Lymphocytes (neutrophils early) | Lymphocytes |
  | Protein | High (often >1 g/L) | Normal or mildly raised | Very high | Raised |
  | Glucose (CSF:plasma) | Low (<0.4) | Normal | Low | Low or normal |
  | Key extra | Gram stain, culture, PCR | PCR | ZN, NAAT, culture | CrAg, India ink, high opening pressure |
treatment: |+
  - **Suspected bacterial meningitis (NICE NG240, UK Joint Specialist Societies)**: **immediate IV ceftriaxone 2 g every 12 hours**; add **IV amoxicillin 2 g 4-hourly** where *Listeria* is a risk (older age, immunosuppression, pregnancy, alcohol dependence), per local policy. In the community, give immediate parenteral antibiotics before transfer.
  - **Dexamethasone 10 mg IV four times daily for 4 days**, started with or just before the first antibiotic dose, in suspected or confirmed bacterial meningitis. Don't give it if more than 12 hours after antibiotics started, or in septic shock/meningococcal septicaemia without meningitis, immunosuppression or post-neurosurgical infection; stop if another diagnosis is confirmed.
  - **Duration (tailor to organism)**: meningococcal about 5–7 days; pneumococcal about 10–14 days; *Listeria* about 21 days.
  - **Notify and give chemoprophylaxis to close contacts** (single-dose ciprofloxacin per UKHSA); consider vaccination (MenACWY, MenB) for contacts of confirmed cases.
  - **HSV and other suspected encephalitis**: **IV aciclovir 10 mg/kg every 8 hours** started **empirically without waiting** for results, continued for 14–21 days if confirmed (maintain hydration to avoid crystal nephropathy; adjust for renal function). Immunotherapy (steroids, IVIG, plasma exchange) for autoimmune encephalitis.
  - **Brain abscess**: **neurosurgical aspiration/drainage** (diagnostic and therapeutic) for lesions >2.5 cm or with mass effect; **IV ceftriaxone plus metronidazole (±vancomycin if *S. aureus* risk)** for typically 6–8 weeks; treat the source. **Subdural empyema** is a neurosurgical emergency.
  - **TB meningitis**: antituberculous therapy for **12 months** with **adjunctive dexamethasone or prednisolone** (NICE NG33). **Cryptococcal meningitis**: liposomal amphotericin B plus flucytosine, then fluconazole, with **therapeutic lumbar punctures** for raised ICP.
protocols: |+
  > Red flag: suspected meningococcal disease (non-blanching rash with fever, or any septic features) is a **time-critical emergency** — immediate antibiotics, Sepsis Six, early critical care review. Do not wait for imaging, LP or senior review to give the first dose.

  - **Signs of raised ICP or herniation** (falling GCS, relative bradycardia with hypertension, unequal pupils, abnormal posturing, papilloedema): no LP; urgent CT, critical care and neurosurgical input.
  - **Complications to monitor**: seizures, hyponatraemia (SIADH), hydrocephalus, cerebral infarction and subdural effusion, and **sensorineural hearing loss** — arrange a hearing assessment before or soon after discharge.
  - Meningitis and meningococcal disease are **notifiable** — inform the local health protection team on clinical suspicion.
  - See the Sepsis and infection factfile (Acute medicine) for the Sepsis Six and the first-hour algorithm. *(That card's CT-before-LP wording follows older guidance; NICE NG240 is the current reference.)*
