background: |+
  A state of **inadequate tissue perfusion caused primarily by cardiac pump failure**, despite an adequate (or corrected) circulating volume — the cardiac equivalent of "the pump itself has failed," as opposed to hypovolaemic, distributive (septic/anaphylactic) or obstructive shock, which all have an adequately functioning heart but a different primary problem.

  - **Commonest cause by far: acute MI** (usually a large anterior infarct, or any infarct complicated by a mechanical problem), accounting for the large majority of cases. Also: **acute severe valve dysfunction** (papillary muscle rupture causing severe mitral regurgitation, acute aortic regurgitation from dissection or endocarditis), **myocarditis** (can cause fulminant, severe shock in otherwise young, previously well patients), **decompensated end-stage heart failure of any cause**, **tachy- or brady-arrhythmia** severe enough to drop cardiac output, **cardiac tamponade** (sometimes classified separately as obstructive shock, but overlaps clinically), and **massive PE** (similarly an overlap with obstructive shock, via acute right heart failure).
  - **Pathophysiology**: falling cardiac output triggers compensatory vasoconstriction and fluid retention (neurohormonal activation), which initially supports blood pressure but increases the heart's workload further — a vicious cycle that, left untreated, leads to worsening pump failure, end-organ hypoperfusion, and death.
diagnosis: |+
  - **Hypotension** (classically sustained SBP <90 mmHg, or needing vasopressors/inotropes to maintain it) **with signs of end-organ hypoperfusion**: cold, clammy, mottled peripheries, prolonged capillary refill, oliguria, and confusion/reduced consciousness — the hallmark "cold and wet" (or sometimes "cold and dry" if intravascularly depleted too) haemodynamic profile, as opposed to the "warm and wet" profile of typical decompensated heart failure with preserved perfusion (see the Heart failure factfile, Acute medicine).
  - Examine specifically for a **clue to the underlying cause**: a new murmur (papillary muscle rupture/acute MR, or acute AR), signs of a large MI (chest pain history, ECG changes), raised JVP with muffled heart sounds (tamponade), an irregular or very fast/slow rhythm (arrhythmic cause), or signs of a massive PE (sudden collapse, hypoxia, raised JVP).
  - This is a clinical diagnosis of a syndrome, not a single disease — the goal of assessment is as much to find the **treatable underlying cause** as to recognise the shock state itself.
investigations: |+
  - **ECG** (for ischaemia, arrhythmia), **troponin**, **lactate and venous/arterial blood gas** (severity, trend with treatment), **FBC, U&E, LFTs, coagulation** (end-organ impact and baseline before intervention).
  - **Urgent bedside echocardiography** is the single most useful investigation — assesses LV and RV function, identifies a mechanical complication (acute severe MR, VSD, tamponade), and estimates filling status, directly shaping the treatment plan.
  - **Invasive haemodynamic monitoring** (arterial line as a minimum; a pulmonary artery catheter in selected, usually ICU-level cases) supports ongoing management once the patient is in a monitored critical care setting.
  - **Coronary angiography** urgently if an acute coronary cause is suspected or not yet excluded, since timely revascularisation is the single most effective treatment where ACS is the cause (see Treatment).
treatment: |+
  - **Critical care-level care from the outset** — this is a diagnosis that needs senior, often multidisciplinary (cardiology, intensive care, sometimes cardiothoracic surgery) input early, not escalation only once simple measures fail.
  - **Treat the underlying cause specifically wherever identifiable** — this is the single most important determinant of outcome, more than any generic shock treatment: **urgent revascularisation (primary PCI, or CABG if appropriate)** for an ACS cause; **urgent valve surgery** for a mechanical complication (papillary muscle rupture, acute severe AR); **pericardiocentesis** for tamponade (see that card); **rhythm correction** (cardioversion, pacing, or antiarrhythmics as appropriate) for an arrhythmic cause; **thrombolysis or thrombectomy** for massive PE (see the VTE card).
  - **Inotropes and vasopressors**: used to support perfusion while the underlying cause is addressed — **noradrenaline** is generally preferred as the first-line vasopressor to support blood pressure; an **inotrope** (e.g. dobutamine) is added to improve contractility/cardiac output where perfusion remains inadequate despite adequate pressure. These support the circulation but do not themselves fix the underlying problem, and higher doses carry increasing arrhythmia risk and myocardial oxygen demand.
  - **Careful fluid management**: unlike other shock types, **indiscriminate fluid boluses can worsen cardiogenic shock** by increasing pulmonary and systemic congestion in an already failing heart — small, cautious fluid challenges are used only if there is a clear element of volume depletion, guided by echo/clinical response rather than given reflexively.
  - **Mechanical circulatory support** (intra-aortic balloon pump, percutaneous ventricular assist devices such as Impella, or veno-arterial ECMO) is considered in specialist centres for patients not responding to the above, either as a bridge to recovery, to definitive surgery, or to transplantation — decided by the specialist team managing the case.
protocols: |+
  > Red flag: a new murmur, sudden deterioration, or new hypoxia in a patient with cardiogenic shock following acute MI should prompt urgent echocardiography for a mechanical complication (papillary muscle rupture, VSD, free wall rupture/tamponade) — these need urgent surgery and are easily missed if shock is simply treated as "pump failure" without looking for a treatable structural cause.
  - Cardiogenic shock is one of the clinical pictures where **early, open discussion of ceiling of care** is appropriate given the significant mortality, particularly in older or frailer patients or where mechanical circulatory support/transplantation are not realistic options — this should run alongside, not instead of, active treatment where appropriate.
  - See the Heart failure, ACS, and Cardiac tamponade factfiles/cards for the detailed emergency management of each underlying cause.
