background: >+
  Thyroid cancer is the **commonest endocrine malignancy**, but overall has a good prognosis for most patients, since the majority are differentiated and slow-growing. Four main types, from the follicular epithelial cells (papillary, follicular) or the parafollicular C-cells (medullary), plus the rare anaplastic type.

  - **Papillary (~80–85%, the most common)**: often young patients, excellent prognosis, spreads mainly via **lymphatics** to cervical nodes (even with nodal spread, prognosis remains favourable). Multifocality is common.
  - **Follicular (~10–15%)**: spreads **haematogenously** (bone, lung) rather than via lymphatics; cannot be distinguished from a follicular adenoma on FNA cytology alone — the diagnosis needs histology showing capsular or vascular invasion (see the Thyroid nodules card).
  - Papillary and follicular together are "**differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC)**" — they retain enough normal thyroid function to take up iodine and produce thyroglobulin, both exploited for treatment and monitoring.
  - **Medullary (~3–5%)**: arises from calcitonin-producing **parafollicular C-cells**, not the follicular epithelium — behaves and is managed differently from DTC. **~25% are hereditary** (RET proto-oncogene mutation), as part of **MEN2A**, **MEN2B**, or familial medullary thyroid cancer — so genetic testing is offered to every patient with confirmed medullary cancer.
  - **Anaplastic (<2%, rare)**: undifferentiated, aggressive, almost exclusively in older patients, with a poor prognosis and often rapid local invasion (airway compromise is a real risk).
diagnosis: >+
  - Usually a **thyroid nodule** (see that card for the triage pathway) or incidental neck node; thyroid cancer itself does not usually cause thyroid hormone excess or deficiency. Features raising suspicion: rapid growth, a hard/fixed mass, hoarse voice, dysphagia, and cervical lymphadenopathy.
  - **Medullary thyroid cancer** can occasionally present with symptoms from calcitonin or co-secreted peptides (flushing, diarrhoea) rather than purely the local mass — and may coexist with the other MEN2 features (**phaeochromocytoma** — see the Adrenal disorders card — and primary hyperparathyroidism in MEN2A).
  - **Anaplastic thyroid cancer** presents differently from the rest — a rapidly enlarging, hard, fixed neck mass over weeks, often with pain, stridor and dysphagia from local invasion, in an older patient — this clinical picture alone should prompt urgent same-day assessment rather than routine referral.
investigations: >+
  - Diagnosis follows the **thyroid nodule pathway** (ultrasound U classification, then FNA/Thy classification) — see that card. A **Thy5 (malignant)** or **Thy3a/3f (atypia/follicular neoplasm)** result, confirmed on definitive histology after surgery, establishes the diagnosis and subtype.
  - **Suspected medullary thyroid cancer**: **serum calcitonin and CEA** (both tumour markers, useful for diagnosis, prognosis and follow-up), plus **calcium/PTH** and **plasma free metanephrines** (to exclude coexisting hyperparathyroidism and phaeochromocytoma before any surgery, since an unrecognised phaeochromocytoma is dangerous during anaesthesia). **RET proto-oncogene testing** is performed in every confirmed case; first-degree relatives of a RET-positive patient are then offered testing themselves.
  - Staging (differentiated thyroid cancer): neck ultrasound for nodal assessment; cross-sectional imaging (CT/MRI) or further staging is reserved for larger, more aggressive, or node/metastasis-suspicious disease, rather than used routinely for small low-risk tumours.
treatment: >+
  Differentiated thyroid cancer (papillary and follicular)

  - **Total (or near-total) thyroidectomy** is standard for most clinically significant disease (follicular cancers >4 cm always need total thyroidectomy); very small, low-risk, unifocal papillary microcarcinomas are sometimes managed with a lesser operation or even active surveillance, per specialist MDT discussion. **Subtotal thyroidectomy is not used** for cancer.
  - **Radioactive iodine (I-131) ablation** of residual thyroid tissue after surgery for most tumours **>1 cm**, exploiting the fact that differentiated thyroid tissue (and most differentiated cancer) takes up iodine; a post-ablation scan is done a few days later to confirm uptake and check for unsuspected metastatic disease.
  - **TSH-suppressive levothyroxine** (dosed higher than simple replacement) is then continued long-term, since TSH is a growth stimulus for residual differentiated thyroid tissue/cancer — the degree of suppression is tailored to recurrence risk.
  - **Follow-up**: serum **thyroglobulin** (undetectable after complete treatment) is the key tumour marker for recurrence in DTC — a **rising thyroglobulin strongly suggests recurrent or persistent disease** and triggers further imaging. Anti-thyroglobulin antibodies are checked alongside it, since their presence can make the thyroglobulin result unreliable.

  Medullary thyroid cancer

  - **Total thyroidectomy with central compartment neck dissection** for confirmed disease >5 mm (radioiodine and TSH suppression are not useful here, since C-cells are not iodine-avid or TSH-responsive). **Prophylactic thyroidectomy** is offered to RET-positive family members, with the timing (sometimes in early childhood for the highest-risk mutations) guided by the specific RET codon involved.
  - Follow-up with serial **calcitonin and CEA**; a short doubling time for either marker indicates more aggressive, progressive disease.
  - **Correct any coexisting phaeochromocytoma (with full pre-operative α- then β-blockade) before thyroid surgery in MEN2** — operating on an unrecognised phaeochromocytoma risks a catecholamine crisis under anaesthesia.

  Anaplastic thyroid cancer

  - Prognosis is poor; management is individualised and often combines **surgery (where feasible), radiotherapy and chemotherapy**, with an early focus on **airway protection** (a tracheostomy may be needed) given the risk of airway obstruction from local invasion. Palliative care input is frequently needed early, alongside oncological treatment, given the aggressive course.
protocols: >+
  - **Post-thyroidectomy hypocalcaemia**: check calcium in every patient after total thyroidectomy (parathyroid glands are at risk during surgery) — see the Hypoparathyroidism card for assessment and management.
  - **Suspected airway compromise from anaplastic (or any rapidly enlarging/locally advanced) thyroid cancer**: treat as an airway emergency — urgent ENT/anaesthetic assessment, since standard oncological staging can wait but airway loss cannot.
  - A **phaeochromocytoma must be excluded and, if present, fully treated before any thyroid or parathyroid surgery in a patient with MEN2** — see the Adrenal disorders card for the pre-operative α-blockade protocol.
