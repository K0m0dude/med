background: >+
  The adrenal cortex (zona glomerulosa → fasciculata → reticularis) makes aldosterone, cortisol and androgens; the medulla makes catecholamines. Five related problems come up repeatedly.

  Adrenal incidentaloma

  - An adrenal mass found on imaging done for another reason. Prevalence **2–3%** of CT scans, rising with age. **85%** are non-functioning adenomas; the rest secrete hormone (subclinical Cushing's, Conn's, phaeochromocytoma) or, rarely, are malignant.
  - Two separate questions for every incidentaloma: **is it functioning?** and **is it malignant?** Size alone does not tell you whether it is functioning.

  Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's syndrome)

  - Autonomous aldosterone production (adenoma, or bilateral adrenal hyperplasia) independent of the renin-angiotensin system. The **commonest identifiable cause of secondary hypertension** (~5–10% of all hypertension), often missed because it mimics essential hypertension.
  - Excess aldosterone → renal Na⁺/water retention (hypertension) and K⁺/H⁺ loss (hypokalaemia, metabolic alkalosis) — though **normokalaemia is common** and does not exclude the diagnosis.

  Phaeochromocytoma (and paraganglioma)

  - A catecholamine-secreting tumour of adrenal medullary chromaffin cells (paraganglioma = the same tumour at an extra-adrenal site, e.g. organ of Zuckerkandl). Rare, but important because it is a surgically curable cause of hypertension that is dangerous if missed before surgery or anaesthesia.
  - **"Rule of 10s"** (approximate, now known to understate the hereditary rate): ~10% bilateral, ~10% extra-adrenal, ~10–20% malignant, ~10% in children, **~30–40% are hereditary** (MEN2A/2B, von Hippel–Lindau, neurofibromatosis type 1, or a succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) paraganglioma syndrome) — so genetic counselling is routinely offered.

  Cushing's syndrome and Cushing's disease

  - **Cushing's syndrome** = any cause of chronic glucocorticoid excess. **Cushing's disease** = the specific case where the cause is an ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma — the commonest endogenous cause.
  - **Exogenous (iatrogenic) steroid use is by far the commonest cause overall** — always ask about steroid inhalers, creams and joint injections, not just tablets.
  - Endogenous causes split into **ACTH-dependent** (80–85%: pituitary = Cushing's disease, or ectopic ACTH from a tumour, classically small-cell lung cancer or a carcinoid) and **ACTH-independent** (15–20%: adrenal adenoma or carcinoma, or exogenous steroid which suppresses ACTH).

  Addison's disease (primary adrenal insufficiency)

  - Destruction of the adrenal cortex → deficiency of **both cortisol and aldosterone** (± adrenal androgens). **Autoimmune adrenalitis is the commonest UK cause**; globally, TB predominates. Other causes: adrenal metastases, haemorrhage (Waterhouse–Friderichsen syndrome in meningococcal sepsis, or anticoagulation), adrenoleukodystrophy, antiphospholipid syndrome.
  - **Secondary** adrenal insufficiency (pituitary ACTH deficiency) and **tertiary** (hypothalamic CRH deficiency, most often from abrupt withdrawal of long-term steroids) spare aldosterone, since that is mostly renin-driven, not ACTH-driven.
  - Often part of **autoimmune polyglandular syndrome** — screen for/ask about type 1 diabetes, autoimmune thyroid disease, pernicious anaemia, vitiligo, premature ovarian failure, hypoparathyroidism.
diagnosis: >+
  Primary hyperaldosteronism

  - Suspect in: hypertension resistant to 3 drugs, hypertension with spontaneous or diuretic-induced hypokalaemia, hypertension with an adrenal incidentaloma, or hypertension at a young age / with a family history.
  - Signs are otherwise non-specific (those of hypertension); muscle weakness or cramps if significantly hypokalaemic.

  Phaeochromocytoma

  - Classic triad: **episodic headache, sweating, palpitations** — often with pallor and anxiety, lasting under an hour, precipitated by exertion, abdominal palpation, surgery or certain drugs (metoclopramide, some anaesthetic agents). **~10% are normotensive between attacks**; others have sustained hypertension.
  - A rare but important cause of hypertensive crisis provoked by starting a **β-blocker before adequate α-blockade** — unopposed α-mediated vasoconstriction.

  Cushing's syndrome

  - Central obesity, moon face, plethora, buffalo hump, **proximal myopathy**, thin skin with easy bruising, **purple striae** (wide, >1 cm, suggest Cushing's rather than simple obesity), hirsutism/acne, hypertension, impaired glucose tolerance, osteoporosis, depression/psychosis, poor wound healing, menstrual disturbance.
  - Rapid onset of these features with marked weight loss, hyperpigmentation and hypokalaemic alkalosis should raise suspicion of **ectopic ACTH** (small-cell lung cancer, carcinoid) rather than classical Cushing's disease.

  Addison's disease

  > Fatigue, anorexia and weight loss, postural dizziness, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, salt craving, and **hyperpigmentation** (palmar creases, buccal mucosa, scars — from excess ACTH/POMC cross-reacting with melanocortin receptors) point to a **primary** cause; hyperpigmentation is absent in secondary/tertiary insufficiency since ACTH is low, not high.

  - Chronic presentation is often non-specific and insidious — a common source of missed or delayed diagnosis. An **adrenal (Addisonian) crisis** can be the first presentation, precipitated by infection, surgery, trauma or missed medication in someone already borderline.
investigations: >+
  Adrenal incidentaloma

  - **Dedicated adrenal CT**: homogeneous, **Hounsfield units (HU) ≤10** on unenhanced CT = lipid-rich, almost certainly a benign adenoma, regardless of size. Higher HU, heterogeneity, or **>4 cm** raises malignancy risk and should prompt MDT discussion ± washout CT/MRI ± biopsy.
  - **Every patient** needs biochemical screening regardless of size: plasma or urinary metanephrines (phaeochromocytoma), a **1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test** (cortisol should suppress to ≤50 nmol/L), and — if hypertensive or hypokalaemic — an aldosterone:renin ratio. Measure adrenal androgens in women if virilisation is suspected.

  Primary hyperaldosteronism

  - **Screen**: plasma aldosterone:renin ratio (ARR) — high aldosterone with suppressed renin. Stop interfering drugs first where possible (spironolactone, eplerenone; high-dose ACEi/ARB/β-blocker can also distort it) — use an α-blocker or verapamil for BP control during work-up instead.
  - **Confirm**: failure of aldosterone to suppress on a **saline infusion test** (or fludrocortisone suppression test).
  - **Localise**: adrenal CT, then **adrenal vein sampling** to distinguish unilateral (adenoma — surgical) from bilateral (hyperplasia — medical) disease, since CT alone is not reliable enough to decide this.

  Phaeochromocytoma

  - **Plasma free metanephrines** (most sensitive, ~97%) or 24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines and catecholamines. Avoid during an acute stressor if possible (false positives). CT or MRI to localise; **MIBG scintigraphy** if extra-adrenal/malignant/metastatic disease is suspected. Genetic testing offered to all, given the high hereditary rate.

  Cushing's syndrome

  - **Two** separate positive screening tests needed from: **1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test** (failure to suppress <50 nmol/L), **24-hour urinary free cortisol** (≥2 collections), **late-night salivary cortisol** (≥2 samples). The overnight DST is preferred when an adrenal incidentaloma was the trigger.
  - Once confirmed, measure **plasma ACTH**: suppressed (<2.2 pmol/L) = **ACTH-independent** → adrenal CT. Normal/high (>4.4 pmol/L) = **ACTH-dependent** → pituitary MRI ± a **high-dose dexamethasone suppression test** or CRH test (pituitary source usually suppresses/responds; ectopic usually does not) ± **inferior petrosal sinus sampling (IPSS)** if the MRI is equivocal or normal, to confirm a pituitary source and lateralise it before surgery.

  Addison's disease

  - **9 am serum cortisol**: <100 nmol/L makes adrenal insufficiency likely; >450–500 nmol/L makes it very unlikely; anywhere in between needs a **short Synacthen test** (250 micrograms IM/IV tetracosactide; cortisol <450–500 nmol/L at 30 min = adrenal insufficiency — confirms the diagnosis regardless of time of day). U&E often shows **hyponatraemia and hyperkalaemia** (aldosterone loss); hypoglycaemia, mild hypercalcaemia and eosinophilia can occur.
  - **21-hydroxylase adrenal autoantibodies** support an autoimmune cause; adrenal CT if autoantibody-negative (looking for TB, haemorrhage, metastases). Measuring **ACTH** distinguishes primary (high ACTH) from secondary/tertiary (low/normal ACTH) once a low cortisol is confirmed.
treatment: >+
  Incidentaloma

  - Non-functioning, <4 cm, benign imaging features: no surgery — repeat imaging and hormonal work-up at 6–12 months, then stop if stable. Functioning tumours, or imaging suspicious for malignancy (generally **>4 cm**, irregular, high HU, poor contrast washout), go to **laparoscopic adrenalectomy**.

  Primary hyperaldosteronism

  - **Unilateral** disease (adenoma): laparoscopic adrenalectomy — often cures or markedly improves the hypertension and hypokalaemia.
  - **Bilateral** disease (hyperplasia), or surgery unsuitable/declined: **mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist** (spironolactone first-line; eplerenone if spironolactone's anti-androgenic side effects — gynaecomastia, impotence — are not tolerated).

  Phaeochromocytoma

  - **Surgery is curative but can precipitate a catecholamine crisis if not properly prepared for.** **α-blockade first** (phenoxybenzamine, titrated over at least **1–3 weeks**, or a selective α1-blocker such as doxazosin), with liberal salt/fluid intake to re-expand the contracted plasma volume; **β-blockade added only once α-blockade is established** (never the other way round — unopposed α-stimulation risks a hypertensive crisis). Then laparoscopic (or open, if large/invasive) adrenalectomy. Lifelong annual biochemical surveillance given recurrence/metachronous tumour risk, especially in hereditary disease.

  Cushing's syndrome

  - **Cushing's disease**: trans-sphenoidal pituitary surgery first-line. **Adrenal adenoma/carcinoma**: adrenalectomy. **Ectopic ACTH**: treat the underlying tumour where possible. Medical therapy (metyrapone, ketoconazole) controls cortisol before surgery or when surgery is not curative/possible. **Exogenous steroid-induced**: wean the causative steroid as far as clinically safe rather than treat as endogenous disease.
  - Post-operative glucocorticoid replacement is needed after successful pituitary/adrenal surgery, since the remaining HPA axis is suppressed — wean slowly under specialist guidance, watching for recovery.

  Addison's disease

  - **Glucocorticoid + mineralocorticoid replacement** in primary disease: **hydrocortisone 15–25 mg/day in 2–3 divided doses** (larger dose on waking, mimicking the normal diurnal rhythm) **plus fludrocortisone 50–200 micrograms/day**. Secondary/tertiary disease needs glucocorticoid only (aldosterone is intact).
  - **Sick-day rules**: double the hydrocortisone dose during intercurrent illness/fever; if vomiting or unable to take oral medication, use the **emergency IM hydrocortisone kit** and seek urgent medical help.
  - Every patient needs a **steroid emergency card**, medical alert jewellery, and an emergency hydrocortisone injection kit with training for the patient (and family) on how to use it.
protocols: >+
  Addisonian (adrenal) crisis

  > Think of this in anyone unwell with unexplained hypotension, hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia or hypoglycaemia, especially with a history of steroid dependence or an abrupt steroid withdrawal.

  - **Do not wait for confirmatory tests.** Take a cortisol (and ACTH) sample, then immediately give **IV hydrocortisone 100 mg bolus**, followed by **100 mg every 6 hours** (or a continuous infusion), plus **IV 0.9% saline** (typically 1 L over 30–60 minutes, then reassess) and correct hypoglycaemia. Fludrocortisone is not needed acutely — high-dose hydrocortisone has enough mineralocorticoid activity.
  - Identify and treat the precipitant (commonly infection). Transition to oral hydrocortisone and fludrocortisone as the patient stabilises, with input from endocrinology.
  - **Any patient on long-term steroids (for any reason) needs their dose increased for intercurrent illness or surgery** — failure to do this is a common preventable cause of crisis on the ward.

  Phaeochromocytoma/catecholamine crisis

  - Severe hypertension, tachyarrhythmia or pulmonary oedema from unrecognised phaeochromocytoma (or from β-blockade given before α-blockade): urgent senior/critical care input, IV α-blockade (phentolamine) or short-acting agents (e.g. nicardipine), and avoid unopposed β-blockade.

  Cushing's-related

  - Severe hypercortisolism predisposes to infection (often atypical/masked by the lack of a normal inflammatory response), venous thromboembolism, hyperglycaemia and hypertension — have a low threshold for sepsis work-up, VTE prophylaxis and glucose monitoring in these patients during any acute admission.
