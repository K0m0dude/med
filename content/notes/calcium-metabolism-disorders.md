background: >+
  Calcium homeostasis depends on **parathyroid hormone (PTH)** (raises calcium — bone resorption, renal reabsorption, and activation of vitamin D), **vitamin D** (raises calcium — gut absorption, works with PTH on bone), and **calcitonin** (minor role in humans). Roughly 99% of body calcium is in bone; always use the **albumin-corrected** serum calcium, since a large fraction circulates bound to albumin.

  Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT)

  - **Autonomous PTH over-secretion**, usually from a single parathyroid adenoma (~80–85%), less often multigland hyperplasia (consider **MEN1** or **MEN2A** if young or multigland) or, rarely, carcinoma. Causes **hypercalcaemia with an inappropriately normal or raised PTH**.
  - Long-term effects: renal stones, bone disease (osteoporosis, classically subperiosteal resorption and "brown tumours" in severe disease), and an association with cardiovascular disease.

  Secondary and tertiary hyperparathyroidism

  - **Secondary**: an appropriate PTH rise in response to chronic hypocalcaemia — classically **CKD** (reduced active vitamin D synthesis and phosphate retention) or vitamin D deficiency/malabsorption. Calcium is low or normal, PTH is high.
  - **Tertiary**: after years of secondary hyperparathyroidism (usually long-standing CKD/dialysis), the parathyroid glands become autonomous and **hypercalcaemia** develops despite the original driving stimulus.

  Hypocalcaemia and hypoparathyroidism

  - See the dedicated **Hypoparathyroidism** card for PTH-deficiency causes. Other causes of hypocalcaemia: vitamin D deficiency, CKD, acute pancreatitis, rhabdomyolysis (phosphate binds calcium), massive transfusion (citrate chelation), hypomagnesaemia (impairs PTH release and action — always check and correct magnesium first, or calcium replacement will not work).

  Vitamin D deficiency and osteomalacia/rickets

  - Deficiency is common (reduced sunlight exposure, darker skin, malabsorption, obesity, CKD, anticonvulsant use). Causes **defective bone mineralisation**: in adults this is **osteomalacia** (bone pain, proximal myopathy, fractures); in children, before the growth plates fuse, it is **rickets** (growth delay, bony deformity).

  Paget's disease of bone

  - Localised disorder of **excessive, disorganised bone turnover** (osteoclast overactivity followed by disorganised osteoblastic new bone) — typically affects the pelvis, femur, skull and spine, in older adults. Calcium is usually normal; **alkaline phosphatase is markedly raised** (a marker of bone turnover, not calcium metabolism).
diagnosis: >+
  Hypercalcaemia (general)

  - "Bones, stones, groans and psychiatric moans": bone pain, renal stones/polyuria/polydipsia (nephrogenic DI from hypercalcaemia itself), abdominal pain/constipation/nausea, and confusion/depression/psychosis. ECG: **shortened QT interval**. Severe or rapid-onset hypercalcaemia can cause marked dehydration and reduced consciousness.
  - **Malignancy and primary hyperparathyroidism together account for most cases.** Malignancy-associated hypercalcaemia tends to be higher and more symptomatic/faster-onset (PTHrP from solid tumours, local osteolysis from bone metastases or myeloma, or rarely ectopic vitamin D from lymphoma) — see the Cancer and oncology emergencies factfile (Acute medicine) for management of this scenario.

  Primary hyperparathyroidism

  - Often **asymptomatic**, found on a routine calcium check. When symptomatic: the hypercalcaemia features above, plus renal stones and reduced bone density/fragility fracture specifically.
  - **Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia (FHH)** is an important differential — a benign, inherited calcium-sensing receptor abnormality causing mild lifelong hypercalcaemia with a normal/mildly raised PTH, but **low urinary calcium excretion** (unlike PHPT) and **no indication for parathyroid surgery**.

  Hypocalcaemia

  - Perioral tingling, paraesthesiae in the hands/feet, muscle cramps, and **tetany**. **Chvostek's sign** (tapping the facial nerve causes facial muscle twitching) and **Trousseau's sign** (inflating a BP cuff above systolic for a few minutes causes carpal spasm) can reveal latent tetany in someone who is not yet overtly symptomatic. Severe: seizures, laryngospasm, prolonged QT and arrhythmia.

  Vitamin D deficiency / osteomalacia

  - Diffuse bone and muscle pain, proximal muscle weakness ("waddling" gait), and fragility fractures; often subtle and easily missed or attributed to ageing.

  Paget's disease of bone

  - Often asymptomatic (incidental raised ALP or X-ray finding). When symptomatic: bone pain, deformity (bowed tibia, skull enlargement), warmth over the affected bone, pathological fracture, and — if the skull or spine is involved — nerve compression (deafness is a recognised complication of skull involvement).
investigations: >+
  - **Albumin-adjusted serum calcium** on at least two occasions to confirm persistent hypercalcaemia before extensive work-up. **PTH** is the key next test: raised/inappropriately normal with high calcium → primary hyperparathyroidism (or FHH — check a 24-hour **urinary calcium:creatinine clearance ratio**, low in FHH); suppressed PTH with high calcium → non-PTH-driven cause (malignancy, vitamin D excess, sarcoidosis, thyrotoxicosis) — investigate accordingly.
  - For confirmed PHPT: **vitamin D** (correct any deficiency — but don't delay referral while doing so), renal function and renal tract ultrasound (stones), and **DXA** (lumbar spine, hip and **distal radius** — cortical bone, specifically affected by PTH, is best seen here) to assess fracture risk.
  - Hypocalcaemia: calcium, phosphate, PTH and magnesium together are the key panel. **Low calcium + high phosphate + low/undetectable PTH** = hypoparathyroidism. **Low calcium + high phosphate + high PTH** = pseudohypoparathyroidism (PTH resistance) or, more commonly, vitamin D deficiency/CKD not yet fully compensated.
  - Vitamin D deficiency: serum **25-hydroxyvitamin D** (the storage form, reflects status); calcium/phosphate may be low-normal, ALP often raised; X-ray in osteomalacia can show **Looser's zones** (pseudofractures).
  - Paget's disease: markedly raised **alkaline phosphatase** with normal calcium, phosphate and liver function (confirms the ALP is of bony origin); plain X-ray shows characteristic lytic and sclerotic change; isotope bone scan to map disease extent.
treatment: >+
  Primary hyperparathyroidism (NICE NG132)

  - **Refer for parathyroidectomy** if: corrected calcium **≥2.85 mmol/L**, or any end-organ disease — renal stones, osteoporosis/fragility fracture, or deteriorating renal function — regardless of calcium level. Referral can still be considered even without these features once the diagnosis is confirmed.
  - Pre-operative imaging (usually ultrasound ± sestamibi scan) if it will change the surgical approach (e.g. a focused, minimally invasive operation rather than full neck exploration). **Cinacalcet** (a calcimimetic) is an option if surgery is unsuitable or declined, or after unsuccessful surgery. A **bisphosphonate** can be used to reduce fracture risk but does not treat the underlying disease.
  - Post-operatively, calcium is checked promptly (risk of **hungry bone syndrome** — severe hypocalcaemia as bone rapidly re-mineralises after years of PTH-driven resorption) and again at 3–6 months to confirm cure.

  Secondary/tertiary hyperparathyroidism

  - Secondary: treat the driver — vitamin D/active vitamin D analogues and phosphate control in CKD. Tertiary (autonomous): cinacalcet, or parathyroidectomy if severe/refractory.

  Acute symptomatic hypocalcaemia

  - **IV calcium gluconate** (e.g. 10 mL of 10% over 10 minutes, with cardiac monitoring, repeated/infused as needed) for tetany, seizures or a prolonged QT. **Check and correct magnesium** — hypocalcaemia will not respond to calcium replacement if magnesium is also low.
  - Chronic: oral calcium plus **calcitriol or alfacalcidol** (active vitamin D, since the normal activation step may itself be impaired) rather than plain vitamin D in true hypoparathyroidism (see the Hypoparathyroidism card for detail).

  Vitamin D deficiency / osteomalacia

  - Loading regimen of vitamin D3 (e.g. a high-dose loading course followed by maintenance), with calcium supplementation if dietary intake is poor. Monitor calcium once treatment starts, especially if primary hyperparathyroidism could coexist.

  Paget's disease of bone

  - Treat if symptomatic (bone pain), or to prevent complications at high-risk sites (skull base, weight-bearing bone, near a joint): a **bisphosphonate** (e.g. zoledronic acid) is first-line, normalising bone turnover and often relieving pain. Asymptomatic disease with a normal-risk site does not necessarily need treatment.
protocols: >+
  Hypercalcaemic crisis

  > Marked hypercalcaemia (often >3.5 mmol/L) with dehydration, confusion or renal impairment is a medical emergency, usually from malignancy or very severe primary hyperparathyroidism.

  - **IV 0.9% saline** to rehydrate and promote renal calcium excretion (typically several litres over 24 hours, with caution in heart failure), then an **IV bisphosphonate** (e.g. zoledronic acid or pamidronate) once rehydrated — the bisphosphonate takes 2–4 days to have full effect. Stop any calcium, vitamin D, thiazide diuretics or lithium. Calcitonin gives a faster but short-lived effect in very severe cases. Dialysis is reserved for severe or refractory cases, especially with renal failure.
  - See the Cancer and oncology emergencies factfile (Acute medicine) for the full hypercalcaemia-of-malignancy protocol.

  Severe hypocalcaemia

  - Laryngospasm, seizures or significant arrhythmia need IV calcium gluconate without delay, cardiac monitoring, and correction of magnesium — don't wait for a formal cause to be established before treating the acute threat.
