background: |+
  A congenital **accessory electrical pathway (the "Bundle of Kent")** connects the atria directly to the ventricles, bypassing the normal AV node. Since this pathway conducts faster than the AV node (which normally delays conduction to protect the ventricles), the ventricles are activated partly through the accessory pathway and partly through the normal pathway — this partial, early ventricular activation is called **pre-excitation**.
  - **WPW pattern** refers to the ECG findings alone (see Diagnosis); **WPW syndrome** specifically means the ECG pattern **plus** episodes of symptomatic tachyarrhythmia — the distinction matters because the pattern alone, found incidentally, carries a much smaller (though not zero) risk than confirmed syndrome with symptoms.
  - **The arrhythmia risk comes from two distinct mechanisms**: (1) **AVRT** (AV re-entry tachycardia) — a re-entry circuit using the accessory pathway in one direction and the AV node in the other, producing a regular narrow- or broad-complex tachycardia depending on the direction of circuit travel; (2) **AF with pre-excited, very rapid ventricular conduction** — the accessory pathway, unlike the AV node, has **no inbuilt rate-limiting property**, so if AF develops, it can conduct to the ventricles extremely fast and **degenerate into VF** — this is the genuinely life-threatening scenario in WPW, not AVRT itself.
diagnosis: |+
  - Many patients are **asymptomatic**, with the ECG pattern found incidentally. Symptomatic patients present with episodic palpitations (from AVRT), which can be associated with dizziness, breathlessness or chest discomfort, similar to other paroxysmal SVTs.
  - **Resting ECG (sinus rhythm) shows the classic triad**: a **short PR interval (<120 ms)** (the accessory pathway bypasses the normal AV nodal delay), a **delta wave** (a slurred, slow initial upstroke of the QRS, from early ventricular activation via the accessory pathway), and a **widened QRS (>110–120 ms)**.
  - **During AVRT**, the delta wave typically disappears (conduction is now going around the re-entry loop, not simultaneously down both pathways as at rest) — the tachycardia looks like a typical narrow-complex SVT in the commoner ("orthodromic") form, which can make it indistinguishable from AVNRT on the tachycardia ECG alone; the resting ECG between episodes is what reveals the underlying pathway.
  - **Pre-excited AF** (AF occurring in a patient with an accessory pathway) looks dramatically different: an **irregular, broad-complex tachycardia with a very fast, often extremely irregular rate** and beat-to-beat variation in QRS morphology (varying fusion between pathway and normal conduction) — this pattern should specifically raise concern for WPW rather than being treated as routine fast AF.
investigations: |+
  - **12-lead ECG in sinus rhythm** is the key test, looking for the short PR/delta wave/wide QRS pattern described above.
  - **Echocardiography** to exclude associated structural heart disease (WPW has a recognised association with **Ebstein's anomaly** of the tricuspid valve, among other congenital conditions, though most patients have a structurally normal heart).
  - **Electrophysiology (EP) study** is used to localise the accessory pathway precisely, assess its conduction properties (specifically, how fast it can conduct — its "refractory period," which predicts the risk of dangerously fast conduction during AF), and is combined with **catheter ablation** as both the definitive risk-stratification and the definitive treatment in one procedure.
treatment: |+
  - **Asymptomatic WPW pattern, found incidentally**: risk-stratify (often with an EP study, particularly in younger patients or those in higher-risk occupations such as pilots/professional drivers) rather than automatically treating — many have a long-conducting (lower risk) pathway and can simply be monitored, though practice varies and specialist cardiology/electrophysiology input should guide this decision.
  - **Symptomatic AVRT (acute termination)**: managed the same way as other narrow-complex re-entry SVT when it presents as a typical narrow-complex tachycardia — vagal manoeuvres first, then IV adenosine (see the Supraventricular arrhythmias card) — this is safe and effective for terminating the AVRT itself.
  - **Definitive treatment: catheter ablation of the accessory pathway** — curative in the large majority of cases, and the treatment of choice for confirmed WPW syndrome (symptomatic arrhythmia), avoiding the need for long-term drug therapy.
protocols: |+
  > Red flag: **AV-nodal blocking drugs — adenosine, verapamil, digoxin, and beta-blockers in this specific context — must be avoided in pre-excited AF (irregular, broad-complex, very fast tachycardia in a patient with known or suspected WPW).** Blocking the AV node removes its competing, slower conduction pathway, which can paradoxically **increase** conduction down the accessory pathway and precipitate **VF**.

  - **Pre-excited AF, or any broad-complex tachycardia in known/suspected WPW**: treat as potentially life-threatening — **synchronised DC cardioversion** is the safest option if the patient is unstable; if stable and expert help is available, **IV flecainide or procainamide** (which slow conduction through the accessory pathway) are the drugs of choice rather than AV-nodal blockers. This is a scenario where getting cardiology/electrophysiology advice early is particularly valuable given the risk of making things worse with an intuitively "standard" rate-control approach.
  - Any new broad-complex, irregular, very fast tachycardia with beat-to-beat morphology variation should prompt consideration of pre-excited AF specifically, rather than being managed as routine fast AF — see the Supraventricular and Ventricular arrhythmia cards for the general approach to each pattern once WPW has been considered and excluded or confirmed.
