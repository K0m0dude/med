background: >+
  The hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis (GnRH → LH/FSH → sex steroids) is the shared framework for hypogonadism in both sexes.

  - **Primary (hypergonadotropic) hypogonadism**: the gonad itself fails, so LH/FSH rise appropriately as negative feedback is lost. In men: **Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY)** is the commonest cause — small firm testes, infertility, gynaecomastia, usually identified at puberty or during an infertility work-up; also testicular damage (mumps orchitis, chemotherapy/radiotherapy, trauma, torsion). In women: **primary ovarian insufficiency** (premature menopause, autoimmune oophoritis, **Turner syndrome (45,X)**, chemotherapy/radiotherapy).
  - **Secondary (hypogonadotropic) hypogonadism**: the hypothalamus/pituitary fails to drive the gonad, so LH/FSH are low/inappropriately normal. **Kallmann syndrome** is a classic cause — failure of GnRH neuron migration, causing hypogonadotropic hypogonadism **with anosmia** (loss of smell) — a useful and specific clue. Other causes: any pituitary/hypothalamic disease (see the Pituitary disorders card), hyperprolactinaemia (prolactin suppresses GnRH), significant systemic illness, excessive exercise/low energy availability, opioids, and anabolic steroid use (which suppresses the axis via negative feedback, exactly like exogenous testosterone would).
  - **Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)**: not primarily a disorder of gonadotrophin failure, but the commonest overall cause of female subfertility and androgen excess — a heterogeneous condition combining anovulation, hyperandrogenism, and (often, not always) polycystic ovarian morphology, closely linked to insulin resistance.
diagnosis: >+
  - **Male hypogonadism**: reduced libido, erectile dysfunction, infertility, reduced muscle bulk, gynaecomastia, reduced body/facial hair, small testes. Pre-pubertal onset causes delayed puberty and eunuchoid body proportions; post-pubertal onset is more subtle.
  - **Female hypogonadism**: oligo/amenorrhoea, infertility, low libido, and (if oestrogen-deficient for a prolonged period) menopausal-type symptoms (hot flushes, vaginal dryness) and accelerated bone loss.
  - **PCOS (Rotterdam criteria — need 2 of 3, after excluding mimics)**: (1) oligo-/anovulation (irregular or absent periods), (2) clinical (hirsutism, acne, male-pattern hair loss) or biochemical hyperandrogenism, (3) polycystic ovaries on ultrasound (or a raised AMH as an alternative). Exclude conditions that mimic it before diagnosing: thyroid disease, hyperprolactinaemia, late-onset congenital adrenal hyperplasia, Cushing's syndrome, and an androgen-secreting tumour (the last suggested by rapid-onset or severe virilisation — deepening voice, clitoromegaly — which should prompt urgent investigation rather than a routine PCOS work-up).
  - **Basic infertility assessment** (either partner, after ~12 months of regular unprotected intercourse without conception, or sooner if there's a known risk factor or the woman is ≥36): a full history and examination of both partners, with day-21 (mid-luteal) progesterone to confirm ovulation in the woman and a semen analysis in the man, before referral to specialist fertility services.
investigations: >+
  - **9 am LH, FSH and testosterone/oestradiol** distinguishes primary (high LH/FSH, low sex steroid) from secondary (low/inappropriately normal LH/FSH, low sex steroid) hypogonadism. **Prolactin** and a wider pituitary panel if secondary hypogonadism is confirmed (see the Pituitary disorders card), since it may be the presenting feature of pituitary disease.
  - **Karyotype** for suspected Klinefelter's or Turner syndrome. **Smell testing and pituitary MRI** if Kallmann syndrome is suspected (anosmia plus hypogonadotropic hypogonadism).
  - **PCOS**: **testosterone and SHBG** (calculate the free androgen index), LH/FSH (an elevated LH:FSH ratio is suggestive but not required for diagnosis), pelvic ultrasound (≥20 follicles per ovary and/or increased ovarian volume on modern criteria) or **AMH** as an alternative to ultrasound. Screen for **metabolic complications**: HbA1c/OGTT and a lipid profile, given the strong association with insulin resistance.
treatment: >+
  - **Male hypogonadism**: **testosterone replacement** (gel, injection or implant) for confirmed hypogonadism in a man not currently seeking fertility — improves libido, energy, muscle mass and bone density; monitor haematocrit (risk of secondary polycythaemia), PSA, and symptoms. **Testosterone replacement suppresses the HPG axis and is not a fertility treatment** — it will worsen sperm production; **gonadotrophin therapy (hCG ± FSH)** is used instead if fertility is wanted in secondary hypogonadism.
  - **Female hypogonadism**: hormone replacement (oestrogen ± progestogen, as appropriate to the cause and whether the uterus is intact) for symptom control and bone protection; specialist fertility treatment (ovulation induction, or gonadotrophins in hypogonadotropic disease) if pregnancy is desired.
  - **PCOS — general**: weight management where relevant (even modest weight loss can restore ovulation), a **combined oral contraceptive pill** for cycle regulation and hirsutism/acne if pregnancy isn't currently wanted, and **metformin** for the metabolic/insulin-resistance features (limited direct effect on fertility).
  - **PCOS — fertility**: **letrozole** is now generally used first-line for ovulation induction (more effective than clomifene per recent international guidance), with clomifene (alone or with metformin) as an alternative, and gonadotrophins or laparoscopic ovarian drilling as further options if first-line treatment fails — managed via specialist fertility services.
protocols: >+
  - Rapid-onset or severe virilisation (clitoromegaly, significant voice change, very high testosterone) is **not** routine PCOS — treat as a possible androgen-secreting tumour and image the ovaries/adrenals urgently rather than starting standard PCOS management.
  - Suspected **ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome** during fertility treatment (abdominal distension/pain, nausea, breathlessness, reduced urine output after ovulation induction) needs urgent specialist fertility/gynaecology assessment — it can progress to significant fluid shifts, thromboembolism and renal impairment.
  - Hypogonadism found incidentally alongside other pituitary hormone deficits, a headache, or a visual field defect should prompt pituitary imaging rather than isolated hormone replacement — see the Pituitary disorders card.
