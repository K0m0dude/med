background: |+
  A common emergency and ward presentation. The job is to **exclude the dangerous and treatable first** (malaria, bacterial sepsis, enteric fever) while screening for the rare but high-consequence infections that need isolation (viral haemorrhagic fever, other high-consequence infectious diseases).

  - **Malaria is the commonest serious diagnosis** and can kill within days. It is the first thing to test for in any traveller with fever from an endemic area (and for up to a year after return, longer for vivax/ovale), whatever prophylaxis was taken.
  - **Most febrile returning travellers have a common, self-limiting or readily treatable illness**: viral respiratory infection, gastroenteritis, UTI, and dengue. Always consider **non-travel causes** (pneumonia, UTI, endocarditis) and, in the right history, **HIV seroconversion**, hepatitis and sexually transmitted infections.
  - **Incubation period is the most useful clue** (shown below). The **dates of exposure, not just the destination**, matter.

  | Time from exposure to fever | Think of |
  |---|---|
  | Under 10 days | Dengue, chikungunya, Zika, rickettsial infection (spotted fever, scrub typhus), travellers' diarrhoea (bacterial), influenza and other respiratory viruses, early malaria, meningococcal disease, **viral haemorrhagic fevers (within 21 days)** |
  | 10–21 days | **Malaria**, typhoid and paratyphoid, leptospirosis, brucellosis, Q fever, acute HIV, hepatitis A and E |
  | Over 21 days | Malaria (especially vivax/ovale relapse), TB, hepatitis, **schistosomiasis (Katayama fever, 3–8 weeks after freshwater exposure)**, amoebic liver abscess, visceral leishmaniasis, HIV |

  > **Microbiology and pathology**
  >
  > - **Fever mechanism**: exogenous pyrogens (PAMPs such as LPS) and endogenous pyrogens (**IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α**) induce **COX-2 and prostaglandin E₂** in the hypothalamus (OVLT), raising the thermoregulatory set-point; this is fever (as opposed to hyperthermia, where the set-point is unchanged).
  > - **Rickettsiae** are obligate intracellular bacteria that infect **endothelial cells**, causing vasculitis (fever, rash, an inoculation **eschar** in scrub typhus and African tick bite fever). **Leptospira** (spirochaetes from rat urine in water) cause vasculitis, with AKI, jaundice and haemorrhage in Weil's disease.
  > - **Viral haemorrhagic fevers** (Ebola, Lassa, Crimean–Congo, Marburg) target **monocytes/macrophages and dendritic cells**, producing profound immune dysregulation, endothelial dysfunction and coagulopathy. They are important because of **onward transmission to healthcare workers**.
  > - **Eosinophilia** on the FBC points to a **helminth infection** (strongyloidiasis, schistosomiasis, filariasis); *Strongyloides* can hyperinfect on steroids, so check before immunosuppression.
diagnosis: |+
  Take a **structured travel history**

  - **Where and when**: countries, rural vs urban, **dates of departure and return**, and the date of symptom onset (work out the incubation period).
  - **Exposures**: mosquito and tick bites, freshwater swimming (schistosomiasis), animal contact or bites (rabies risk), raw or undercooked food, unpasteurised milk, untreated water, sexual contact, tattoos/injections, hospital care abroad, **contact with sick people or funerals** (VHF), caves/bats.
  - **Prophylaxis and vaccinations**: which antimalarial, how adherent; hepatitis A/B, typhoid, yellow fever, rabies, and others.
  - **Examine thoroughly**: rash, **eschar**, jaundice, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, conjunctival suffusion, bleeding, meningism, neurological signs, and look for localising sources of infection as well as tropical causes.
  - **Severity assessment**: NEWS2 and sepsis screen at triage; many travellers are sicker than they look.
investigations: |+
  - **Malaria: rapid test plus thick and thin films urgently**, in every febrile traveller from an endemic area; **repeat at 12–24 hour intervals (three sets in total) if negative** while suspicion remains.
  - **Initial bloods**: FBC with film (**thrombocytopenia** common in malaria/dengue; **eosinophilia** for helminths; **lymphopenia**), U&E, LFTs, CRP, glucose, lactate, clotting, **two sets of blood cultures** (enteric fever), urinalysis and culture, CXR. **Hold a serum sample** for later serology and tests.
  - **Targeted tests**: dengue NS1/PCR/IgM; HIV (including 4th-generation antigen/antibody for seroconversion); hepatitis A/B/C/E serology; stool culture/PCR and ova, cysts and parasites; schistosomiasis serology (with exposure) and typhoid cultures; leptospirosis PCR/serology; rickettsial serology/PCR; Lassa/Ebola/CCHF only after risk assessment (below).
  - **Viral haemorrhagic fever (VHF) risk assessment — in anyone with fever (or a history of fever) within 21 days of return from a relevant area, or contact with a VHF case**: ask about **exposure risk and symptoms**. If a risk is identified, **isolate the patient in a single room**, use **PPE per UK high-consequence infectious disease guidance**, and **contact the local infectious diseases/microbiology consultant and the UKHSA imported fever service at once**. **Malaria should still be tested for**, using appropriate precautions, since it is far more likely.
treatment: |+
  - **Treat the most likely and most dangerous diagnosis promptly.** If malaria is confirmed, see the Common tropical infections card. If the patient is septic and no source is clear, follow the **Sepsis Six pathway** and start **broad-spectrum antibiotics** (typically **ceftriaxone**, adding cover for atypical/rickettsial infection, for example **doxycycline**, if clinically indicated), on infectious diseases advice.
  - **Dengue**: supportive (avoid NSAIDs). **Typhoid**: azithromycin or ceftriaxone. **Leptospirosis**: IV benzylpenicillin or ceftriaxone (doxycycline for milder disease). **Rickettsial infection/scrub typhus**: **doxycycline** (empirical, without waiting for confirmation). **Amoebic liver abscess**: metronidazole.
  - **Schistosomiasis**: praziquantel (timing matters — Katayama fever may need steroids with it). **Strongyloides**: ivermectin.
  - **Isolation and infection control**: contagious diarrhoea, respiratory viruses, and any suspected VHF need appropriate precautions from the point of first contact.
  - **Notify** notifiable diseases (malaria, typhoid/paratyphoid, hepatitis A, dengue-related VHF considerations, and others per local practice). **Advise on malaria prevention and vaccines** before future travel.
protocols: |+
  > Red flag: **fever after travel + any sign of severity** (confusion, shock, bleeding, jaundice, respiratory distress) = treat as severe malaria or sepsis, test urgently, and escalate. **Don't discharge a febrile traveller with an unexplained thrombocytopenia without a plan** — dengue and malaria can deteriorate over 24–48 hours.

  - **Suspected VHF**: do **not** send routine samples to the general lab or have multiple staff examine the patient; follow the UK high-consequence infectious disease pathway (specialist laboratory arrangements, designated treatment centres).
  - **Sexual-health and blood-borne exposures** (HIV seroconversion, hepatitis, syphilis) should not be forgotten; offer testing with consent and follow-up.
  - See the Tropical infections, Gastrointestinal infections and Pyrexia of unknown origin cards for the detail of specific diagnoses.
