background: |+
  Pericarditis (inflammation of the pericardium) and myocarditis (inflammation of the myocardium itself) share many causes and often coexist ("myopericarditis"), but differ in their key risk: pericarditis is usually self-limiting and benign, while myocarditis carries a real risk of arrhythmia, acute heart failure and sudden cardiac death.

  Pericarditis

  - **Viral infection is the commonest cause** (coxsackievirus and other enteroviruses, among others) in otherwise idiopathic cases — often no specific organism is ever identified, and "idiopathic" and "viral" are used almost interchangeably in practice. Other causes: **post-MI** (early, within days — fibrinous pericarditis; or **Dressler's syndrome**, an autoimmune pericarditis weeks to months after MI or cardiac surgery), TB, bacterial, uraemia, autoimmune/connective tissue disease (SLE, rheumatoid arthritis), malignancy, radiotherapy, and drugs.

  Myocarditis

  - Also usually **viral** (coxsackie B classically, also parvovirus B19, adenovirus, influenza, and others, including some vaccines/drugs rarely) — direct viral invasion of myocytes and/or a subsequent immune-mediated response causes the damage. Other causes: autoimmune disease, **immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy** (an important, potentially severe cause — see the Cancer factfile, Acute medicine), and toxins (alcohol, cocaine).
  - Can present anywhere from a mild, self-limiting illness to **fulminant myocarditis** — acute, severe heart failure and/or life-threatening arrhythmia in a previously well (often young) person, which can be rapidly fatal without aggressive support.
diagnosis: |+
  Pericarditis (diagnosis needs ≥2 of the following four features)

  - **Pleuritic chest pain** that characteristically improves sitting forward and worsens lying flat.
  - **Pericardial friction rub** on auscultation — a superficial, scratchy sound, best heard at the left sternal edge with the patient leaning forward, classically with up to three components (atrial systole, ventricular systole, rapid ventricular filling); it can be transient, so its absence does not exclude the diagnosis.
  - **Characteristic ECG changes** (see Investigations).
  - **New or worsening pericardial effusion** (see that card).

  Myocarditis

  - Can present with **chest pain** (often mimicking ACS), **breathlessness and signs of heart failure**, **palpitations/arrhythmia**, or simply **unexplained fatigue** — frequently preceded by a recent viral illness (fever, myalgia, coryzal symptoms) in the preceding days to weeks.
  - A high index of suspicion is needed in a **young patient with "ACS-like" chest pain and a normal or near-normal coronary angiogram**, or anyone presenting with new unexplained heart failure/arrhythmia after a recent viral illness.
investigations: |+
  Pericarditis

  - **ECG**: widespread ("saddle-shaped") **concave ST elevation** across multiple territories not respecting a single coronary distribution (unlike MI), with **PR depression** (sometimes **PR elevation in aVR** specifically) — the combination of ST elevation with PR depression, in a pattern crossing several territories, points away from ACS.
  - **Inflammatory markers** (CRP, ESR) are often raised and can be used to track disease activity/response to treatment. **Troponin** can be mildly raised with associated myocardial involvement (myopericarditis) — a significantly raised troponin should prompt consideration of myocarditis rather than pericarditis alone.
  - **Echocardiography** to look for an accompanying effusion and to assess LV function.

  Myocarditis

  - **Troponin is usually raised**, sometimes substantially, mimicking ACS. **ECG** can show a range of changes — ST elevation/depression, T wave inversion, or new conduction abnormalities/arrhythmia — again often mimicking ACS or another cause.
  - **Coronary angiography** is frequently performed to exclude epicardial coronary disease when the presentation mimics ACS, particularly in someone with cardiac risk factors — a normal or non-obstructive angiogram in this context supports myocarditis.
  - **Cardiac MRI** is the key non-invasive diagnostic test — oedema and late gadolinium enhancement in a non-coronary distribution (the **Lake Louise criteria**) support the diagnosis and help define the extent of myocardial involvement.
  - **Endomyocardial biopsy** is reserved for specific scenarios (fulminant presentation, suspected giant cell myocarditis, or where the result would change management) rather than used routinely, given its invasiveness and sampling limitations.
treatment: |+
  Pericarditis

  - **High-dose NSAIDs (e.g. ibuprofen or aspirin) plus colchicine** is the standard first-line combination — colchicine both improves symptom resolution and significantly reduces the risk of recurrence, so is added even though NSAIDs alone can control symptoms.
  - **Corticosteroids are not first-line** and are generally reserved for cases with a contraindication to NSAIDs/colchicine, an autoimmune cause, or recurrent pericarditis refractory to standard treatment — steroids given early/as initial treatment are actually associated with a **higher** recurrence rate.
  - **Avoid strenuous exercise** until symptoms resolve and inflammatory markers normalise.
  - **Recurrent pericarditis**: colchicine continued for a longer course is key to prevention; refractory, frequently recurrent cases may need specialist immunomodulatory treatment (e.g. an IL-1 antagonist) under cardiology input.

  Myocarditis

  - **Largely supportive** — there is no specific antiviral treatment for most causes. Manage any heart failure (see the Heart failure factfile) and arrhythmia (see the relevant arrhythmia cards) as they arise.
  - **Avoid strenuous exercise/competitive sport for at least 3–6 months** (per specialist cardiology guidance) given the arrhythmia and sudden cardiac death risk during the inflamed/recovery phase — this is a key, specific piece of safety-netting advice that should not be omitted.
  - **Fulminant myocarditis with cardiogenic shock** needs critical care-level support, potentially including mechanical circulatory support (see the Cardiogenic shock card) — some patients recover fully with aggressive support despite a dramatic initial presentation.
  - **Immune checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis** is managed with immediate cessation of the drug and high-dose corticosteroids, with early cardiology and oncology input — see the Cancer factfile (Acute medicine); it carries a disproportionately high mortality for an immune-related adverse event and should be treated as a medical emergency.
protocols: |+
  > Red flag: new arrhythmia, heart block, or signs of heart failure in a patient with suspected or confirmed myocarditis needs continuous cardiac monitoring and early cardiology involvement — clinical deterioration can be rapid, and this is one of the few "benign-looking" presentations (young, previously fit patient, viral prodrome) that can progress to cardiac arrest with little warning.
  - **Cardiac tamponade** can complicate pericarditis if a significant effusion develops — see the Cardiac tamponade and Pericardial effusion cards; reassess urgently for this if a patient being treated for simple pericarditis deteriorates (new hypotension, rising JVP).
  - Chest pain that doesn't fit the classic pericarditis pattern, or any diagnostic uncertainty with ACS, should prompt the same urgent work-up as suspected ACS (serial troponin, early cardiology review) rather than assuming pericarditis/myocarditis by default — see the ACS factfile (Acute medicine).
