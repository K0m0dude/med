background: |+
  Infection of the nose, sinuses, pharynx, larynx or upper airway above the vocal cords — overwhelmingly **viral** and self-limiting, but including a small number of important bacterial and airway-threatening mimics that must not be missed.

  - **Common cold (viral rhinosinusitis)**: rhinovirus is the commonest cause; also coronaviruses, RSV, influenza, parainfluenza. The most frequent human infection, self-limiting over about a week.
  - **Pharyngitis/tonsillitis**: mostly viral; **group A streptococcus (*Streptococcus pyogenes*)** is the main bacterial cause worth identifying, since it is the one URTI organism with both a specific complication profile and clear antibiotic benefit in the right context.
  - **Acute sinusitis**: usually viral, following or alongside a cold; bacterial superinfection is uncommon despite how often antibiotics are prescribed for it.
  - **Acute otitis media**: common in children, usually viral or a mix of viral/bacterial; *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Haemophilus influenzae* are the typical bacterial causes when present.
  - **Infectious mononucleosis (glandular fever)**: Epstein-Barr virus — a distinctive, often more prolonged URTI-mimicking illness, important to recognise because of its specific complications and because **amoxicillin/ampicillin characteristically triggers a widespread itchy maculopapular rash** if mistakenly given for presumed bacterial tonsillitis.
  - **Croup**: parainfluenza virus (usually), laryngotracheobronchitis causing subglottic swelling — a paediatric diagnosis, included here as a key differential for acute airway-threatening URTI.
  - **Epiglottitis**: now rare since *Haemophilus influenzae* type b (Hib) vaccination, but a critical "must not miss" airway emergency when it does occur.
diagnosis: |+
  - **Common cold**: coryza, sore throat, mild cough, low-grade or no fever, generally well — symptoms peak around day 2–3 and usually resolve within a week to ten days.
  - **Pharyngitis/tonsillitis**: sore throat, fever, enlarged/inflamed tonsils ± exudate, tender cervical lymphadenopathy. **Centor criteria** (fever, tonsillar exudate, tender anterior cervical lymphadenopathy, absence of cough — one point each) and **FeverPAIN** (Fever, Purulence, Attend rapidly ≤3 days, severely Inflamed tonsils, No cough/coryza) both estimate the likelihood of group A strep and help guide the antibiotic decision (see Treatment) — neither should be used in isolation without clinical judgement, and neither reliably rules out a more serious cause on its own.
  - **Infectious mononucleosis**: sore throat, fever and prominent **lymphadenopathy (often posterior cervical, sometimes generalised)**, marked fatigue, and often **splenomegaly** (and sometimes hepatomegaly/mild jaundice) — the illness and fatigue typically last longer (weeks) than a typical bacterial or viral tonsillitis.
  - **Acute sinusitis**: facial pain/pressure (worse bending forward), nasal blockage and purulent discharge, following a cold; most "sinusitis" seen in primary care is viral and self-limiting.
  - **Acute otitis media**: ear pain (or pulling at the ear in a pre-verbal child), fever, a bulging or perforated tympanic membrane with discharge, reduced hearing.
investigations: |+
  - **Most URTIs are diagnosed clinically — no routine investigations are needed.** Throat swabs are not routinely recommended for simple pharyngitis, since clinical scoring (Centor/FeverPAIN) is used to guide management rather than waiting for a culture result that takes days.
  - **Suspected infectious mononucleosis**: **Monospot/heterophile antibody test** (can be falsely negative early in the illness, particularly in the first week — a repeat test or EBV-specific serology is used if clinical suspicion remains high), FBC (looking for lymphocytosis with atypical lymphocytes), and LFTs (mild transaminitis is common).
  - **Suspected quinsy (peritonsillar abscess)**: usually a clinical diagnosis (trismus, a muffled "hot potato" voice, uvula deviation away from the swollen side) — same-day ENT assessment rather than imaging in the first instance.
  - **Suspected epiglottitis or a compromised airway**: **do not examine the throat, attempt to visualise the epiglottis with a tongue depressor, or lie the patient down** — this can precipitate complete airway obstruction. Keep the patient calm and sitting up, and get urgent senior anaesthetic/ENT help; imaging (a lateral neck X-ray showing the "thumb sign") is only appropriate once the airway is secured or if it can be done without distressing the patient, and should never delay getting expert airway help.
treatment: |+
  - **Most URTIs need only symptomatic treatment**: fluids, rest, paracetamol/NSAIDs for fever and pain, and simple safety-netting about expected duration and when to seek further review.
  - **Pharyngitis/tonsillitis — antibiotics**: guided by Centor (≥3) or FeverPAIN (4–5) scores, which identify those more likely to have group A strep and more likely to benefit — even then, the absolute benefit of antibiotics (mainly shortening symptoms by about a day) is modest, so a **back-up/delayed prescription** is often appropriate rather than immediate antibiotics for everyone scoring highly. **Penicillin V (phenoxymethylpenicillin)** is first-line where an antibiotic is indicated; **avoid amoxicillin/ampicillin** if infectious mononucleosis is a realistic possibility, because of the characteristic rash.
  - **Acute sinusitis**: most cases don't need antibiotics; consider one only if symptoms are severe, worsening after initial improvement, or persisting beyond about 10 days without improvement, per usual antimicrobial stewardship principles — similar reasoning to acute bronchitis (see that card).
  - **Acute otitis media**: most resolves without antibiotics; a back-up/delayed prescription is standard, with an immediate antibiotic reserved for systemic illness, a perforated/discharging ear, bilateral disease in a young child, or significant comorbidity/immunosuppression.
  - **Infectious mononucleosis**: supportive care (rest, fluids, analgesia); advise **avoiding contact sports for about 3–4 weeks** because of the splenomegaly and a small but serious risk of splenic rupture.
  - **Quinsy (peritonsillar abscess)**: needs ENT assessment for **aspiration or incision and drainage**, plus antibiotics — this does not settle with oral antibiotics alone.
  - **Croup**: a single dose of **oral dexamethasone** for even mild cases significantly improves symptoms and reduces the need for further healthcare contact; nebulised adrenaline is reserved for more severe cases with significant stridor/distress at rest, alongside senior paediatric input.
protocols: |+
  Epiglottitis

  > A rare but classic "cannot miss" emergency: rapid-onset high fever, severe sore throat (often disproportionate to how little is visible on examination), drooling (unable to swallow secretions), a muffled voice, and stridor, with the patient instinctively **sitting forward** to maximise their airway.

  - **Do not examine the throat or attempt to lie the patient flat** — this can trigger complete obstruction. Keep the patient calm, sitting up, with oxygen given only if tolerated without distress, and call for **urgent senior anaesthetic and ENT support** to secure the airway in a controlled environment (theatre), rather than attempting airway instrumentation on the ward.
  - IV antibiotics (per local policy, typically covering *H. influenzae* and other likely organisms) are given once the airway is secured or under direct senior supervision — airway control always comes first.

  Other red flags

  - **Quinsy with trismus, drooling, or deviation of the uvula** needs same-day ENT assessment — don't manage as simple tonsillitis with oral antibiotics alone.
  - **Infectious mononucleosis with severe abdominal pain** (possible splenic rupture, a rare but serious complication) is a surgical emergency needing urgent assessment.
  - **Croup with stridor at rest, marked respiratory distress, or exhaustion** needs urgent escalation (senior paediatric/anaesthetic review) rather than routine dexamethasone and discharge.
