background: >+
  - Total body water ≈**60%** of body weight. Of this, **⅔ is intracellular**
  and **⅓ extracellular**; of the extracellular compartment, **¾ is
  interstitial** and **¼ is intravascular (plasma)**. ∴ even a large IV
  crystalloid bolus mostly ends up outside the vessels within an hour or two –
  only a minority stays intravascular to support BP.

  - Normal regulation: osmoreceptors and ADH control water/osmolality;
  baroreceptors and the RAAS control volume/sodium; natriuretic peptides oppose
  both when volume is high. Obligate losses even with no intake: urine (≥400–500
  mL/day to excrete the solute load), insensible losses via skin/respiration
  (**~500–800 mL/day** at rest, more with fever, tachypnoea or sweating), and a
  small faecal loss.

  - **Why mismanagement matters**: too little → hypovolaemia → reduced organ
  perfusion → AKI, confusion, falls. Too much → interstitial and pulmonary
  oedema, worse wound healing, prolonged ileus, and (specifically with 0.9%
  saline) a **hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis** from its supraphysiological
  chloride load.

  - **NICE CG174** (“intravenous fluid therapy in adults in hospital”) organises
  all of this around the **5 Rs**: **R**esuscitation, **R**outine maintenance,
  **R**eplacement, **R**edistribution, **R**eassessment – covered as four
  practical algorithms (Assessment → Resuscitation / Routine maintenance /
  Replacement and redistribution → back to Reassessment).

  - **Scope**: adults (≥16 yrs) in hospital. **Excludes** pregnancy, severe
  renal or hepatic impairment, diabetic emergencies (DKA/HHS have their own
  fluid protocols – see the Diabetes factfile) and burns (which use the Parkland
  formula: total crystalloid in the first 24h (mL) = 4 × %TBSA burn ×
  weight(kg), half given in the first 8h from the time of injury – outside
  CG174’s remit, but commonly examined). Children use the separate guideline
  NG29.

diagnosis: >-
  **History**: oral intake, GI losses (vomiting, diarrhoea,
  stoma/fistula/NG/drain output), blood loss, fever/sweating, urine output,
  comorbidities that change the target (heart failure, CKD, cirrhosis), and
  drugs that confound the picture (diuretics, ACEi/ARBs, NSAIDs).


  **Examination – hypovolaemia**: tachycardia, hypotension (or a postural drop),
  reduced JVP, dry mucous membranes, reduced skin turgor, prolonged capillary
  refill, cool peripheries, reduced urine output (<0.5 mL/kg/hr).


  **Examination – fluid overload**: raised JVP, bibasal crackles,
  peripheral/sacral oedema, weight gain, ascites.


  > **Indicators that a patient needs urgent fluid resuscitation** (NICE):
  systolic BP <100 mmHg, HR >90 bpm, capillary refill >2 s or cold peripheries,
  RR >20/min, NEWS2 ≥5. A **45° passive leg raise** that transiently improves
  BP/perfusion suggests the patient is “fluid responsive.”


  **“Third-spacing” / abnormal distribution** – a patient can be
  **intravascularly deplete while overall fluid-overloaded**: sepsis,
  pancreatitis, ileus/bowel obstruction, post-op capillary leak, and
  hypoalbuminaemia (cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome) all shift fluid into the
  interstitium. This is why “just give more fluid” can worsen oedema without
  fixing perfusion – it’s a frequent source of difficult fluid decisions and
  senior input is often needed.


  **Bedside tools**: fluid balance chart (ins: oral/IV/NG; outs: urine, vomit,
  diarrhoea, drains, blood, estimated insensible losses), daily weights (**1 kg
  change ≈ 1 L of fluid** – a useful check when balance charts are unreliable,
  which is common), urine output trend.
investigations: >+
  - **Bloods**: U&E and creatinine (trend, not just one value),
  bicarbonate/venous blood gas, FBC/haematocrit (haemoconcentration vs
  dilution), lactate. Urinary sodium (<20 mmol/L supports a
  pre-renal/hypovolaemic picture).

  - **Monitoring while on IV fluids**: at minimum, daily clinical review and
  U&E; more frequently (e.g. 4–6-hourly bloods, continuous monitoring) if
  resuscitating, if losses are large/ongoing, or if electrolytes are already
  abnormal. If a patient is transferred elsewhere, fluid status and the plan
  should be reassessed on arrival.

  - **Estimated serum osmolality** = 2 × (Na⁺ + K⁺) + urea + glucose (all
  mmol/L); normal range ≈ **275–295 mosmol/kg**. Worked example: Na 140, K 4,
  urea 6, glucose 6 → 2×144 + 6 + 6 = **300 mosmol/kg**. Useful for hyperosmolar
  states (see HHS, Diabetes factfile).


  **Fluid composition table** (electrolytes in mmol/L):



  |  | Na⁺ | Cl⁻ | K⁺ | HCO₃⁻ | Glucose |

  | ------------------------- | ------- | ------ | ----- | --------------- |
  ------- |

  | Plasma | 135–145 | 98–105 | 3.5–5 | 22–28 | – |

  | 0.9% saline | 154 | 154 | – | – | – |

  | 5% glucose | – | – | – | – | 50 g/L |

  | 0.18% saline + 4% glucose | 30 | 30 | – | – | 40 g/L |

  | Hartmann’s solution | 131 | 111 | 5 | 29 (as lactate) | – |



  - **0.9% saline**’s Na⁺/Cl⁻ are both supraphysiological (plasma Cl⁻ is only
  98–105) – large volumes → **hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis** (normal anion
  gap, falling HCO₃⁻, rising Cl⁻).

  - **Hartmann’s** is near-physiological and is generally preferred for
  replacement/resuscitation in large volumes – but contains K⁺ (caution in renal
  impairment/hyperkalaemia) and a lactate buffer metabolised by the liver
  (caution in severe hepatic impairment, which is outside CG174’s scope anyway).
  **Never run blood products through the same line as Hartmann’s** – its calcium
  can trigger clotting of citrate-anticoagulated blood; use 0.9% saline on a
  shared line instead.

  - **5% glucose** distributes across total body water almost immediately, so
  very little stays intravascular – poor for resuscitation, and large volumes
  risk hyponatraemia.

  - Colloids (gelatins, e.g. Gelofusine) are used far less now – anaphylaxis
  risk, no proven benefit over crystalloid for resuscitation.

treatment: |+
  **1. Resuscitation**

  - Crystalloid containing Na⁺ **130–154 mmol/L**, bolus **500 mL over <15 min** (**250 mL** if frail, elderly, or known heart/renal impairment).
  - Reassess with **ABCDE**. Still hypovolaemic → further **250–500 mL** bolus, reassess again. Repeat.
  - **Once 2000 mL has been given and the patient is still hypovolaemic (or at any point you’re unsure), get senior/expert help** rather than continuing alone.

  > The resuscitation bolus is a **fixed volume, not weight-based** – unlike paediatric practice (10–20 mL/kg), this is a frequently tested distinction.

  - Do **not** use tetrastarch. Consider human albumin solution 4–5% only in severe sepsis.
  - Active haemorrhage: treat as major haemorrhage (blood components, not crystalloid, once available) – see the GI bleeding factfile.

  **2. Routine maintenance** (no abnormal losses, not resuscitating)

  - **25–30 mL/kg/day** water; **~1 mmol/kg/day** each of Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻; **50–100 g/day** glucose (fixed, **not** weight-based – just enough to limit starvation ketosis, not a nutritional prescription).
  - **Consider the lower end (20–25 mL/kg/day)** for: older or frail patients, renal impairment, cardiac failure, and anyone at risk of **refeeding syndrome** (malnourished, minimal intake for days) – check and replace phosphate/magnesium/potassium *before and during* feeding/fluids in this group.

  *Worked example (your 80 kg patient):* target = **2000–2400 mL** water, **80 mmol** each Na⁺/K⁺/Cl⁻, **50–100 g** glucose/day.

  Using 1 L bags of 0.18% saline/4% glucose + 27 mmol/L KCl (Na 30, K 27, Cl 30, glucose 40 g per litre):


  | Bags/24h | Water | Na⁺/Cl⁻ | K⁺ | Glucose |
  | -------- | --------------------------- | ------------------------- | ---------------------- | ------------------- |
  | 2 | 2000 mL (bottom of range ✓) | 60 mmol (below target) | 54 mmol (below target) | 80 g (mid-range ✓) |
  | 3 | 3000 mL (above range) | 90 mmol (close to target) | 81 mmol (on target ✓) | 120 g (above range) |


  - Standard bag strengths **can’t hit all four targets simultaneously** for every weight – in practice, **3 bags/24h (often 8-hourly)** is the commonly used “default” because it lands closest on potassium, accepting slightly more volume/glucose than strictly calculated; it’s then individualised against the next day’s U&E rather than chased exactly.

  **3. Replacement and redistribution** (existing deficit/excess, ongoing abnormal losses, or abnormal distribution)

  - Match the replacement fluid to what’s actually being lost where possible:


  | Source | Rich in | Replacement principle |
  | --------------------------------------------- | ----------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
  | Vomiting / NG losses | H⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻ | Risk of hypokalaemic, hypochloraemic *alkalosis*; replace K⁺ |
  | Diarrhoea / high ileostomy | K⁺, HCO₃⁻ | Risk of hyperchloraemic *acidosis* if over-replaced with 0.9% saline |
  | Biliary drainage | Na⁺, HCO₃⁻ | Needs more Na⁺/bicarbonate-rich replacement |
  | “Third space” (sepsis, pancreatitis, post-op) | – | Abnormal distribution, not a true loss – treat the cause; avoid chasing volume with more fluid once perfusion is adequate (“de-resuscitate” as the patient stabilises) |


  - More frequent monitoring (U&E, fluid balance, sometimes twice-daily) while losses are ongoing or complex. This is the group most likely to need **expert input** – defined by NICE as a healthcare professional with core competencies in managing acute illness.

  **4. Reassessment** – built into every step above: ABCDE and continuous monitoring during resuscitation (RR, pulse, BP, perfusion, venous lactate/arterial pH and base excess); daily (or more often) clinical and laboratory review otherwise; reassess on transfer to a new location; reassess the prescription if hyperchloraemia or acidaemia develops.

  **Practical calculations**

  - **Infusion rate (mL/hr) = volume (mL) ÷ time (hr).** E.g. 1 L over 8 h = **125 mL/hr**; 1 L over 6 h = **167 mL/hr**.
  - **Drip rate (drops/min) = volume (mL) × drop factor (drops/mL) ÷ time (min).** Standard crystalloid giving sets run **20 drops/mL** (blood products are often ~15 drops/mL; paediatric burettes 60 drops/mL). E.g. 1000 mL over 8 h (480 min) at 20 drops/mL = 1000×20÷480 ≈ **42 drops/min**.
  - **Sodium correction limit**: no faster than **8–10 mmol/L in 24 h** in either direction (hypo- or hypernatraemia) to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome/cerebral oedema – faster correction only for acute, severe, symptomatic hyponatraemia (seizures, coma), under specialist guidance with hypertonic saline.
  - **Potassium replacement limit**: no faster than **10 mmol/hr** via a peripheral line without continuous cardiac monitoring (up to 20 mmol/hr only on a monitored unit).

protocols: >+
  #### Emergency protocols and red flags


  - **Fluid overload/pulmonary oedema**: breathlessness, bibasal crackles,
  falling SpO₂ during or after fluids → stop/slow the infusion, sit the patient
  up, give oxygen if hypoxic, consider IV furosemide, and get help (see the
  Heart failure factfile for acute pulmonary oedema management).

  - **Hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis**: normal anion gap, rising Cl⁻,
  falling HCO₃⁻/pH, usually after large volumes of 0.9% saline – switch to a
  more balanced fluid (e.g. Hartmann’s) and reassess.

  - **Resuscitation not working**: if still hypovolaemic after 2000 mL, stop
  giving more alone and get senior/critical care help – reconsider occult
  bleeding, sepsis, or a cardiogenic cause rather than simply continuing fluid.


  > Red flag: don’t correct chronic hyponatraemia or hypernatraemia faster than
  8–10 mmol/L/24h – rapid correction can cause irreversible neurological injury
  (osmotic demyelination / cerebral oedema), even though the sodium abnormality
  itself may look like the more urgent problem.


  - **Refeeding syndrome**: in anyone malnourished or with minimal intake for ≥5
  days, starting fluids/feeding can cause a sudden intracellular shift of
  phosphate, potassium and magnesium → arrhythmia, respiratory and cardiac
  failure. Check and replace these *before* fluids/feeding, start cautiously,
  and monitor daily.

  - Patients excluded from CG174 (pregnancy, severe renal/hepatic disease,
  DKA/HHS, burns) need their own specific protocols – don’t apply the standard
  maintenance or resuscitation numbers unmodified.
