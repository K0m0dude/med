background: |+
  Delay or failure of electrical conduction from the atria to the ventricles (AV block), or within the ventricular conduction system itself (bundle branch block). The key clinical question for any AV block is **where the block is** (AV node versus infranodal/His-Purkinje system), since this determines the risk of progression to complete heart block and whether pacing is needed.

  - **AV node disease** (Mobitz I/Wenckebach pattern) is usually **benign**, often from high vagal tone, and the escape rhythm (if complete block does occur) arises just below the block from the His bundle — narrow QRS, reasonably reliable rate.
  - **Infranodal (His-Purkinje) disease** (Mobitz II pattern) is more dangerous — it can progress abruptly and without warning to complete heart block, and the resulting escape rhythm (if any) arises much lower in the conduction system — wide QRS, slow and unreliable, with a real risk of asystole.
  - **Causes**: ischaemia (inferior MI classically affects the AV node — often reversible, since the right coronary artery supplies both; anterior MI affecting the septum can damage the His-Purkinje system — less reversible, worse prognosis), degenerative fibrosis of the conduction system (the commonest cause overall, age-related), drugs (beta-blockers, rate-limiting calcium-channel blockers, digoxin, amiodarone), myocarditis, infiltrative disease (sarcoidosis, amyloidosis), infective endocarditis with aortic root abscess, and cardiac surgery/TAVI.
  - **Bundle branch block**: failure of conduction down the right or left bundle branch, causing delayed depolarisation of that ventricle via slower cell-to-cell spread — widens the QRS (**≥120 ms**) without necessarily implying any block higher up at the AV node.
diagnosis: |+
  - **1st degree AV block**: asymptomatic, found incidentally on ECG — **prolonged PR interval (>200 ms)**, but every P wave is still followed by a QRS (1:1 conduction, just delayed).
  - **2nd degree, Mobitz type I (Wenckebach)**: progressive PR prolongation over successive beats until a P wave is not conducted (a "dropped beat"), then the cycle resets — usually asymptomatic or mild (occasional dizziness), reflects AV nodal disease.
  - **2nd degree, Mobitz type II**: PR interval is **constant** on conducted beats, with an unpredictable, sudden dropped beat (no preceding prolongation) — reflects infranodal disease and carries a significant risk of progressing to complete heart block; can cause syncope.
  - **2:1 AV block**: every other P wave is conducted — this pattern **cannot be classified as type I or II from a single strip**, since there's no run of conducted beats to show progressive PR prolongation or not; look at old ECGs, vary the rate (exercise/atropine), or assume the more dangerous pattern (Mobitz II-like) until proven otherwise.
  - **3rd degree (complete) heart block**: **complete dissociation between P waves and QRS complexes** — atria and ventricles beat independently, each at their own regular rate, with no fixed relationship between them. Presents with dizziness, syncope (Stokes-Adams attacks), heart failure, or sudden collapse; examination can show cannon A waves (atria contracting against a closed tricuspid valve) and a variable first heart sound intensity.
  - **Bundle branch block**: usually asymptomatic itself, found on ECG. **RBBB**: "**MaRROW**" pattern — RSR' ("M"-shaped) in V1, wide slurred S wave ("W") in V6. **LBBB**: "**WiLLiaM**" pattern — W-shaped (QS or rS) in V1, M-shaped (broad, notched R) in V6. **New LBBB** can mask or mimic the ECG changes of MI (see Investigations).
investigations: |+
  - **12-lead ECG** is the key investigation for all of the above. **Ambulatory ECG monitoring** (24–72 hour Holter, or a longer-term implantable loop recorder) is used when the suspected rhythm is intermittent and not captured on a resting ECG (e.g. intermittent high-grade block causing syncope).
  - Look for and treat a reversible cause alongside characterising the block: U&E (potassium), troponin/ECG changes of ischaemia, digoxin level if relevant, TFTs, and medication review (any AV-nodal blocking drug).
  - **New LBBB with a history of chest pain** should be treated as a likely acute MI equivalent in the right clinical context — **Sgarbossa criteria** (concordant ST elevation, concordant ST depression in V1–V3, or markedly discordant ST elevation) help identify a true acute coronary occlusion underneath a LBBB pattern, which otherwise makes standard STEMI ECG criteria unusable. See the ACS factfile (Acute medicine).
  - **Bifascicular block** (RBBB + left anterior or posterior fascicular block) and **trifascicular block** (bifascicular block + 1st degree AV block, reflecting disease in the remaining fascicle too) identify patients at higher risk of progressing to complete heart block, particularly if they also have syncope.
treatment: |+
  - **1st degree block and Mobitz I, asymptomatic**: no specific treatment needed — monitor, and review/stop any contributing drug (beta-blocker, rate-limiting calcium-channel blocker, digoxin) if appropriate.
  - **Mobitz II, 2:1 block of uncertain type, high-grade block, or complete heart block**: these need **pacing** — permanent pacemaker implantation is indicated even if currently asymptomatic, given the unpredictable risk of progression to asystole, unless there is a clearly identified and reversible cause (e.g. acute inferior MI, which often recovers within days; or a drug effect that resolves on stopping it).
  - **Bundle branch block alone**, without AV block or symptoms, does not need pacing — it is a marker to note and monitor, not an indication by itself.
  - **Symptomatic bradycardia from any high-grade block** (presyncope, syncope, haemodynamic compromise) is managed acutely per the bradycardia algorithm (see Protocols) while definitive pacing is arranged.
  - Address the underlying cause wherever identifiable (revascularise an ischaemic cause, stop/adjust a causative drug, treat infective endocarditis) — this can sometimes avoid the need for permanent pacing if the block resolves.
protocols: |+
  Acute symptomatic bradycardia (Resuscitation Council UK bradycardia algorithm)

  > Applies to any bradycardia (including high-grade AV block) causing haemodynamic compromise — shock, syncope, myocardial ischaemia, or heart failure — or with risk factors for asystole (recent asystole, Mobitz II, complete heart block with broad QRS, ventricular pauses >3 seconds).

  - **IV atropine 500 micrograms**, repeated up to a maximum of **3 mg**, as first-line treatment.
  - If atropine is ineffective or risk factors for asystole are present: **transcutaneous pacing** (external pads), with **IV isoprenaline, adrenaline infusion, or glucagon (if beta-blocker/calcium-channel blocker overdose is the cause)** as alternatives while arranging pacing, per specialist/anaesthetic advice.
  - **Transvenous temporary pacing** is used as a bridge to a permanent pacemaker in anyone with high-grade/complete heart block not expected to resolve quickly, or whose symptoms aren't controlled by the measures above.
  - **Caution with atropine in a heart transplant recipient** — the denervated heart does not respond to atropine, so alternative measures (pacing, isoprenaline) should be used directly.
