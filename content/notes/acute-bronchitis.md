background: |+
  Self-limiting inflammation of the bronchial airways, almost always **viral** (influenza, parainfluenza, RSV, coronaviruses, rhinovirus), occasionally bacterial (including atypicals such as *Mycoplasma* and *Bordetella pertussis*). It is a clinical diagnosis of exclusion against pneumonia — the key distinguishing feature is the **absence of focal chest signs and systemic illness** that would suggest consolidation.

  - Very common, especially in autumn/winter, and one of the commonest reasons for a primary care consultation about cough.
  - The cough itself results from airway inflammation and hyper-responsiveness, and classically **outlasts the rest of the illness by weeks** — a source of significant patient anxiety and repeat consultation if this natural course isn't explained up front.
diagnosis: |+
  - Cough (productive or dry), which may follow a few days of coryzal symptoms (sore throat, runny nose) and is often preceded or accompanied by **low-grade fever**, malaise, and mild wheeze or chest discomfort from the coughing itself.
  - **No focal chest signs** (no dullness, no bronchial breathing, no consistent focal crackles) and the patient is **not systemically very unwell** — these two features are what separate it from pneumonia clinically.
  - **Typical duration is about 3 weeks**, though patients are often not warned of this and re-present worried that a cough persisting at 10–14 days must mean they need antibiotics or further tests.
investigations: |+
  - **Usually a clinical diagnosis — no routine investigations needed** in an otherwise well patient with a short history and no red flags.
  - **CXR** is reserved for diagnostic uncertainty: focal chest signs, systemic illness (high fever, rigors, confusion), haemoptysis, suspected foreign body, or a cough that doesn't settle as expected (particularly to exclude pneumonia, an underlying mass, or another cause).
  - Consider **whooping cough (pertussis)** testing (PCR/serology depending on duration of symptoms) if there is a paroxysmal cough with a whoop or post-tussive vomiting, especially with a relevant exposure, in unvaccinated individuals, or during a local outbreak — it is notifiable.
treatment: |+
  - **NICE guidance (NG120) does not recommend antibiotics for most patients** — the evidence shows minimal benefit (shortening the illness by less than a day on average) against the harms of resistance and side effects, since the large majority of cases are viral or self-limiting.
  - **Self-care advice**: adequate fluids, rest, paracetamol or NSAIDs for fever/discomfort, and honey (in adults and children >1 year) can help the cough. Routine over-the-counter cough suppressants/expectorants are not strongly evidence-based but are not harmful if the patient finds them helpful.
  - **Consider an immediate antibiotic** for: systemically very unwell, symptoms/signs of a more serious illness or complication, or high risk of complications (pre-existing significant comorbidity — e.g. significant heart, lung, renal, liver or neuromuscular disease; immunosuppression; cystic fibrosis; young children born prematurely).
  - **Consider a back-up (delayed) antibiotic prescription**, to use if symptoms significantly worsen or don't start settling as expected, for patients not meeting the immediate-antibiotic criteria but where some safety-netting is wanted. **Doxycycline is first-line** where an antibiotic is indicated (avoided in children, pregnancy and breastfeeding — amoxicillin is the alternative in these groups).
  - **Safety-net clearly**: explain the expected ~3-week course, and advise return if breathless, systemically unwell, symptoms significantly worsen, or the cough has not started improving after 3–4 weeks.
protocols: |+
  > Red flag: haemoptysis, significant breathlessness, focal chest signs, a high fever with systemic upset, or a cough persisting beyond 3–4 weeks (particularly in a smoker or someone ≥40) should prompt reassessment for pneumonia, lung cancer or another diagnosis rather than being treated as prolonged acute bronchitis — see the suspected lung cancer referral criteria (Lung cancer card) and the Lower respiratory tract infections card.
  - Suspected whooping cough is a notifiable disease — inform the local health protection team, and consider chemoprophylaxis/vaccination advice for close contacts per national guidance, particularly where there's contact with unvaccinated infants.
