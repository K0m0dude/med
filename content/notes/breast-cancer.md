background: >+
  - Breast cancer is the most common cancer in the UK (**15%** of all new cancer
  cases; **~56,000–59,000** new cases/yr in women, ~400 in men; ♀:♂ ≈ 99:1).
  Lifetime risk ≈**1 in 7** women. Incidence has risen ~**24%** since the 1990s.
  A quarter of cases are diagnosed at age ≥75.


  Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS)


  - Malignant cells confined within the duct, no invasion ∴ no metastatic
  potential itself, but a precursor that can progress if untreated. Usually
  found on screening (microcalcification). Graded low/intermediate/high;
  high-grade carries greater recurrence risk.


  Lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS)


  - A marker of increased future risk in either breast rather than a direct
  precursor like DCIS. Usually incidental. Pleomorphic LCIS is the exception,
  managed more like DCIS.

  - **Invasive ductal carcinoma, no special type (~70–80%)** – most common
  invasive subtype, forms a firm irregular mass.

  - **Invasive lobular carcinoma (~10%)** – loss of E-cadherin → single-file
  infiltration ∴ can be subtle clinically/on mammography (diffuse thickening
  rather than a discrete lump), more often bilateral/multifocal.


  Inflammatory breast cancer


  - Rare (1–5%) but aggressive – dermal lymphatic tumour emboli → rapid-onset
  erythema, warmth, oedema, peau d'orange over ≥⅓ of the breast, often
  **without** a discrete lump. Mimics mastitis but doesn't settle with
  antibiotics.


  > Red flag: "mastitis" in a non-lactating woman, or any mastitis not settling
  within ~2 weeks of antibiotics, needs urgent breast clinic referral to exclude
  inflammatory breast cancer.


  Paget's disease of the nipple


  - Malignant cells in the nipple epidermis, usually with underlying DCIS or
  invasive cancer. Unilateral eczematous nipple-areola change (erythema,
  scaling, itch, ± destruction) – often mistaken for dermatitis.


  > Red flag: unilateral, persistent nipple eczema that doesn't respond to
  topical treatment is Paget's disease until proven otherwise.


  Receptor subtypes (drive prognosis and treatment choice)


  - **ER/PR-positive (luminal) ~70–80%** – oestrogen-driven, generally
  slower-growing, responds to endocrine therapy; relapse risk persists for
  >10–20 yrs (late relapse).

  - **HER2-positive ~15–20%** (can overlap with ER+) – *ERBB2* amplification
  drives aggressive growth but is targetable, which has transformed prognosis.

  - **Triple negative (ER–/PR–/HER2–) ~10–15%** – no targetable receptor, relies
  on chemotherapy; younger patients, higher grade, over-represented in BRCA1
  carriers; higher early relapse risk but fewer late relapses than ER+ disease.


  Risk factors


  - Most breast cancer is **not** due to single high-penetrance gene mutations –
  these account for only **5–10%** of all cases (BRCA1/2 carriers ~2%;
  population prevalence of BRCA1/2 mutations only ~0.25–0.5%).

  - Unmodifiable RFs: female sex, increasing age, early menarche/late menopause,
  nulliparity or late first pregnancy, not breastfeeding, dense breast tissue,
  previous breast cancer/DCIS/LCIS, previous chest-wall radiotherapy, family
  history.

  - Modifiable – only ~**23–30%** of breast cancers are thought preventable by
  lifestyle change overall; the two biggest contributors are **obesity**
  (post-menopausal, ~8%) and **alcohol** (~8%). Combined HRT and the combined
  pill give a small, reversible increase in risk. **Smoking** has only a
  **minor** effect on breast cancer risk specifically (unlike most other
  cancers) – don't overstate it here.


  > Screening detects cancer earlier; it does not prevent it, so it doesn't
  belong on a list of preventive measures.


  Genetics / familial risk ([NICE
  CG164](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg164))


  - BRCA1/BRCA2 (lifetime risk up to ~60–85% in carriers), also TP53
  (Li-Fraumeni), PALB2, PTEN (Cowden), STK11 (Peutz-Jeghers), CDH1 (lobular).

  - CG164 risk bands by lifetime risk from age 20: **near-population** (<17%,
  managed in primary care), **moderate** (17–29%), **high** (≥30%, or a known
  pathogenic variant carrier) – moderate/high risk is referred to a family
  history/genetics clinic for surveillance (earlier/annual mammography ± MRI)
  and consideration of risk-reducing endocrine therapy or surgery.


  Metastatic spread


  - Local: skin, chest wall. Lymphatic: axillary → internal
  mammary/supraclavicular nodes. Haematogenous: **bone > lung/pleura > liver >
  brain**.


  Male breast cancer


  - Rare (<1% of all breast cancers), almost always ER-positive, stronger BRCA2
  association; often presents later/more advanced due to delayed recognition.

diagnosis: |+
  #### Diagnosis and clinical signs

  - Most common presentation: a **painless**, **unilateral**, discrete lump. Bilateral symptoms, pain alone, and pain in younger women are far more often benign (fibroadenoma, cyclical mastalgia, cysts) – but don't dismiss a palpable abnormality in an older woman just because there's pain.
  - Other presentations: unilateral blood-stained/single-duct nipple discharge, nipple retraction/inversion, skin change (peau d'orange, dimpling/tethering, ulceration), axillary lump, Paget's disease, inflammatory change, or an asymptomatic finding on screening.

  NICE NG12: suspected cancer referral


  | Referral | Criteria |
  | --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
  | Urgent (2WW) | Age ≥30 with an unexplained breast lump (with or without pain) |
  | Urgent (2WW) | Age ≥50 with unilateral nipple discharge, retraction, or other change of concern |
  | Consider urgent (2WW) | Any age with skin changes suggestive of breast cancer |
  | Consider urgent (2WW) | Age ≥30 with an unexplained axillary lump |
  | Non-urgent | Age <30 with an unexplained breast lump (with or without pain) |


  - A recent negative mammogram does **not** override these criteria – refer a symptomatic woman regardless of a recent "normal" screen.

  NHS Breast Screening Programme

  - Women invited from around their **50th to 71st** birthday for **3-yearly** mammography (an ongoing AgeX trial is assessing extending this to 47–73). Women >71 aren't routinely recalled but can self-refer.
  - Mammographic sensitivity isn't 100% – roughly **10%** of breast cancers aren't visible on mammography (interval cancers).
  - Most women recalled after screening do **not** have cancer – only about **20–25%** of recalls are confirmed malignant.

  > The programme is estimated to prevent around **1,300** breast cancer deaths/yr in the UK, at the cost of over-diagnosing and treating around **4,000** cases/yr that would never have caused a problem – a trade-off worth understanding, not a reason to dismiss screening.

  Triple assessment (one-stop breast clinic)

  - **Clinical** assessment + **imaging** (mammography and/or ultrasound – ultrasound preferred under ~35–40 due to dense tissue) + **core biopsy** (preferred over FNA, since it gives histology and receptor status) – ideally in a single visit.

investigations: >+
  #### Investigations


  - **ER, PR and HER2** status assessed on every invasive cancer (and DCIS) at
  diagnosis – by immunohistochemistry, with FISH/ISH for HER2 if IHC is
  equivocal.

  - **Histological grade** – Nottingham (Elston-Ellis modified
  Bloom-Richardson): tubule formation, nuclear pleomorphism and mitotic count
  each scored 1–3, summed out of 9. **Grade I = 3–5, Grade II = 6–7, Grade III =
  8–9** (most tumours are grade II).

  - **TNM staging** – T (size/invasion, including Tis for in-situ disease), N
  (assessed by **sentinel lymph node biopsy** if clinically/radiologically
  node-negative), M (distant metastases). NG101: don't routinely stage with CT
  or isotope bone scan in early, low-risk disease – reserve for larger tumours,
  heavier nodal burden, locally advanced disease, or symptoms of metastases.

  - Breast **MRI** isn't routine pre-operatively, but is offered for invasive
  lobular cancer being considered for breast-conserving surgery, a discrepancy
  between imaging and what's needed to plan surgery, or breast density
  precluding assessment.

  - **Nottingham Prognostic Index (NPI)** = (0.2 × tumour size in cm) + nodal
  stage score (1–3) + grade (1–3); lower scores = better prognosis, commonly
  banded excellent/good/moderate/poor.

  - **Gene expression profiling** (EndoPredict, Oncotype DX or Prosigna – [NICE
  HTG719](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/htg719)) can be used in
  ER/PR-positive, HER2-negative, node-negative (or 1–3 node-positive,
  postmenopausal) early disease at intermediate clinical risk, to help decide
  whether chemotherapy is likely to add benefit beyond endocrine therapy alone.


  Suspected relapse/metastatic disease


  - Presentation depends on the site(s) involved: **bone** (pain, fracture,
  hypercalcaemia), **lung/pleura** (breathlessness, cough), **liver** (anorexia,
  weight loss, deranged LFTs), **brain** (headache, seizures, focal neurology,
  personality change), or non-specific (fatigue, weight loss, night sweats).
  Investigate with directed imaging and biopsy of an accessible site where
  feasible – receptor status can change at relapse and alters treatment.

treatment: >+
  #### Treatment


  Early and locally advanced (operable) disease – [NICE
  NG101](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng101)


  - Managed by the breast **MDT**. Every patient should have a named CNS/key
  worker and prompt access to psychological support.

  - **Surgery**: breast-conserving surgery + adjuvant whole-breast radiotherapy,
  **or** mastectomy ± reconstruction – equivalent survival when margins are
  clear.

  - **Axilla**: sentinel node biopsy if node-negative; axillary clearance or
  radiotherapy if nodes are involved – carries a real lymphoedema risk.

  - **Radiotherapy**: whole-breast RT after conserving surgery cuts local
  recurrence substantially; post-mastectomy RT for higher-risk disease.

  - **Neoadjuvant** chemotherapy ± HER2-targeted therapy for larger, HER2+ or
  triple-negative tumours, to downstage and assess in-vivo response.


  > Adjuvant decisions balance baseline relapse risk against the risks of a
  given treatment. A treatment that cuts relative risk by 40% makes only a
  **2%** absolute difference to someone with a 5% baseline risk, but a **24%**
  absolute difference to someone with a 60% baseline risk – same relative
  benefit, very different absolute benefit.


  - **Endocrine therapy** (ER+): **tamoxifen** (pre-/peri-menopausal) or an
  **aromatase inhibitor** (post-menopausal only). Ovarian suppression ± an AI
  for higher-risk pre-menopausal disease. Duration **5–10 years**.

  - **Chemotherapy**: triple-negative, HER2+, high-grade/node-positive, or
  genomically higher-risk ER+ disease.

  - **HER2-targeted therapy**: **trastuzumab** (± pertuzumab), usually **1
  year** – cardiotoxic, needs regular LVEF monitoring.

  - **Bone-protective therapy**: adjuvant bisphosphonate for post-menopausal
  women with node-positive or high-risk node-negative disease.

  - **Outcomes**: 10-year survival is now around **75%** – this includes people
  already relapsed on palliative treatment, plus some relapsing **>10–20 years**
  later (especially ER+ disease).


  > Surgical excision with clear margins is **not** "sufficient" alone for most
  invasive cancers – systemic adjuvant treatment (where indicated) is what cuts
  the subsequent risk of relapse and death.


  Advanced (locally advanced/unresectable or metastatic) disease – [NICE
  CG81](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/cg81)


  - **Metastatic breast cancer is incurable but very treatable** – treatment is
  palliative, balancing quality **and** quantity of life.

  - As a systemic disease, systemic treatment is the mainstay; local treatment
  (surgery/RT) is reserved for a specific problem at one site (cord compression,
  a painful bone met, brain mets) rather than to change overall prognosis.

  - **ER+**: endocrine therapy first-line, usually with a **CDK4/6 inhibitor**
  (palbociclib/ribociclib/abemaciclib).

  - **HER2+**: continued anti-HER2 therapy, then antibody-drug conjugates in
  later lines.

  - **Triple-negative**: chemotherapy mainstay; immunotherapy in PD-L1-positive
  disease; antibody-drug conjugates later.

  - Usually given as **sequential single agents** rather than intensive
  combinations – combinations haven't shown a clear survival benefit but do
  cause more toxicity.

  - **Outcomes**: median survival often quoted as ~**2 years**, range weeks to
  >20 years – over **20%** survive beyond 5 years.

protocols: >+
  > #### Emergency protocols and red flags

  >

  > - Most acute oncological emergencies here are generic to solid tumours – see
  the **Cancer and oncology emergencies** factfile (Acute medicine) for full
  management of neutropenic sepsis, MSCC, hypercalcaemia, SVCO and tumour lysis.
  The points below are breast-specific flags.

  > - **Hypercalcaemia** is relatively common with bone-dominant metastatic
  disease – check corrected calcium in anyone with known bone mets and new
  non-specific symptoms.

  > - **Metastatic spinal cord compression** – new back pain in anyone with a
  breast cancer history is spinal metastasis until proven otherwise; any limb
  weakness, sensory level or sphincter disturbance needs an urgent whole-spine
  MRI and dexamethasone.

  > - **Brain metastases** – new headache, seizure, focal neurology or
  personality change needs urgent CT/MRI head; more common with HER2+ and
  triple-negative disease.

  > - **Neutropenic sepsis** – fever during/after chemotherapy needs antibiotics
  within 1 hour; don't wait for the neutrophil count.

  > - **Trastuzumab cardiotoxicity** – baseline and 3-monthly LVEF monitoring.

  >

  > > Red flag: inflammatory breast cancer and Paget's disease are both
  frequently mistaken for benign/infective skin conditions – treatment that
  doesn't settle needs an urgent breast clinic referral, not a repeat course of
  the same thing.
