background: |+
  Rib fractures are common after blunt chest trauma (falls, road traffic collisions, assaults) and also occur spontaneously in osteoporotic bone or from severe coughing. The injury itself matters less than what it signals and what it causes.

  - **The real danger is pain, not the fracture itself.** Pain splints the chest wall, so the patient breathes shallowly and can't cough effectively — this leads to basal atelectasis, sputum retention and pneumonia, especially in older patients. This is the dominant cause of morbidity and mortality, more so than any direct lung injury.
  - **Flail chest**: ≥3 consecutive ribs each fractured in ≥2 places, creating a free-floating segment that moves paradoxically (inward on inspiration, outward on expiration). Usually implies a high-energy injury and a significant underlying **pulmonary contusion**, which is often the main driver of respiratory failure rather than the flail segment's mechanics alone.
  - **Site matters**: lower rib fractures (9–12) raise suspicion of splenic (left) or hepatic (right) injury; upper rib fractures (1–3) imply a very high-energy mechanism and raise concern for great vessel or brachial plexus injury.
  - **Underlying injuries to always consider**: pneumothorax, haemothorax (see that card), pulmonary contusion, and — with sternal or multiple rib fractures — myocardial contusion.
diagnosis: |+
  - Localised chest wall pain, worse on inspiration, coughing or movement; point tenderness and bony crepitus over the fracture site; bruising. Reduced chest expansion and shallow breathing from pain (splinting).
  - **Flail segment**: visible paradoxical chest wall movement — can be subtle early on, before fatigue sets in, so watch for it rather than assuming it will be obvious.
  - Examine for associated injuries: surgical emphysema, reduced air entry or hyperresonance (pneumothorax), dullness (haemothorax), abdominal tenderness (splenic/hepatic injury with lower rib fractures).
  - In **older patients, even a single rib fracture is a marker of significant frailty-related risk** — mortality and pneumonia risk rise steeply with the number of fractures and with age, out of proportion to how the injury might look in a younger patient.
investigations: |+
  - **CXR** is the initial investigation — looks for pneumothorax, haemothorax, and widened mediastinum (aortic injury) — but **can miss a substantial proportion of rib fractures**, especially non-displaced ones; the clinical findings matter more than a negative film.
  - **CT chest** is more sensitive for rib fractures and for associated injuries (pulmonary contusion, occult pneumothorax, vascular injury) and is used in higher-energy trauma or when the CXR doesn't explain the clinical picture.
  - **ECG and troponin** if sternal fracture, multiple rib fractures, or any chest wall injury with a plausible mechanism for cardiac contusion.
  - **Incidental spirometry/bedside observations** are not routine, but **regular respiratory rate, SpO₂ and a simple pain score** should be monitored closely, since deterioration is usually from progressive atelectasis/pneumonia rather than the fracture itself.
treatment: |+
  - **Analgesia is the cornerstone of treatment** — inadequate analgesia is the single most preventable cause of a bad outcome. Use a multimodal approach: regular paracetamol, NSAIDs if not contraindicated, and opioids as needed — but opioids themselves suppress respiratory drive and cough, so balance is needed.
  - **Regional techniques** (intercostal nerve blocks, paravertebral block, or an epidural) are used for multiple fractures or flail chest where systemic analgesia alone isn't controlling pain enough to allow effective coughing and deep breathing — involve acute pain/anaesthetic teams early in this group.
  - **Respiratory physiotherapy**: incentive spirometry, breathing exercises and assisted coughing to prevent atelectasis and sputum retention — at least as important as the analgesia itself, and the two work together (good analgesia enables effective physiotherapy).
  - **Flail chest**: supportive management is now preferred over automatically ventilating for the flail segment itself — good analgesia, physiotherapy, and treating the pulmonary contusion. **Non-invasive ventilation** is used where respiratory failure develops but intubation can be avoided; **invasive ventilation** is reserved for more severe respiratory failure, often driven by the contusion rather than the flail mechanics. Rarely, **surgical fixation** of the ribs is considered for severe flail chest, especially if thoracotomy is needed for another reason.
  - Most isolated rib fractures without complication are managed as an outpatient with analgesia and safety-netting advice; admission is for inadequate pain control, hypoxia, multiple fractures, flail chest, significant comorbidity, or older/frail patients even with few fractures, given their disproportionate pneumonia risk.
protocols: |+
  > Red flag: in an older or frail patient, even a seemingly minor rib fracture count can carry a serious risk of pneumonia and death — have a low threshold to admit for analgesia optimisation and physiotherapy rather than relying on fracture number alone to judge severity.

  - Tension pneumothorax from an underlying lung injury is a clinical diagnosis needing immediate needle decompression — don't wait for imaging (see the Pleural disease factfile, Acute medicine).
  - Deteriorating oxygenation or respiratory rate after rib trauma should prompt reassessment for a missed pneumothorax/haemothorax, worsening pulmonary contusion, or evolving atelectasis/pneumonia from inadequate analgesia — not simply an increase in opioid dose without addressing the underlying cause.
  - Suspected aortic or great vessel injury (upper rib fractures, widened mediastinum, high-energy mechanism) needs urgent CT angiography and trauma team/cardiothoracic involvement.
