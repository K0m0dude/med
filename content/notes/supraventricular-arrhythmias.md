background: |+
  Arrhythmias arising at or above the AV node — the atria, the AV node itself, or a re-entry circuit using the AV node.

  - **Atrial ectopics (premature atrial contractions)**: extremely common, usually benign, arising from an ectopic atrial focus firing early — felt as an occasional "skipped" or extra beat. Rarely need treatment beyond reassurance and addressing triggers (caffeine, alcohol, stress, stimulants).
  - **Atrial fibrillation (AF)**: chaotic, disorganised atrial electrical activity with no coordinated atrial contraction, conducted irregularly through the AV node to the ventricles — the commonest sustained arrhythmia, affecting **2–3% of the UK population**, rising sharply with age. Causes/associations: hypertension, ischaemic and valvular heart disease, heart failure, hyperthyroidism, sepsis/acute illness, alcohol ("holiday heart"), obesity, and obstructive sleep apnoea.
  - **Atrial flutter**: a single, organised macro-re-entry circuit (typically around the tricuspid valve annulus — "typical" flutter) causing a rapid, regular atrial rate (**~300/min**), usually conducted to the ventricles with a fixed block ratio (commonly **2:1, giving a ventricular rate of ~150/min**) — shares the same stroke risk and anticoagulation approach as AF.
  - **AV nodal re-entry tachycardia (AVNRT)**: a re-entry circuit confined to the AV node itself (using "fast" and "slow" pathways within the node) — the commonest cause of paroxysmal "SVT" in people with a structurally normal heart, typically young and otherwise well.
  - **AV re-entry tachycardia (AVRT)**: a re-entry circuit using the AV node plus an accessory pathway outside it (e.g. in Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome — see that card for the specific pre-excitation pattern and precautions).
diagnosis: |+
  - **AF**: irregularly irregular pulse, often with a **pulse deficit** (apical rate faster than the radial rate, since not every contraction generates a palpable peripheral pulse) — can be asymptomatic (found incidentally) or cause palpitations, breathlessness, fatigue, dizziness, or decompensate existing heart failure. **Classified as paroxysmal** (self-terminating, usually within 48 hours), **persistent** (lasting beyond 7 days, or needing cardioversion to terminate), or **permanent** (an ongoing, accepted rhythm with a rate-control-only strategy).
  - **Atrial flutter**: often a regular tachycardia around 150/min — can be harder to recognise clinically than AF since the pulse is regular; think of flutter specifically in any unexplained narrow-complex tachycardia around this rate.
  - **AVNRT/AVRT ("paroxysmal SVT")**: sudden-onset, sudden-offset palpitations, often rapid (commonly 140–220/min) and regular, sometimes with dizziness, breathlessness or chest discomfort — typically in a younger patient, often with no structural heart disease.
investigations: |+
  - **12-lead ECG** is the key test in all cases. **AF**: absent P waves, irregularly irregular narrow-complex rhythm. **Atrial flutter**: regular "sawtooth" flutter waves (best seen in the inferior leads, II/III/aVF), typically at ~300/min, with a regular ventricular response if the block ratio is fixed. **AVNRT**: a regular narrow-complex tachycardia, often with no visible P waves (buried within the QRS) or a P wave immediately after the QRS ("pseudo-R' " in V1). **AVRT**: similar, though a delta wave may be visible on the resting ECG between episodes if pre-excitation is present (see the WPW card).
  - **Ambulatory ECG monitoring** (Holter, event recorder, or an implantable loop recorder) if episodes are paroxysmal/intermittent and not captured on a resting ECG.
  - **Assess and treat reversible precipitants**: TFTs (thyrotoxicosis), U&E, FBC/CRP (sepsis), echocardiogram (structural heart disease, valve disease, left atrial size), and alcohol history.
  - **Stroke risk**: the **CHA2DS2-VASc score** (Congestive heart failure, Hypertension, Age ≥75 [2 points], Diabetes, prior Stroke/TIA/thromboembolism [2 points], Vascular disease, Age 65–74, Sex category female) is used to decide on anticoagulation in **AF and atrial flutter alike**. **Bleeding risk** (e.g. the ORBIT score) is assessed alongside, to inform (not simply override) the anticoagulation decision.
treatment: |+
  Atrial fibrillation (NICE NG196)

  - **Anticoagulation**: offer if **CHA2DS2-VASc ≥2**; consider in men scoring **1** (sex category alone does not justify anticoagulation in women with a score of 0 otherwise) — taking bleeding risk into account. **A DOAC is preferred over warfarin** for most patients. **Aspirin monotherapy is not recommended purely for stroke prevention in AF.**
  - **Rate control is first-line for most patients**: a **standard beta-blocker** (not sotalol) or a **rate-limiting calcium-channel blocker** (diltiazem/verapamil); **digoxin** is reserved for the largely sedentary, or as an add-on. Combination therapy (two of beta-blocker/diltiazem/digoxin) is used if a single agent doesn't control the rate.
  - **Rhythm control is preferred instead when**: the AF is **new-onset**, has a **reversible cause**, there is **heart failure thought to be primarily caused by the AF**, or **symptoms persist despite adequate rate control**.
  - **Cardioversion**: if AF has been present **<48 hours**, cardioversion (electrical or pharmacological — flecainide or amiodarone, avoiding flecainide/propafenone if structural or ischaemic heart disease is present) can proceed without a 3-week anticoagulation lead-in, though anticoagulation should still be considered around the procedure. If **>48 hours or uncertain**, anticoagulate and rate-control first, then either anticoagulate for **≥3 weeks before elective cardioversion**, or perform **TOE-guided cardioversion** to exclude left atrial thrombus and proceed sooner — anticoagulation is continued for at least **4 weeks after cardioversion** regardless of method, since atrial mechanical function (and stroke risk) lags behind the return of a normal rhythm.
  - **Catheter (left atrial) ablation** is offered when drug treatment has failed or is unsuitable, particularly effective for paroxysmal AF.

  Atrial flutter

  - Managed similarly to AF for **anticoagulation** (same CHA2DS2-VASc-based approach) and **rate control**, but **catheter ablation of the cavotricuspid isthmus is highly effective (curative in the large majority) for typical flutter** and is often offered earlier/preferentially over long-term drug therapy, given this high success rate.

  AVNRT/AVRT (acute termination)

  - **Vagal manoeuvres** first (Valsalva manoeuvre — ideally the modified, "legs-raised" technique, which improves success; carotid sinus massage after excluding a carotid bruit).
  - **IV adenosine** (rapid bolus, e.g. 6 mg then 12 mg then a further 12 mg if needed, via a large-bore cannula with a rapid saline flush) if vagal manoeuvres fail — transiently blocks the AV node, terminating most AVNRT/AVRT. Warn the patient about a brief unpleasant sensation (flushing, chest tightness, a feeling of impending doom) as expected and short-lived. **Caution/avoid in asthma** (can cause bronchospasm) — verapamil is an alternative in this context.
  - **Catheter ablation** is the definitive, usually curative treatment for recurrent AVNRT/AVRT, avoiding the need for long-term antiarrhythmic drugs.
protocols: |+
  > Red flag: **any haemodynamically unstable tachycardia** (signs of shock, syncope, myocardial ischaemia, or severe heart failure) — whatever the specific rhythm — needs **immediate synchronised DC cardioversion** per the Resuscitation Council UK tachycardia algorithm, not a trial of drug therapy first.

  - **New fast AF/flutter in acute illness** (sepsis, post-operative, acute MI) is often a response to the underlying illness rather than the primary problem — treat the underlying cause alongside rate control, and reassess the rhythm as the patient improves, rather than committing immediately to long-term rhythm-control strategies.
  - **AF/flutter in the presence of ventricular pre-excitation (WPW)**: avoid AV-nodal blocking drugs (adenosine, verapamil, digoxin, and beta-blockers in this specific context) — see the WPW card for why, and for the alternative approach.
  - See the Heart failure, Cardiogenic shock, and WPW cards for related complications and special situations.
