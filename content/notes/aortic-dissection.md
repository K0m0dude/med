background: |+
  A tear in the aortic intima allows blood to track into the media, creating a false lumen that can propagate along the aorta, occlude branch vessels, and rupture. **Hypertension is the single most important risk factor**, present in 70–80% of cases. Other risk factors: connective tissue disease (Marfan's, Ehlers-Danlos), bicuspid aortic valve, aortic coarctation, pregnancy, cocaine use, vasculitis (giant cell arteritis, Takayasu's), prior cardiac surgery/aortic instrumentation, and blunt chest trauma.

  - **Stanford classification** (the one used clinically): **Type A** involves the ascending aorta (regardless of where the original tear is) — **60–70%** of cases; **Type B** is confined to the descending aorta, distal to the left subclavian artery origin. The DeBakey classification subdivides further but adds little clinically.
  - **Why the split matters**: Type A can occlude the coronary ostia (causing an inferior MI pattern), disrupt the aortic valve (acute regurgitation), or rupture into the pericardium (**tamponade** — the leading cause of death in Type A), so it is a **surgical emergency**. Type B more often threatens distal branch vessels (renal, mesenteric, spinal, limb) causing malperfusion syndromes, and is usually managed medically unless complicated.
  - Untreated proximal (Type A) dissection carries a mortality of roughly **1–2% per hour** in the first 24–48 hours — among the most time-critical diagnoses in medicine.
diagnosis: |+
  - **Sudden-onset, severe, "tearing" chest and/or back pain**, often maximal at onset (unlike the crescendo pattern of ACS), which can migrate as the dissection propagates (e.g. chest to back to abdomen).
  - **Inter-arm blood pressure difference (>20 mmHg)** and/or a **pulse deficit** (asymmetric or absent peripheral pulses) — classic but not universally present; their absence does not exclude dissection.
  - **New early diastolic murmur of aortic regurgitation** — suggests Type A with valve involvement. **Muffled heart sounds and raised JVP** suggest tamponade (see that card). **Focal neurological deficit** can occur from carotid involvement (stroke-like presentation) or spinal artery occlusion (paraplegia). Abdominal pain and lower limb ischaemia suggest mesenteric/limb malperfusion, more typical of Type B.
  - Always consider dissection in chest pain with **atypical features for ACS**, a disproportionately severe presentation, or neurological/vascular signs alongside the pain — missing it while treating for presumed ACS (and giving antiplatelets/anticoagulants) can be catastrophic.
investigations: |+
  - **ECG**: often normal, or non-specific changes; can show an **inferior STEMI pattern** if the dissection occludes a coronary ostium (usually the right coronary) — a trap for diagnosing isolated ACS without considering the underlying dissection. Helps exclude concurrent ischaemia, but a normal ECG does not exclude dissection.
  - **CXR**: a **widened mediastinum** is suggestive but neither sensitive nor specific enough to rule the diagnosis in or out alone.
  - **CT angiography (chest, abdomen and pelvis)** is the definitive investigation in a stable patient — confirms the diagnosis, defines the Stanford type, extent, and branch vessel involvement, and guides surgical/endovascular planning. **Transoesophageal echocardiography** is the investigation of choice if the patient is too unstable for CT, since it can be done at the bedside and also assesses the aortic valve and for tamponade.
  - **D-dimer**: a level below the standard threshold makes dissection unlikely and can support ruling it out in a low pre-test-probability patient, but should not be relied upon alone when clinical suspicion is high.
treatment: |+
  - **Immediate measures regardless of type**: IV access, analgesia (opioids — pain itself drives a sympathetic response that raises BP further), and **aggressive heart rate and blood pressure control** — **IV beta-blocker first-line** (e.g. labetalol or esmolol), targeting **heart rate 60–80 bpm and systolic BP 100–120 mmHg**. If beta-blockade is contraindicated, a rate-limiting calcium-channel blocker (diltiazem/verapamil) is used instead. **Never start a vasodilator (GTN/sodium nitroprusside) before rate control is established** — vasodilation alone causes reflex tachycardia, increasing the shear stress on the dissection flap.
  - **Type A**: **emergency surgery** — replacement of the affected ascending aorta (± arch) with a graft, with aortic valve repair or replacement if it is disrupted, and coronary reimplantation if the ostia are involved. Mortality without surgery is very high (climbing further each hour), so surgical referral should not be delayed by further non-essential investigation once the diagnosis is clear.
  - **Type B, uncomplicated** (no malperfusion, rupture, refractory pain or uncontrollable hypertension): **medical management** — BP/HR control as above is the mainstay, with long-term surveillance imaging, since most do not need surgery acutely.
  - **Type B, complicated** (visceral/limb malperfusion, rupture, refractory pain, or BP that cannot be controlled): **endovascular repair (TEVAR, thoracic endovascular aortic repair)** is now preferred over open surgery where feasible.
  - All patients need long-term BP control and surveillance imaging (echo/CT/MRI) after the acute event, given the ongoing risk of aneurysmal expansion, recurrence, or late complications even in medically-managed Type B disease.
protocols: |+
  > Red flag: treat "tearing chest pain radiating to the back, with a blood pressure difference between the arms" as aortic dissection until proven otherwise — do not give antiplatelets, anticoagulants or thrombolysis (as might be reflexively considered for ACS or PE) until dissection has been excluded, since these can be catastrophic if the diagnosis is actually dissection.

  - **Cardiac tamponade from haemopericardium** (Type A) is the leading cause of death — if suspected (hypotension, raised JVP, muffled heart sounds), see the Cardiac tamponade card; needle pericardiocentesis is used cautiously and only as a bridge to emergency surgery here, since it can temporarily worsen bleeding into the pericardium by relieving the tamponade physiology that was limiting it.
  - **Acute severe aortic regurgitation** with new heart failure needs urgent cardiology/cardiothoracic input alongside the dissection repair itself.
  - **Malperfusion syndromes** (acute limb ischaemia, mesenteric ischaemia, paraplegia from spinal artery occlusion, anuria from renal involvement) in Type B dissection should prompt urgent reclassification as "complicated" and escalation to endovascular/surgical referral rather than continued medical management alone.
