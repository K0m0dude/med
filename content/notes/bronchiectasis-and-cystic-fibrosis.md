background: |+
  Permanent, abnormal dilatation of the bronchi, from a cycle of infection, inflammation and impaired mucociliary clearance that becomes self-perpetuating (the "vicious cycle/vortex" of bronchiectasis) — thick, poorly-cleared secretions become infected, driving further airway inflammation and damage.

  - **Causes (non-CF)**: post-infective (severe childhood pneumonia, measles, pertussis, TB), immunodeficiency (primary antibody deficiency, HIV), allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA, a hypersensitivity reaction to *Aspergillus* in asthmatics), primary ciliary dyskinesia (also causes infertility and, in about half, situs inversus — Kartagener syndrome), rheumatoid arthritis and other connective tissue disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and idiopathic (no cause found in a substantial proportion despite full work-up).
  - **Cystic fibrosis (CF)**: an autosomal recessive disorder from mutations in the **CFTR gene** (chromosome 7), impairing chloride channel function — this produces thick, dehydrated secretions across multiple organ systems, not just the lungs. **F508del is the commonest mutation** in the UK. CF causes bronchiectasis as one part of a multisystem disease; **not all bronchiectasis is CF**, and CF should be actively tested for in anyone presenting with bronchiectasis without an obvious alternative cause, especially if young.
  - **Chronic infecting organisms**: *Haemophilus influenzae* and *Streptococcus pneumoniae* are common; **chronic *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* infection** is a marker of more severe disease, faster decline and worse prognosis in both CF and non-CF bronchiectasis, and changes antibiotic choice significantly.
diagnosis: |+
  - **Chronic productive cough** with large volumes of purulent sputum, recurrent chest infections/exacerbations, and **haemoptysis** (sometimes significant — from hypertrophied, friable bronchial arteries) are the hallmark features. Breathlessness and wheeze occur as disease progresses.
  - Examination: coarse crackles (often change or clear partially with coughing), occasionally wheeze, and **finger clubbing** in more severe/longstanding disease.
  - **CF-specific features beyond the chest**: pancreatic insufficiency (steatorrhoea, failure to thrive, fat-soluble vitamin deficiency), meconium ileus in neonates, CF-related diabetes (increasingly common with age), male infertility (congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens), and recurrent sinusitis/nasal polyps.
  - CF is identified by **newborn screening** in the UK (heel-prick immunoreactive trypsinogen, followed by genetic testing) — most patients are now diagnosed in infancy rather than presenting later with chest symptoms, though a minority (milder CFTR variants) can present in adulthood.
investigations: |+
  - **HRCT chest** is the key diagnostic test — a **bronchoarterial ratio >1** (airway wider than its accompanying pulmonary artery), lack of airway tapering, and airways visible near the pleural surface confirm bronchiectasis and show its distribution (upper lobe predominance points towards CF or ABPA; localised disease points towards a focal post-obstructive cause).
  - **Routine work-up for a new bronchiectasis diagnosis** (BTS guideline): sputum culture, FBC, immunoglobulins (antibody deficiency), *Aspergillus* IgE/skin test and total IgE (ABPA), and — in everyone, not only those who seem young or atypical — **testing for cystic fibrosis** (sweat chloride ± CFTR genetics). Add rheumatoid factor/ANA/ANCA, ciliary studies (if primary ciliary dyskinesia is suspected from the clinical pattern), and HIV testing where clinically indicated.
  - **Sweat chloride test**: **<30 mmol/L is normal**, **30–60 mmol/L is borderline/indeterminate** (needs further genetic evaluation), **>60 mmol/L supports a CF diagnosis** — interpreted alongside CFTR genotyping.
  - **Severity assessment**: the **Bronchiectasis Severity Index** combines age, BMI, FEV1%, hospital admissions, exacerbation frequency, dyspnoea score, *Pseudomonas* colonisation and radiological extent to predict future risk and guide how intensively to manage someone.
treatment: |+
  - **Airway clearance techniques** (taught by a respiratory physiotherapist — active cycle of breathing techniques, postural drainage, oscillating positive expiratory pressure devices) are the cornerstone of treatment for everyone with bronchiectasis, done regularly, not just during exacerbations.
  - **Pulmonary rehabilitation** for those with significant breathlessness/exercise limitation, as in COPD.
  - **Long-term macrolide therapy** (e.g. azithromycin three times weekly) reduces exacerbation frequency in patients with frequent exacerbations (BTS guidance: typically considered at ≥3 exacerbations/year needing antibiotics) — check baseline ECG (QT interval), LFTs, and exclude non-tuberculous mycobacterial infection first (macrolide monotherapy risks inducing resistance in undiagnosed NTM disease).
  - **Exacerbations**: a **prolonged antibiotic course (typically around 14 days)**, guided by previous sputum culture results where available, is standard — longer than the courses used for simple pneumonia, reflecting the difficulty of fully clearing infection from damaged airways.
  - **Mucoactive agents** (e.g. nebulised hypertonic saline or carbocisteine) can help sputum clearance in selected patients, used alongside, not instead of, physiotherapy.
  - **Significant haemoptysis**: see Protocols.
  - **Cystic fibrosis-specific treatment**: **CFTR modulator therapy** (e.g. elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor, for eligible genotypes including F508del) has transformed outlook for many patients — improving lung function, reducing exacerbations and improving nutritional status, by partially correcting the underlying protein defect rather than just treating its consequences. **Pancreatic enzyme replacement** (with meals, for exocrine insufficiency), fat-soluble vitamin supplementation, high-calorie nutritional support, and proactive management of **CF-related diabetes** are essential alongside airway-focused treatment. CF care is delivered through specialist multidisciplinary CF centres.
  - Treat the underlying cause where identifiable and treatable (immunoglobulin replacement for antibody deficiency, corticosteroids ± antifungals for ABPA).
protocols: |+
  Significant haemoptysis

  > Bronchiectasis is a common cause of haemoptysis, usually from hypertrophied bronchial (not pulmonary) arteries. Most episodes are minor and self-limiting, but massive haemoptysis is life-threatening via airway flooding/asphyxiation rather than exsanguination alone.

  - **Massive haemoptysis** (large volume, or any haemoptysis causing haemodynamic compromise or respiratory distress): resuscitate (ABCDE), **position the patient with the known or suspected bleeding side down** (protects the good lung from aspirated blood), urgent CT angiography, and **bronchial artery embolisation** (interventional radiology) as the usual first-line definitive treatment — surgery is reserved for failed embolisation or specific localised disease.
  - Correct any coagulopathy, and stop anticoagulant/antiplatelet drugs where the bleeding risk outweighs their benefit, in discussion with the relevant specialty.

  - **Infective exacerbation with respiratory failure**, or an acute deterioration in known CF with faltering growth, weight loss or declining lung function, should prompt admission to (or discussion with) a specialist CF or bronchiectasis centre rather than purely local management, given the specialised nature of ongoing care.
