background: >+
  Arginine vasopressin (AVP, antidiuretic hormone/ADH) is made in the hypothalamus, stored in and released from the **posterior pituitary**, and acts on **V2 receptors** in the renal collecting duct to insert aquaporin-2 water channels, concentrating the urine. Release is driven mainly by **plasma osmolality** (via hypothalamic osmoreceptors) and, less sensitively, by volume/pressure (via baroreceptors).

  Diabetes insipidus (DI)

  - Passage of large volumes of dilute urine despite a normal or high plasma osmolality — the opposite physiological problem to SIADH.
  - **Cranial DI (AVP deficiency)**: insufficient AVP release. Causes: idiopathic, pituitary surgery/trauma, tumours (craniopharyngioma, pituitary macroadenoma, metastases), infiltrative disease (sarcoidosis, Langerhans cell histiocytosis), infection (meningitis, encephalitis), or genetic.
  - **Nephrogenic DI (AVP resistance)**: AVP is present but the collecting duct doesn't respond. Causes: **lithium** (common and often irreversible), hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia, genetic (AVPR2 or AQP2 mutations), chronic kidney disease, post-obstructive uropathy.
  - **Primary polydipsia** mimics DI clinically (excessive water intake suppresses AVP appropriately) and is an important differential, especially in psychiatric patients.

  Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH)

  - AVP secretion (or action) continues despite a low plasma osmolality, where it should normally be switched off — water is retained, diluting sodium, causing a **euvolaemic hyponatraemia**. The commonest cause of hyponatraemia in hospital inpatients.
  - **Causes** — malignancy (classically small-cell lung cancer, also head and neck, GI, prostate), CNS disease (stroke, haemorrhage, meningitis, encephalitis, trauma), pulmonary disease (pneumonia, abscess, TB), drugs (SSRIs, carbamazepine, sulfonylureas, PPIs, antipsychotics, and desmopressin itself), post-operative pain/nausea, and idiopathic.
diagnosis: >+
  Diabetes insipidus

  - **Polyuria** (often >3 L/day) with **polydipsia**; nocturia disturbing sleep is common. Can present acutely post-pituitary surgery, or insidiously over months. In cranial DI, look for associated anterior pituitary hormone deficits and other features of the underlying cause (visual field defect with a large pituitary lesion — see the Visual field defects card).
  - Mild hypernatraemia may be present if the patient cannot keep up with fluid losses (e.g. reduced consciousness, no access to water); if thirst and access to water are intact, sodium is often normal because the patient simply drinks enough.

  SIADH

  > SIADH is a **euvolaemic** hyponatraemia — no oedema, no signs of hypovolaemia (postural drop, dry mucosa). If there's clinical evidence of volume depletion or overload, look elsewhere first (diuretics, GI losses, heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome) before diagnosing SIADH — see the Hyponatraemia card for the full differential by volume status.

  - Symptoms track the **severity and speed of fall** in sodium: mild/chronic can be asymptomatic or just cause headache, nausea, lethargy and mild confusion; severe or rapidly-developing hyponatraemia causes seizures and reduced consciousness.
investigations: >+
  Diabetes insipidus

  - Confirm true polyuria (24-hour urine volume) and exclude diabetes mellitus (glucose), hypercalcaemia and hypokalaemia first, since these all cause polyuria/nephrogenic resistance.
  - **Water deprivation test**: fluids withheld (closely supervised) and urine osmolality measured hourly. Normally, urine concentrates (>600 mosmol/kg) as plasma osmolality rises. **Failure to concentrate** despite a high/rising plasma osmolality confirms DI (or primary polydipsia if mild). **Desmopressin is then given**: urine osmolality **rises >50%** in cranial DI (kidneys can respond once AVP is supplied) but **stays low** in nephrogenic DI (kidneys can't respond regardless). **Copeptin** (a stable AVP surrogate) measurement, with or without hypertonic saline stimulation, is increasingly used instead of the water deprivation test where available, as it is quicker and avoids prolonged dehydration.
  - **Cranial DI confirmed**: pituitary MRI (looking for a mass, or loss of the normal posterior pituitary "bright spot" on T1) and full anterior pituitary function testing.

  SIADH (diagnostic criteria)

  - **Hyponatraemia** with **low plasma osmolality** (<275 mosmol/kg), **inappropriately concentrated urine** (urine osmolality >100 mosmol/kg — i.e. not maximally dilute as it should be), **urine sodium usually >30 mmol/L** (with a normal salt and water intake), clinical **euvolaemia**, and normal thyroid, adrenal and renal function.
  - **Always exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency** (both can mimic SIADH biochemically) with TFTs and a short Synacthen test or 9 am cortisol before confirming the diagnosis. Chase the cause: CXR/CT chest (malignancy, infection), CT head if a neurological cause is suspected, full drug history.
treatment: >+
  Cranial DI

  - **Desmopressin** (DDAVP) — oral, sublingual, intranasal or subcutaneous/IM — titrated to urine output and plasma sodium. **Risk of dilutional hyponatraemia with over-treatment**, so start low and monitor sodium, especially when starting or changing dose.
  - Post-pituitary-surgery DI is often transient — avoid over-treating in the first few days, and watch for the classic **"triple-phase response"** (initial transient DI from stalk trauma/oedema → a few days of SIADH-like antidiuresis from uncontrolled hormone release by dying axons → then either recovery or permanent DI), which needs close sodium monitoring rather than fixed dosing.

  Nephrogenic DI

  - Treat/remove the cause where possible (stop lithium if feasible — discuss with psychiatry first, as it may be the only effective mood stabiliser; correct hypercalcaemia/hypokalaemia). If it persists: low-salt, low-protein diet, a thiazide diuretic (paradoxically reduces urine volume by causing mild volume depletion that increases proximal water/sodium reabsorption), and/or NSAIDs; desmopressin is largely ineffective since the problem is renal resistance, not AVP lack.

  SIADH

  - **Treat the underlying cause** where identifiable and stop any causative drug. **Fluid restriction** (typically 500–1000 mL/day, and at least 500 mL less than the previous day's urine output) is first-line. Response can be limited if urine osmolality is very high, combined urine Na⁺+K⁺ exceeds serum Na⁺, or urine output is already low (<1500 mL/day) — these predict a poor response to restriction alone.
  - **Demeclocycline** (induces a mild, reversible nephrogenic DI) or a **vasopressin receptor antagonist** ("vaptan", e.g. tolvaptan) are options for refractory or severe chronic SIADH, under specialist supervision — tolvaptan carries a risk of overly rapid correction and needs initiation as an inpatient with frequent sodium checks.
  - **Fluid restriction is avoided in SIADH secondary to subarachnoid haemorrhage** — reducing intravascular volume risks worsening cerebral vasospasm and ischaemia; isotonic fluids and specialist neurovascular input are used instead.
protocols: >+
  > Red flag: whatever the cause, **do not correct chronic hyponatraemia faster than 8–10 mmol/L in 24 hours** (see the Hyponatraemia and Fluid therapy cards) — overly rapid correction, even of SIADH, risks osmotic demyelination syndrome.

  - **Severe, acute, symptomatic hyponatraemia** (seizures, significantly reduced consciousness) from SIADH is an emergency regardless of the chronic treatment plan above: hypertonic saline, usually under critical care/specialist guidance, with frequent sodium rechecks to stay within the safe correction limit.
  - **Desmopressin overdose/over-treatment** in cranial DI causing hyponatraemia: withhold the next dose(s) and allow an aquaresis, monitoring sodium.
  - Undiagnosed/untreated DI in a patient who cannot access water (reduced consciousness, post-operative, sedated) can cause dangerous hypernatraemia and dehydration quickly — ensure adequate IV fluid replacement and frequent U&E monitoring in this situation.
