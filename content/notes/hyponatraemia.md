background: >+
  Serum sodium <135 mmol/L — the **commonest electrolyte disturbance in hospital inpatients**. It almost always reflects a problem with **water** handling (too much water relative to sodium), not a pure sodium deficit, which is why the assessment is built around **volume status**, not just the sodium number. Severity is usually graded mild (130–134), moderate (125–129) and severe (<125 mmol/L), but **symptoms and risk depend more on how fast it developed than the absolute number** — a slow chronic fall to 118 may cause few symptoms, while a rapid fall to 128 can cause seizures.

  - **Artefactual ("pseudohyponatraemia")**: severe hyperlipidaemia or hyperproteinaemia can falsely lower a lab sodium reading depending on the assay used — worth considering if the clinical picture doesn't fit.
  - **Hyperglycaemia** causes a true dilutional hyponatraemia by osmotically drawing water into the vascular space — correct the measured sodium upward (roughly +1.6–2.4 mmol/L for every 5.5 mmol/L of glucose above normal) before interpreting it.
diagnosis: >+
  - Mild/chronic: often asymptomatic, or non-specific — headache, lethargy, nausea, mild confusion, unsteady gait (a significant and under-recognised cause of falls in older people). Severe or rapidly-developing: seizures, marked confusion, and reduced consciousness.
  - **Classify by clinical volume status** — this is the single most useful step, since it directs the differential and the fluid management are opposite in each group:

  > **Hypovolaemic** (signs of volume depletion — tachycardia, postural drop, dry mucosa, reduced skin turgor): GI losses (vomiting, diarrhoea), diuretics (classically thiazides), Addison's disease, salt-wasting nephropathy, burns. **Hypervolaemic** (oedema, raised JVP, ascites): heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, advanced CKD — total body sodium and water are both increased, but water more than sodium. **Euvolaemic** (no signs of either): SIADH (the commonest cause in this group — see the dedicated AVP/SIADH card), hypothyroidism, secondary adrenal insufficiency, primary polydipsia, or a "reset osmostat".

  - Ask about diuretics specifically (a very common iatrogenic cause), alcohol intake (can cause both volume depletion and a "beer potomania"-type picture from very low solute intake), and recent IV fluid prescriptions (hypotonic fluid such as 5% glucose or 0.18% saline/4% glucose can itself cause or worsen hyponatraemia — see the Fluid therapy card).
investigations: >+
  - **Paired serum and urine osmolality**, and **urine sodium**, alongside the clinical volume assessment above.
  - **Serum osmolality low** confirms true (hypotonic) hyponatraemia. If serum osmolality is **normal or high** despite a low sodium, think pseudohyponatraemia or a hyperglycaemia/mannitol-driven osmotic shift instead — the usual hyponatraemia work-up doesn't apply.
  - A **low urine osmolality** (<100 mosmol/kg) in true hyponatraemia suggests the kidney is appropriately maximally diluting urine — primary polydipsia or very low solute intake. A urine osmolality that is **not** appropriately low (>100 mosmol/kg) means ADH is active despite low plasma osmolality — either appropriately (true volume depletion stimulating ADH via baroreceptors) or inappropriately (SIADH) — the volume status and urine sodium then distinguish these.
  - Baseline screen in every case: U&E, glucose, TFTs and a 9 am cortisol (or short Synacthen test) to exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency, both of which can mimic or contribute to euvolaemic hyponatraemia.
treatment: >+
  - **Treat the underlying cause** and the volume status, not just the number:
    - **Hypovolaemic**: IV 0.9% saline (or oral rehydration if mild) restores volume and, by switching off the appropriate ADH drive, allows the kidneys to correct the sodium themselves — sometimes faster than expected, so monitor closely (see the correction-rate caveat below).
    - **Hypervolaemic**: treat the underlying condition (heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome) and restrict fluid/sodium; diuretics for the oedema, used cautiously.
    - **Euvolaemic**: fluid restriction is the default (see the AVP/SIADH card for SIADH-specific detail); replace thyroxine or hydrocortisone if hypothyroidism/adrenal insufficiency is the cause.
  - Mild, asymptomatic, chronic hyponatraemia often needs no urgent treatment beyond addressing the cause — rapid "normalisation" is not the goal and is actively dangerous (see Protocols).
protocols: >+
  > Red flag: whatever the cause or the treatment used, **do not correct sodium faster than about 8–10 mmol/L in any 24-hour period** (some protocols use a lower limit, e.g. 4–6 mmol/L/24h, in patients at particularly high risk — see below). Overcorrection risks **osmotic demyelination syndrome** (irreversible central pontine/extrapontine myelinolysis — dysarthria, dysphagia, spastic quadriparesis, which can appear days after an overly rapid correction).

  - **Higher-risk patients for osmotic demyelination** (use an even more cautious correction target in these groups): chronic/severe hyponatraemia (<120 mmol/L for ≥48 hours or of unknown duration), alcohol use disorder, malnutrition, liver disease, and hypokalaemia.
  - **Severe, acute, symptomatic hyponatraemia** (seizures, marked reduced consciousness, impending herniation) is a genuine emergency: **hypertonic saline** is given under senior/specialist guidance specifically to interrupt the acute neurological threat, accepting a rapid initial rise — but the *total* 24-hour rise is still capped at the safe limit above, so the rate is slowed once the emergency has passed. This is a specialist-led intervention, not a ward-based one.
  - **Unexpectedly rapid auto-correction** can happen once the cause of ADH excess is removed (e.g. fluids given for hypovolaemic hyponatraemia, or steroid replacement in adrenal insufficiency) — recheck sodium within a few hours of starting treatment in anyone with significant hyponatraemia, and be ready to slow correction (even giving desmopressin plus 5% glucose, under specialist guidance, to deliberately re-lower a sodium that is rising too fast) if it is overshooting the safe rate.
  - See the Fluid therapy card for the same correction-rate principle applied to hypernatraemia, and the AVP/SIADH card for SIADH-specific management detail.
