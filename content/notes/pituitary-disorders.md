background: >+
  The anterior pituitary makes **GH, prolactin, ACTH, TSH, LH/FSH**; the posterior pituitary stores/releases AVP and oxytocin (see the AVP disorders card). Most pituitary disease in practice is a **pituitary adenoma** — almost always benign, but causing problems either by **hormone excess**, by **mass effect** (on the optic chiasm above, or the cavernous sinus laterally), or, when large, by **compressing normal pituitary tissue and causing hypopituitarism**.

  - **Non-functioning adenoma**: the commonest type overall — secretes no clinically significant hormone, so usually presents late, through mass effect (visual field defect, headache) or incidental hypopituitarism, rather than through a hormone-excess syndrome.
  - **Prolactinoma**: the commonest *functioning* adenoma. Excess prolactin suppresses GnRH, causing hypogonadism.
  - **Acromegaly**: a GH-secreting adenoma (almost always a pituitary adenoma; very rarely ectopic GHRH). Insidious, since the physical changes develop over years — diagnosis is often delayed by a decade or more.
  - **Cushing's disease** (an ACTH-secreting adenoma) is covered in full in the Adrenal disorders card, since the clinical problem is the downstream hypercortisolism; a **TSH-secreting adenoma** causing central hyperthyroidism is covered in the Hyperthyroidism card. Both are rare functioning adenoma subtypes.
  - **Hypopituitarism**: loss of one or more anterior pituitary hormones, from any destructive or compressive pituitary/hypothalamic process (large adenoma, surgery, radiotherapy, apoplexy, **Sheehan's syndrome** — post-partum pituitary infarction after major obstetric haemorrhage, infiltrative disease such as sarcoidosis or haemochromatosis, or a severe head injury). **Hormone axes fail in a fairly predictable order as a mass enlarges: GH first, then LH/FSH, then TSH, and ACTH last** — though any pattern can occur with an acute insult such as apoplexy or trauma.
  - **Pituitary apoplexy**: sudden haemorrhage or infarction within a (usually pre-existing, often previously undiagnosed) pituitary adenoma — a neuroendocrine emergency.
diagnosis: >+
  - **Mass effect** (any adenoma, once large enough): headache, and a **bitemporal hemianopia** or other visual field defect from chiasmal compression (see the dedicated Visual field defects card), diplopia/ophthalmoplegia if the cavernous sinus is involved (CN III, IV, VI).
  - **Prolactinoma**: galactorrhoea, oligo/amenorrhoea and infertility in women; reduced libido, erectile dysfunction and infertility in men — men more often present later, with a larger tumour and mass-effect symptoms, since the hormonal symptoms are less immediately obvious.
  - **Acromegaly**: enlargement of the hands, feet, jaw (prognathism) and soft tissues, coarsening facial features, macroglossia, excessive sweating, deepening voice, carpal tunnel syndrome, and, systemically, hypertension, impaired glucose tolerance/diabetes, obstructive sleep apnoea, and an increased risk of colonic polyps/cancer and cardiomyopathy — comparing old photographs over years is often the most striking clue.
  - **Hypopituitarism**: symptoms of whichever axes are lost — GH deficiency (reduced muscle mass, fatigue, poor quality of life — most noticeable in children as growth failure), gonadotrophin deficiency (amenorrhoea, loss of libido, erectile dysfunction, infertility), TSH deficiency (hypothyroid symptoms, but **without** a goitre and **without** the TSH rise seen in primary disease), ACTH deficiency (fatigue, hypotension, hypoglycaemia — **without** the hyperpigmentation of Addison's disease, since ACTH itself is low, not high).
investigations: >+
  - **Pituitary MRI** for any suspected adenoma or apoplexy. **Formal visual field testing** for any adenoma with supra-sellar extension or chiasmal contact on imaging, and at follow-up if the tumour is managed conservatively.
  - **Baseline anterior pituitary hormone panel** for every pituitary mass: 9 am cortisol (± short Synacthen test), free T4 and TSH, LH/FSH with oestradiol or testosterone, **prolactin**, and **IGF-1** (a better screening test for acromegaly than a random GH level, since GH is pulsatile).
  - **Prolactin interpretation**: very high levels (**>5,000 mU/L**, often much higher with a macroprolactinoma) are essentially diagnostic of a prolactinoma. **Mild-to-moderate elevation** is non-specific and far more often due to pregnancy, primary hypothyroidism (TRH mildly stimulates prolactin), stress, breast/chest wall stimulation, renal failure, or **drugs** (antipsychotics, metoclopramide, domperidone, SSRIs, oestrogens) — exclude these before attributing it to a tumour. A **non-functioning adenoma compressing the pituitary stalk** can also cause a mild prolactin rise (reduced dopamine delivery to the lactotrophs, which normally suppresses prolactin) — this "stalk effect" rarely exceeds the very high levels seen with a true prolactinoma, which matters because the two need different management.
  - **Acromegaly confirmation**: a raised IGF-1 is confirmed with a **glucose tolerance test** — GH should suppress to a very low level after an oral glucose load; **failure to suppress** confirms autonomous GH excess. Pituitary MRI follows to find the adenoma; screen for associated complications (echocardiogram, colonoscopy, sleep study, glucose).
treatment: >+
  - **Prolactinoma**: a **dopamine agonist** (cabergoline preferred over bromocriptine — more effective, better tolerated) is first-line **even for large macroadenomas with mass effect**, often shrinking the tumour substantially and avoiding surgery; the dose is titrated against prolactin level and tumour size on repeat imaging. Surgery is reserved for dopamine agonist-resistant or -intolerant cases, or an apoplexy/acute visual threat.
  - **Non-functioning adenoma**: observation with serial imaging for small, asymptomatic lesions; **trans-sphenoidal surgery** for mass effect (visual field defect, significant hypopituitarism) or significant growth on surveillance.
  - **Acromegaly**: **trans-sphenoidal surgery** is first-line for most GH-secreting adenomas. If surgery is not curative or not possible: **somatostatin analogues** (octreotide, lanreotide — first-line medical therapy), a **GH-receptor antagonist** (pegvisomant) for inadequate response, or a dopamine agonist for mildly-elevated or co-secreting (GH + prolactin) tumours. Radiotherapy is reserved for residual/recurrent disease not controlled by the above.
  - **Hypopituitarism**: replace each deficient axis — hydrocortisone for ACTH deficiency, levothyroxine for TSH deficiency, sex hormone replacement for gonadotrophin deficiency (or fertility treatment if conception is wanted), and GH replacement in selected adults with proven deficiency and significantly impaired quality of life.
  - > Red flag: when both cortisol and thyroxine need replacing, **always start hydrocortisone before (or at the same time as) levothyroxine, never thyroxine first** — thyroxine accelerates cortisol metabolism and can precipitate an adrenal crisis in someone with unrecognised ACTH deficiency.
protocols: >+
  Pituitary apoplexy

  > Sudden-onset severe headache (often described as the worst ever, mimicking subarachnoid haemorrhage), visual field defect or reduced acuity, ophthalmoplegia (CN III most often, causing a painful third nerve palsy), and features of acute hypopituitarism (collapse, hypotension) — usually in a patient with a known or previously undiagnosed pituitary adenoma. A recognised trigger in some cases is starting anticoagulation or a pituitary stimulation test, though most cases are spontaneous.

  - **Do not wait for a formal endocrine work-up before treating the emergency.** Take a cortisol sample, then give **immediate stress-dose IV hydrocortisone** (e.g. 100 mg) regardless of the result, since acute ACTH deficiency can be rapidly fatal and the biochemical confirmation can follow.
  - Urgent **pituitary MRI** (or CT if MRI is not rapidly available) to confirm the diagnosis, and urgent **neurosurgical and endocrinology referral**. Formal visual field assessment as soon as feasible.
  - **Surgical decompression (usually trans-sphenoidal) is considered urgently** for significant or deteriorating visual loss or reduced consciousness; patients with milder, stable symptoms (normal vision, stable conscious level) can sometimes be managed conservatively with hormone replacement and close monitoring, based on specialist assessment.
  - Assess and replace the full anterior pituitary axis once stable (cortisol first, as above, then thyroid and sex hormones as needed) — some deficits recover over time, others are permanent, so periodic re-testing is needed even after the acute event has resolved.
